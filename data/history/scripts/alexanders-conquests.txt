## OPEN
N: Alexander the Great is supposed to have wept when he saw the size of what he had won, because there were no more worlds left to conquer.
N: His own record calls that line paraphrased, and probably not his at all.
N: Nobody who wrote a full account of Alexander's life ever met him. Arrian, Plutarch, and Curtius Rufus, the three fullest histories that survive, were all writing centuries after he died.
N: Everything spoken in his own words in what follows carries that warning, every time.

## TITLE
N: The Conquests of Alexander. Three hundred and thirty four to three hundred and twenty three before the common era.

## SCENE 1 | Thebes, before the crossing
N: Philip the Second is two years dead, murdered by one of his own bodyguards, and several Greek cities have decided his son will not hold what his father built.
N: Alexander marches on Thebes first. When the city falls, six thousand people are killed and thirty thousand are sold into slavery.
N: No other Greek city rises against him again while he lives.

## REST | short

## SCENE 2 | Anatolia, three thirty four to three thirty three before the common era
N: In the spring, Alexander crosses the Hellespont with thirty seven thousand troops and one and a half million pounds of debt.
N: At the Granicus River in May, he leads the first cavalry charge himself and nearly dies. A Persian commander swings a sword at his head, and Cleitus the Black cuts the man's arm off before it lands.
N: Cleitus has just saved the life of the man who will kill him at a dinner table eight years later.
N: By November, Alexander meets Darius the Third at Issus, on a coastal plain too narrow for a Persian army of some hundred thousand men to use its size. Darius flees, leaving his mother, wife, and children behind.

## SCENE 3 | Tyre, Gaza, and Egypt, three hundred and thirty two before the common era
N: Tyre will not surrender, so Alexander spends seven months building a causeway half a mile long through open sea to reach the island city.
N: When Tyre falls, two thousand military age men are crucified along the beach and thirty thousand people are sold into slavery.
N: Gaza holds out two more months. Its commander, Batis, is dragged alive behind a chariot when the city falls.
N: Egypt opens its gates and welcomes him as a liberator from Persian rule. At the oracle of Siwa, the priests call him son of Ammon, and he never once says they are wrong.

## DOCUMENT | Alexander, as reported by Plutarch | Life of Alexander | c. 100 CE
N: Plutarch, writing about four hundred years after Alexander was dead, has him say this about Diogenes.
M: If I were not Alexander, I would wish to be Diogenes.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | Gaugamela, the first of October, three hundred and thirty one before the common era
N: Darius gathers an army ancient writers put at two hundred and fifty thousand men. Modern historians think fifty to a hundred thousand is closer to the truth.
N: Alexander has forty seven thousand. He breaks the Persian center with a diagonal cavalry charge still studied in military academies two thousand years later.
N: Darius runs a second time. Within a year his own satrap, Bessus, murders him. Alexander hunts Bessus into Central Asia and has him executed.

## SCENE 5 | Maracanda, three hundred and twenty seven before the common era
N: Between Gaugamela and India, Alexander campaigns for three years through what is now Afghanistan, Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan. He marries Roxana, a Bactrian noblewoman, over the fury of his own officers.
N: At a drunken banquet in Maracanda, now Samarkand, he kills Cleitus the Black with a spear, in an argument over whether Philip or Alexander deserved more credit for the victories.
N: Cleitus is the man who cut off the arm reaching for Alexander's head at the Granicus. Alexander refuses food for three days afterward.

## DOCUMENT | Curtius Rufus | Historiae Alexandri Magni | c. 50 CE
N: Curtius Rufus, a Roman writing about three hundred seventy years later, used one sentence for what Alexander had become.
M: His body, panting with unquenchable thirst for blood, was now turning against his friends and generals.

## ASIDE
N: The man Cleitus had saved was gone. What was left killed him.

## REST

## SCENE 6 | The Hyphasis, and the road home, three hundred and twenty six to three hundred and twenty three before the common era
N: Alexander beats King Porus at the Hydaspes River in May, against two hundred war elephants his phalanx has never faced in that number. He wins, but eight years of marching, monsoon rain, and disease have used up his army's patience.
N: At the next river, the Hyphasis, his veterans refuse to go one step further east. Alexander sulks in his tent for three days, then turns the army around.
N: The march home through the Gedrosian Desert kills a quarter of his men, roughly fifteen thousand, to heat, thirst, and flash floods.
N: He reaches Babylon in three hundred and twenty three before the common era and dies on the tenth or eleventh of June, aged thirty two. His empire breaks apart within hours.

## REST | short

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of the same eleven years survive, and whether Alexander was a liberator or a catastrophe depends entirely on which side of the Hellespont a person was standing.

## PERSPECTIVE | Greco-Macedonian | victor
N: The historians who marched with Alexander told this as a Greek war of revenge. Persia had burned Athens a century and a half earlier, and Alexander threw a spear into Asian soil and came ashore first.
N: His own courage backs the claim. He led cavalry into the worst fighting at Issus and Gaugamela both, and at a fortress in India an arrow went through his lung and his own bodyguards carried him out over the wall.
N: The cities were not raiding camps. Alexandria in Egypt held three hundred thousand people within a generation, and a library that stayed the center of Greek learning for nine hundred years.
N: Persian officials and a mass wedding at Susa, where eighty of his officers married Persian noblewomen: this account calls that statesmanship. Someone had to govern fifty million people, and Macedonians alone could not.
N: Before the Sogdian Rock, Plutarch, writing about four hundred years after Alexander was dead, has him tell his own men this.
M: There is nothing impossible to him who will try.

## PERSPECTIVE | Persian / Achaemenid | vanquished
N: The empire Alexander ended had run from the Aegean to the Indus for two hundred and twenty years, the largest the world had yet seen, administering twenty three provinces in multiple languages.
N: Alexander burned Persepolis after a banquet. He sent five thousand mules and three thousand camels to carry off the treasury first, an estimated four thousand seven hundred metric tons of silver.
N: The stone reliefs survived. The archives, the wooden ceilings, and the textiles did not, and with them went nearly every Persian word about what happened, written by the people it happened to.
N: Everything historians know about Darius the Third comes from the Greek and Roman writers who beat him, which is why he reads as a coward who fled two battles, not as the king whose own tax and grain records kept running up to the day the Macedonians arrived.
N: Ferdowsi wrote Iran's national epic, the Shahnameh, more than thirteen hundred years later, and it still calls him the destroyer.
M: And from that day forth the fortunes of Iran declined.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Indian / Paurava | indigenous
N: Greek writers describe a giant of a man on the largest elephant on the field. Indian sources do not mention him at all, and that silence is itself the argument: a battle that fills Western accounts of Alexander barely registers in Indian memory.
N: Porus fought with his full army at the Hydaspes, two hundred war elephants against a phalanx that had never faced them in that number. Alexander crossed the river in monsoon rain and won, but it cost him his own horse, Bucephalus, dead of wounds or exhaustion.
N: Arrian, writing about four hundred seventy years later, is the only place Porus's answer to the victor survives.
M: How shall I treat you? As a king.
N: Alexander restored him as a vassal and marched on. His own army refused to go further at the next river, and the Macedonians turned back. Within five years, Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the Nanda dynasty and built an empire larger than anything Alexander held in India.

## PERSPECTIVE | Modern Central Asian | academic
N: The cities Alexander planted across Central Asia became nodes on what would later be the Silk Road. Tajik and Uzbek historians ask what those cities were founded for.
N: A Sogdian nobleman named Spitamenes led a guerrilla campaign that killed two thousand Macedonian troops and held Alexander to two years of counterinsurgency, the longest resistance he met anywhere. Alexander answered by wrecking the region's farmland and deporting its people.
N: Central Asian tradition reads the marriage to Roxana differently than the Greek sources do: not a love match at a banquet, but a political price, paid to end the resistance by giving her father a satrapy.
N: Curtius Rufus, a Roman writing about three hundred seventy years later, needed one sentence for the whole campaign.
M: Alexander found it easier to conquer Sogdiana than to hold it.

## PERSPECTIVE | Revisionist / Anti-Heroic | revisionist
N: Strip away the romance and the count runs from a hundred thousand dead to seven hundred fifty thousand, depending on which ancient tally a person trusts, across the destruction of Thebes, Tyre, and Persepolis.
N: After three hundred and thirty before the common era he began demanding the prostration Persian court custom required, and executing men who refused it. Callisthenes, his own tutor Aristotle's nephew, was arrested for refusing and died in custody.
N: He executed his general Philotas, and had Philotas's father Parmenion, seventy years old and his most experienced commander, quietly assassinated the same day.
N: He marched his exhausted army through the Gedrosian Desert to outdo the legendary conquerors before him, and it cost roughly fifteen thousand of his own men.
N: He built no civil service and named no heir. Asked on his deathbed who should have the empire, later writers give him one two word answer, to the strongest, though the Greek could just as easily have named his own general Krateros.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: Each account skips what would complicate it.
N: The Greek and Macedonian record calls the destruction the convention of siege warfare, and says far less about Thebes, Tyre, Gaza, and the burning of Persepolis, or the drinking and paranoia of the last years.
N: The Persian account names every ton of silver Alexander carried off, and says less about the satraps who had already revolted against Darius before Alexander arrived, or the courtesy Alexander showed Darius's own captured family.
N: The Indian account is built on an army that mutinied, and says little about the battle Alexander actually won at the Hydaspes, or the Greek coinage and city planning northwestern India kept using long after the Macedonians left.
N: The Central Asian account, which names the guerrilla war in the most detail, says less about the Greco-Bactrian kingdoms it produced, kingdoms where a Greek king later became a Buddhist.
N: And the revisionist case, which spares him nothing, has the least interest in the library at Alexandria or the language the New Testament was later written in, both of which trace back to the same eleven years it condemns.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: He founded more than twenty cities across three continents, and named nearly all of them Alexandria, after himself.
N: On the bank of the Hydaspes he named one more. Bucephala, after the horse that had carried him into battle and died there, from wounds or from exhaustion. The ancient sources cannot agree on which.
