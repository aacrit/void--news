## OPEN
N: This is not the story of a colony winning its freedom. Algeria was not a colony. In French law it was three departments of France itself, governed from Paris since eighteen forty eight, with its own deputies in the National Assembly.
N: So when the first attacks came in November nineteen fifty four, the French Interior Minister was not talking about a possession overseas. Algeria is France, he said, and the only negotiation is war.
N: France did not call what followed a war for thirty seven years. It named it one in June nineteen ninety nine.

## TITLE
N: The Algerian War of Independence. November first, nineteen fifty four, to July fifth, nineteen sixty two.

## SCENE 1 | Three in the morning
N: Three in the morning, November first, nineteen fifty four. All Saints' Day.
N: Roughly thirty coordinated attacks hit police posts, barracks, and farms across Algeria at once. Seven people die.
N: From Cairo, a radio broadcast announces a new organization, the National Liberation Front.

## DOCUMENT | National Liberation Front (FLN) | Proclamation of November 1, 1954 | November 1, 1954
N: The proclamation the National Liberation Front broadcast that night demanded one thing.
M: The restoration of the Algerian state, sovereign, democratic and social, within the framework of Islamic principles.

## ASIDE
N: The government's answer came within days, from the Interior Minister, Francois Mitterrand.
M: Algeria is France, and the only negotiation is war.
N: To offer negotiation is to admit there is a territory to negotiate over. Mitterrand had just ruled that out.

## SCENE 2 | Setif, 1945
N: Nine years earlier, May eighth, nineteen forty five. VE Day. Celebrations in Setif turn into a rising, then into weeks of killing by the army and settler militias.
N: Algerian accounts count tens of thousands dead. The number is still contested, from fifteen hundred to forty five thousand.
N: A generation concludes that petitions get nothing. Men who will found the FLN date their conviction to that day.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | Algiers, 1957
N: January nineteen fifty seven. General Jacques Massu is handed police powers over the city of Algiers.
N: His paratroopers break the FLN's bombing network within eight months. They do it with mass arrests, electrodes, water, and the disappearance of thousands.
N: On June eleventh, a twenty five year old mathematician named Maurice Audin is arrested. The army says he escaped from a moving jeep. He is never seen again.
N: His friend Henri Alleg is taken the next day, to the same building. Alleg survives, and later writes down what four words Audin said to him before they were separated for good.

## DOCUMENT | Maurice Audin | Last words to Henri Alleg, reported in La Question | 1957
N: Audin's last words to Alleg, as Alleg recorded them, were flat and human.
M: It is tough, Henri.

## ASIDE
N: Alleg's book sold sixty thousand copies in two weeks. Then the French state seized it, the first book seizure in France since the eighteenth century.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | Algiers, June 1958
N: Charles de Gaulle returns to power in nineteen fifty eight, carried by settlers and generals chanting for French Algeria. In Algiers, he speaks to the crowd from a balcony.

## DOCUMENT | Charles de Gaulle | Speech from the Forum, Algiers | June 4, 1958
N: De Gaulle told the crowd four words, and they heard a promise.
M: I have understood you.

## ASIDE
N: He turned instead toward self-determination. The settlers who had brought him back formed the Secret Army Organization and answered with plastic bombs and a failed generals' putsch in April nineteen sixty one.

## SCENE 5 | Paris, then Evian
N: October seventeenth, nineteen sixty one. Paris police under prefect Maurice Papon attack a peaceful Algerian march. Men are beaten, shot, and thrown into the Seine.
N: The Evian Accords bring a ceasefire on March nineteenth, nineteen sixty two. A referendum on July first passes with ninety nine point seven percent.
N: On July fifth, nineteen sixty two, Algeria becomes independent.

## SCENE 6 | The dead were never agreed upon
N: Algeria's founding memory enshrines one and a half million martyrs. French and independent historians estimate the Algerian dead at three hundred thousand to four hundred thousand, alongside about twenty five thousand French soldiers killed.
N: Over two million rural Algerians had been forcibly relocated into camps during the war. Tens of thousands of harkis were killed in reprisals after it ended, and about one million pieds-noirs fled to France in nineteen sixty two.
N: Two states have never agreed on a single number since.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: What this war was, and what it cost, is contested from five directions. Each gets its own case, in its own terms, before anything is said against it.

## PERSPECTIVE | Algerian National Liberation (FLN) | victor
N: The Algerian account starts with arithmetic. In nineteen fifty four, one hundred twenty four years after French troops took Algiers, roughly one million European settlers held the citizenship, the best land, and the vote. About nine million Muslim Algerians held little of either.
N: Setif taught the founding generation what petitions were worth. Men who would found the FLN dated their conviction to that single day.
N: The FLN called the Algiers bombings answers to the guillotine. France executed FLN prisoners, and the FLN carried the war to the settler city that demanded those executions.
N: Larbi Ben M'hidi, an FLN commander captured in nineteen fifty seven, was killed in French custody. The army called it suicide. Decades later, an army officer admitted he had him hanged.
N: Ahmed Ben Bella was seized in nineteen fifty six when France forced down his aircraft. He spent the rest of the war in French prisons, and emerged from them as independent Algeria's first president.
N: Its own revolutionary slogan put the claim of authorship in three words.
M: One hero, the people.
N: Independence, in this account, is not a gift granted. It is a reversal, earned in eight years for a dispossession that had lasted one hundred thirty two.

## PERSPECTIVE | French State and the Pied-Noir Settlers | vanquished
N: For Paris, Algeria was not a possession to surrender. It was three departments of France, represented in the National Assembly since eighteen forty eight.
N: The pieds-noirs were not a planter aristocracy. The million European Algerians were mostly clerks, mechanics, and shopkeepers, many born in Algeria and knowing no other home. At Philippeville in August nineteen fifty five, FLN fighters killed around one hundred twenty Europeans, some of them women and children. To the settlers this was proof the FLN was a terrorist movement, not an army.
N: The army believed it had won. It broke the FLN's network in Algiers and sealed the border with the guerrillas' bases. By nineteen fifty nine the FLN inside Algeria was militarily broken. The war, in this account, was won on the ground and lost in Paris.
N: When independence came, the settlers' own slogan named the choice they said they faced.
M: The suitcase or the coffin.
N: On July fifth, the day independence was declared, hundreds of Europeans were killed in Oran while French troops under orders stood aside. To this account, that is French abandonment confirmed.
N: About one million crossed the Mediterranean within months, to a France that had made little provision for them.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The Harkis, Caught Between | vanquished
N: Between one hundred fifty thousand and two hundred thousand Algerian Muslims served the French side, as harkis, moghaznis, and soldiers. Their reasons ranged from wages to family tradition to belief in a French Algeria. The Evian Accords promised them amnesty.
N: It was not honored. A nineteen sixty two government directive ordered officers not to bring harkis across the Mediterranean. Many were disarmed and left in place.
N: What followed was reprisal. The number killed is fiercely contested: French official figures suggested around ten thousand; harki associations have claimed as many as one hundred fifty thousand.
N: Some French officers defied orders and smuggled families out at their own risk. The roughly ninety thousand who reached France were interned in camps for years, apart from French society and despised by the pieds-noirs who had also fled. For sixty years no state mourned them.
N: In September twenty twenty one, President Emmanuel Macron spoke directly to harki families.
M: I ask their forgiveness. We will not forget.

## PERSPECTIVE | Anti-War Dissent and the Torture Debate | revisionist
N: In January nineteen fifty seven, General Massu was handed police powers over Algiers. This account's central claim is about method: mass arrests in the Casbah, interrogation by electrode and water, and the disappearance of thousands never accounted for.
N: The case that reached metropolitan France was Maurice Audin's, a communist mathematician who died under torture at twenty five. His friend Henri Alleg survived and described what was done to him in a book seized by the state after it sold sixty thousand copies.
N: In September nineteen sixty, more than one hundred French intellectuals signed the Manifesto of the hundred twenty one, declaring the right of conscripts to refuse to fight an unjust war. Simone de Beauvoir and the lawyer Gisele Halimi took up the case of Djamila Boupacha, a young FLN militant raped and tortured in custody, and made it a public reckoning.
N: Frantz Fanon, a psychiatrist who had treated both French torturers and their Algerian victims, resigned his hospital post and joined the FLN. He argued that colonialism was itself a permanent violence, and that decolonization was its reckoning.
M: Decolonization is always a violent phenomenon.
N: To this account, the state's censorship of Alleg's book only confirmed the crime it was trying to bury.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Memory Scholarship and France's Reckoning | academic
N: For thirty seven years the French state refused the word war. Officially, the conflict was operations to maintain order.
N: The historian Benjamin Stora diagnosed the silence in nineteen ninety one as the gangrene and the forgetting, a wound that never closed because France would not name it.
N: Scholarship filled what the state left blank. Army records showed torture had been institutional policy, not the excess of a few units. Historians reconstructed the October nineteen sixty one Paris massacre that a government commission had put at forty dead, and counted at least one hundred twenty.
N: Papon himself had organized deportations of Jews under Vichy, and was convicted of crimes against humanity in nineteen ninety eight, for that earlier crime, never for the Algerians.
N: Under Macron the reckoning accelerated: in twenty eighteen, acknowledgment that the state killed Maurice Audin; in twenty twenty one, that the Paris massacre was inexcusable, and an apology to the harkis.
N: But on the war itself, Stora drew a line the government would not cross.
M: Apologising will not get to the heart of the issue.
N: Recognition, in this account, arrived without repentance, on both shores. Algeria's own state guards a fixed number of martyrs it will not submit to a historian's revision.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: None of these five is invented, and each has a gap it would rather not discuss.
N: The Algerian account leaves out the Melouza massacre of nineteen fifty seven, the purges of rival nationalists, and the one party state the FLN built once it had won.
N: The French account leaves out the legal inequality that made Muslim Algerians subjects, the army's own institutional torture, and Setif and the Paris massacre both.
N: The harki account leaves out that some harkis carried out abuses of their own, and that poverty, not conviction, drove many to enlist.
N: The dissenters' account leaves out the FLN's own terror against civilians and rival nationalists, and how readily its intellectuals romanticized the violence they defended.
N: The memory scholars leave out the lived suffering underneath their own careful politics, and the migrants who carry this history home with them still.
N: Each one omits exactly the part that would cost its own side the most.
