## OPEN
N: A photograph of Angkor Wat's towers looks like a photograph of one temple.
N: In twenty twelve, a plane carrying a laser flew over the jungle north of Siem Reap and stripped away six hundred years of forest growth. What it found was a city of a million people, more than a thousand square kilometers of reservoirs, canals, roads and neighborhoods.
N: No pre industrial city on Earth has been shown to be that large. It dwarfed medieval London.
N: The jungle did not hide a temple. It hid a city.

## TITLE
N: The Khmer Empire. Eight oh two to fourteen thirty one.

## SCENE 1 | Phnom Kulen, eight oh two
N: A sandstone plateau forty kilometers northeast of where Angkor Wat will stand. A king has just returned from the court of Java, whether as a guest or a hostage the record does not agree.
N: He climbs to a ritual ground with a Brahman priest and declares himself chakravartin, universal monarch, owing nothing to Java or to anyone else.
N: His name is Jayavarman the Second. What he starts on this hill will run for six hundred twenty nine years.

## DOCUMENT | Sdok Kok Thom inscription (K.235) | Sanskrit temple inscription | 1052 CE, recording 802 CE
N: The ceremony is recorded two hundred fifty years later, on a stone tablet called the Sdok Kok Thom inscription, naming the Brahman who performed it.
M: His Majesty, having returned, commenced to rule. He established the sacred linga of Shri Parameshvara on Mahendraparvata. A Brahman named Hiranyadama, learned in the magic science, came at the request of the king to perform a ceremony so that the land of the Khmer should no longer be dependent on Java.

## ASIDE
N: That ceremony did more than crown a king. It bound the king to a god, and made the capital itself a symbol of Mount Meru, the axis of the universe.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | Angkor Wat, eleven thirteen
N: Suryavarman the Second takes the throne in eleven thirteen and starts building. What rises is a temple complex of one hundred sixty two point six hectares, a moat one hundred ninety meters wide, running five and a half kilometers around it.
N: The temple faces west, toward the setting sun, toward death. Suryavarman dedicated it to Vishnu and built it as his own mortuary temple.
N: It remains the largest religious monument ever constructed.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | Angkor Thom, after eleven seventy seven
N: In eleven seventy seven, the Cham kingdom sails up the Tonle Sap lake and sacks Angkor.
N: Jayavarman the Seventh drives them out, takes the throne, and converts the state religion from Hinduism to Mahayana Buddhism.
N: He builds Angkor Thom, a walled capital three kilometers square, centered on the Bayon temple: two hundred sixteen carved stone faces on fifty four towers, looking outward in four directions at once.
N: He builds one hundred two hospitals across the empire and one hundred twenty one rest houses along the royal roads. He builds more than every king before him combined.

## DOCUMENT | Ta Prohm inscription (K.273) | Sanskrit temple inscription | c. 1186
N: One of his monasteries, the Ta Prohm inscription, itemized its own upkeep on stone, down to the dancer.
M: Attached to the temple are seventy nine thousand three hundred sixty five persons. Among them are two thousand seven hundred forty officiants, two thousand two hundred two assistants, and six hundred fifteen dancers.

## ASIDE
N: That is not a boast. It is a payroll, carved so it would outlast paper.

## REST | short

## SCENE 4 | Angkor, twelve ninety six
N: A Chinese diplomat named Zhou Daguan arrives at the capital in twelve ninety six and stays about a year. He is the only outsider whose account of daily life inside Angkor survives at all.

## DOCUMENT | Zhou Daguan | The Customs of Cambodia | c. 1297
N: This is what Zhou Daguan wrote about the temple at the center of the city he was visiting.
M: At the center of the kingdom there is a golden tower flanked by more than twenty stone towers and several hundred stone chambers. On the eastern side is a golden bridge guarded by two stone lions. There are eight golden Buddhas at the foot of the stone chambers.

## ASIDE
N: Zhou Daguan also wrote about markets run by women traders, and about an underclass forced into service, and about corporal punishment in the courts.
N: Everything the record has about daily life in Angkor's streets comes from this one visitor's eyes.

## REST

## SCENE 5 | Drought, thirteen sixty two to fourteen thirty one
N: The empire did not end in a single moment. Tree ring data from Vietnamese cypress trees recorded two long droughts, from thirteen sixty two to thirteen ninety two and again from fourteen fifteen to fourteen forty, each followed by catastrophic flooding.
N: The hydraulic system that had fed multiple rice harvests a year for six centuries could not absorb it.
N: In fourteen thirty one, the Ayutthaya kingdom, founded eighty years earlier as a Khmer vassal, sacked Angkor. The Khmer court moved south, to Phnom Penh and Lovek.
N: The temples were not abandoned. Angkor Wat stayed a Buddhist pilgrimage site, and Cambodian families kept living among the ruins.

## REST

## SCENE 6 | Le Tour du Monde, eighteen sixty three
N: A French naturalist named Henri Mouhot reaches Angkor in January eighteen sixty, collecting insects and plants. His sketches are published three years later, after his death, in a magazine called Le Tour du Monde.
N: Cambodian monks are living at the temple when he arrives. They have been maintaining it the whole time.

## DOCUMENT | Henri Mouhot | Travels in the Central Parts of Indo-China, Siam, Cambodia, and Laos | 1860
N: This is how Henri Mouhot described what he found.
M: One of these temples, a rival to that of Solomon, and erected by some ancient Michelangelo, might take an honourable place beside our most beautiful buildings. It is grander than anything left to us by Greece or Rome.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of what this empire was, and one thing to say before they start.
N: They come from five different rooms: a royal court, a colonial office, an independence movement, a rival kingdom, and a laboratory. Each is telling the truth about the room it was written in.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Khmer Court | victor
N: The first account is the one carved into the stone itself. Over one thousand two hundred Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions survive on temple walls across four modern countries, one of the densest written records in Asia.
N: Its case is that this was a literate, precise state, not a mysterious ruin. The Ta Prohm inscription counted its own staff down to the dancer. The Koh Ker inscription set penalties for officials who stole from temples. The Sdok Kok Thom inscription traces one priestly family across two hundred fifty years and nine kings.
N: The hydraulic network was never just plumbing. The West Baray held fifty three million cubic meters of water, and the West Mebon temple sat on an island at its center, reachable only by boat, a model of the cosmic ocean around Mount Meru. Water was sanctified before it irrigated a single field.
N: When the inscriptions stop, in the late fourteenth century, the record does not explain why.

## PERSPECTIVE | The French Discovery | victor
N: The second account is the one that built the modern study of Angkor, and its case is genuine scholarship. The Ecole francaise d'Extreme Orient, founded in nineteen hundred, produced the first systematic archaeology and epigraphy on the site. George Coedes's eight volume Inscriptions du Cambodge remains the foundation of Khmer epigraphy.
N: Its teams cleared jungle from the temples and rebuilt collapsing structures stone by stone, a technique called anastylosis, creating the archaeological park that stands today.
N: Before Henri Mouhot's sketches reached a French audience in eighteen sixty three, nobody in Europe knew Angkor existed at that scale. After them, everybody did.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Cambodian Nationalists | revisionist
N: The third account starts from a single fact: no other country puts a building on its flag. Cambodia does, and has since independence in nineteen fifty three.
N: Its argument is that the empire is the nation. For six centuries the Khmer held territory across present day Cambodia, Thailand, Laos and Vietnam, longer than most kingdoms of the same era lasted at all.
N: French, Thai and Vietnamese pressure reduced that empire to a small, dependent state. This account reads Angkor as proof the diminishment was never destiny, and every government that has held the capital since has put the temple on its flag to claim that inheritance.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Thai Successor State | bystander
N: The fourth account comes from the kingdom that succeeded Angkor, not the one that erased it. Ayutthaya was founded in thirteen fifty one, originally as a Khmer vassal, in the Chao Phraya river valley.
N: Its case is cultural continuity, not conquest. The Ayutthayan court used Khmer administrative titles, Khmer court ritual and Khmer legal terms. Classical Thai court dance descends from Khmer court dance, and the Thai Ramakien draws on Khmer literary tradition.
N: Thai chronicles describe the sack of Angkor in fourteen thirty one as a response to Khmer aggression, not an act of destruction. Its own chronicle records what its army took home.
M: The Thai kingdom sent its armies against Angkor, and the people of Angkor submitted. The Thai king took the Khmer king and all his family and his dancers and his musicians and his Brahmans to Ayutthaya.
N: From this account, Thailand is not the empire's destroyer. It is one of its heirs.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Hydraulic Archaeologists | academic
N: The fifth account is the newest, and it does not care who won any war. In April twenty twelve, a plane equipped with a laser system called LIDAR flew systematic passes over three hundred seventy square kilometers of jungle around the temples.
N: The results, published the next year by archaeologist Damian Evans and his colleagues, showed occupation mounds, canals, reservoir walls and road grids connecting every known temple into one continuous settlement, more than a thousand square kilometers of it.
M: The survey results confirm that the urban complex of Greater Angkor is the most extensive settlement system of the pre-industrial world.
N: Tree ring data collected by climate scientist Brendan Buckley found two periods of severe drought, each followed by catastrophic flooding, in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.
M: The coincidence between severe droughts, extreme floods, and the progressive decline of the Angkorian hydraulic system is now well established.
N: In this account, Angkor did not fall to an army. It de-densified, over decades, when a climate system it depended on stopped behaving the way six centuries of engineering assumed it would.

## REST | short

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each account is not carrying.
N: The Khmer court's own inscriptions leave out who quarried and carried the sandstone, and Zhou Daguan's account of slavery and corporal punishment inside that same court.
N: The French account leaves out that Cambodian monks were already living at Angkor Wat when Mouhot arrived, and that a Portuguese missionary had described it two hundred seventy four years earlier. It found a mystery in a place that had never stopped being tended.
N: The Cambodian nationalist account leaves out that the empire was never one people: Cham and Chinese communities lived inside its borders too, and Khmer identity itself was as much a royal court construction as it was a fixed people.
N: The Thai account leaves out the scale of what fourteen thirty one actually cost Angkor: its population dispersed and its urban system ended, whatever the chronicle calls the campaign.
N: And the newest account, the most recent and the most persuasive, leaves out the people's own choices. It describes them dispersing rather than choosing to relocate, and it explains a religious city only as infrastructure.

## CLOSE
N: The stones record six centuries of kings, gods, wars and water management. They do not record the end.
N: Angkor Wat stayed a Buddhist pilgrimage site after the royal court left for Phnom Penh and Lovek. Japanese visitors carved their names into its walls in the sixteen thirties.
N: Cambodian families were still living among the ruins when a French naturalist wrote home that he had found a lost city.
