## OPEN
N: Nelson Mandela is remembered as a man of patience, not war.
N: In December nineteen sixty one, the ANC's armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe, began attacking power stations and railway lines. Mandela had gone underground to help build it.
N: It chose to begin on the sixteenth of December: the day white South Africans celebrated their own military victory, over the Zulu, at Blood River, in eighteen thirty eight. The symbolism was deliberate.

## TITLE
N: Apartheid and its end. Nineteen forty eight to nineteen ninety four.

## SCENE 1 | An election apartheid didn't win
N: The twenty sixth of May, nineteen forty eight. The Herenigde Nasionale Party won seventy seats in South Africa's general election.
N: Jan Smuts's United Party had won more votes. Rural constituencies were weighted to overrepresent Afrikaner farming districts, and that counted for more than the vote count did.
N: D.F. Malan became prime minister. Within two years Parliament passed three laws: one that classified every South African into one of four racial groups, one that decided where each group could live, and one that banned marriage across the lines it had just drawn.
N: None of it was improvised. It was legislative, and it was indexed.

## SCENE 2 | Fifteen kilometers, twice a day
N: Hendrik Verwoerd became Minister of Native Affairs in nineteen fifty, and prime minister in nineteen fifty eight. He turned separate benches and separate entrances into something much larger: ten homelands, carved from thirteen percent of the country's land, for seventy five percent of its people.
N: A man commuting fifteen kilometers, from Soweto to Johannesburg, now crossed an international border twice a day. He carried a passbook. If he could not produce it on demand, he could be arrested.
N: Between nineteen forty eight and nineteen eighty six, seventeen million people were prosecuted under the pass laws.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | Sharpeville
N: The twenty first of March, nineteen sixty, Sharpeville. A crowd had gathered to protest the pass laws. Police opened fire. Sixty nine people were killed.
N: The African National Congress had been founded in nineteen twelve, and had spent decades petitioning. After Sharpeville, it and the Pan Africanist Congress were banned, and within the year it turned to armed struggle.

## DOCUMENT | Nelson Mandela | Statement from the dock, Rivonia Trial | April 20, 1964
N: Mandela had gone underground, training in Algeria and Ethiopia, before he was captured in August nineteen sixty two. At the Rivonia Trial two years later he spoke from the dock for four hours, without notes.
M: I have fought against white domination, and I have fought against black domination. I have cherished the ideal of a democratic and free society in which all persons live together in harmony and with equal opportunities. It is an ideal which I hope to live for and to achieve. But if needs be, it is an ideal for which I am prepared to die.
N: The judge sentenced Mandela and seven others to life imprisonment rather than death. Mandela was forty six years old. He would not leave Robben Island until nineteen ninety, at seventy one.

## ASIDE
N: The trial was meant to end the movement. Instead the speech became the founding text of the struggle it was meant to bury.
N: It would be twenty six more years before anyone at that trial sat down to negotiate.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | Soweto
N: The sixteenth of June, nineteen seventy six. Police in Soweto opened fire on schoolchildren marching against being taught in Afrikaans.
N: Hector Pieterson was twelve years old. He was among the first killed. A photographer named Sam Nzima caught Mbuyisa Makhubu carrying his body, with Pieterson's sister Antoinette running beside them. It became the most reproduced photograph of apartheid.
N: The uprising killed at least a hundred and seventy six people in its first week, and spread to a hundred and sixty communities within months.
N: Steve Biko was arrested at a roadblock near Grahamstown in August nineteen seventy seven. He died in police custody the next month, at thirty years old, of head injuries officers called a scuffle.

## SCENE 5 | Eleven hours in line
N: Four years of negotiation followed. The Boipatong massacre in June nineteen ninety two left forty five dead. Chris Hani was assassinated in April nineteen ninety three. The threat of a right wing Afrikaner uprising never fully went away.
N: On the twenty seventh of April, nineteen ninety four, nineteen point seven million South Africans voted in the country's first universal suffrage election. Lines stretched for kilometers. Some people waited eleven hours.
N: The African National Congress won sixty two point six percent of the vote.

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of forty six years. One of them, the Afrikaner Nationalist account, is not a sixth version of what happened. It is what was argued at the time, and it belongs here as evidence of that argument, not as a competing history.

## PERSPECTIVE | ANC and the Liberation Movement | victor
N: The first account is the movement's own, and it does not apologize for the guerrilla war.
N: After Sharpeville, decades of petitioning were judged to have failed. Mandela went underground, trained in Algeria and Ethiopia, and was captured in August nineteen sixty two.
N: On Robben Island the prisoners built what they called a university. Walter Sisulu, who had recruited Mandela to the ANC in nineteen forty four, served beside him there for twenty five years. Oliver Tambo ran the movement from exile in Lusaka for thirty years, and built the network that isolated the regime abroad.
N: Its case is that the transition was not a defeat. The ANC gave up immediate majority rule. De Klerk gave up white minority rule. Neither side capitulated.
N: Mandela put the argument this way, in nineteen ninety four.
M: If you want to make peace with your enemy, you have to work with your enemy. Then he becomes your partner.

## PERSPECTIVE | Afrikaner Nationalist | vanquished
N: The second account begins in eighteen thirty eight, at Blood River, where four hundred and seventy Voortrekkers defeated an estimated fifteen to twenty thousand Zulu warriors without a single Boer death. It called that a sign.
N: In the second Anglo Boer War, the British interned a hundred and sixteen thousand Afrikaner women and children in camps. Twenty seven thousand nine hundred and twenty seven died, most of them children under sixteen. For Afrikaners this was proof the English speaking world would destroy them given the chance.
N: Verwoerd argued apartheid was not oppression but separate development: each group governing itself, developing at its own pace, the way Britain was granting independence to its own African colonies.
N: Verwoerd told Parliament this in nineteen fifty nine.
M: We did not colonize the Bantu peoples' land. We found a largely empty country.
N: De Klerk's negotiations were received by many Afrikaners as betrayal.

## PERSPECTIVE | Black Consciousness Movement | revisionist
N: The third account says the first two are not the whole picture, and its target was not only the state.
N: Steve Biko was twenty three when he founded the South African Students' Organisation in nineteen sixty eight. His sharper argument was with white liberals who ran multiracial groups on their own terms and called it inclusion.
N: Liberation, Biko argued, had to start in the mind.
M: The most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed.
N: The students who marched in Soweto in nineteen seventy six were not ANC. The ANC had been banned for sixteen years. They were Biko's students, and their refusal to be taught in Afrikaans spread the uprising to a hundred and sixty communities.
N: Biko was arrested near Grahamstown in August nineteen seventy seven. He died in custody the next month, at thirty, of injuries police called a scuffle. The minister of justice said his death left him cold.

## PERSPECTIVE | International Sanctions Movement | bystander
N: The fourth account measures the distance between condemnation and cost.
N: The United Nations made the arms embargo mandatory in nineteen seventy seven, the first mandatory sanctions against a member state in its history. But the sharper weapon was sport. South Africa was expelled from the Olympic movement in nineteen seventy, and exclusion from rugby and cricket wounded Afrikaner identity in a way trade restrictions never did.
N: By nineteen eighty eight, a hundred and fifty five American companies had divested. Chase Manhattan's refusal in nineteen eighty five to renew short term loans cost the rand thirty five percent of its value in two weeks and forced a debt standstill.
N: Its case also names who slowed it down. Margaret Thatcher opposed sanctions at every Commonwealth summit.
F: The lady's not for sanctioning.
N: The United States Congress overrode Reagan's veto and passed sanctions into law anyway, in October nineteen eighty six.

## PERSPECTIVE | Truth and Reconciliation | academic
N: The fifth account is the one that had to end the other four without a war.
N: The security forces would not accept a transition that ended in trials. The ANC could not accept a blanket amnesty. The compromise, one sentence in the nineteen ninety three interim constitution, called for ubuntu, not vengeance.
N: Archbishop Desmond Tutu chaired the commission that followed. Between April nineteen ninety six and July nineteen ninety eight, it heard testimony from more than twenty one thousand victims and received more than seven thousand applications for amnesty.
N: Tutu's theology reframed the whole exercise.
M: Without forgiveness there is no future, but without confession there can be no forgiveness.

## REST | short

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each one has to leave out.
N: The movement's account does not linger on the necklacing carried out by ANC aligned groups in the townships it organized, or on Winnie Madikizela Mandela's football club and the killings associated with it.
N: The Afrikaner account calls the camps its Holocaust, and does not mention the three and a half million people the Group Areas Act itself forced out of their homes.
N: The Black Consciousness account credits itself with Soweto, and says little about the decades of ANC organizing that came before it, or about the activists who folded back into the ANC after Biko died.
N: The sanctions account counts the rand's drop and the companies that divested. It does not count the thousands killed in South African raids into Angola, Mozambique, and Botswana: the cost the front line states paid for other countries' pressure.
N: The reconciliation account produced a public record and almost no reparations. Most of what the commission recommended was never paid, and the businesses that profited from apartheid wages were never called to testify.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: Mandela was inaugurated on the tenth of May, nineteen ninety four, at the Union Buildings in Pretoria. Forty six years earlier, the apartheid state had been administered from the same complex.
N: The Truth and Reconciliation Commission later heard testimony from twenty one thousand victims and granted amnesty to fifteen hundred people who told the truth about what they had done.
N: In nineteen ninety four, white South Africans, ten percent of the population, owned eighty seven percent of the land. By twenty twenty four, that number had barely moved.
