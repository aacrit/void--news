## OPEN
N: You think you know how this one starts. A vendor sets himself on fire to protest a dictatorship, and the dictatorship falls.
N: That is not quite what happened. Mohamed Bouazizi did not stand outside a palace or a parliament. He stood outside the local governor's office, in a town of forty thousand people, after an inspector took his fruit cart and his scale, worth about two hundred dollars, and refused to let him inside to complain.
N: Two more presidents fell within the year that followed, in three completely different ways, and of the six countries this shook, exactly one became a democracy.

## TITLE
N: The Arab Spring. Two thousand ten to two thousand twelve.

## SCENE 1 | Sidi Bouzid
N: December the seventeenth, two thousand ten. Mohamed Bouazizi pushes his cart to its usual spot outside the governor's office in Sidi Bouzid. He is twenty six.
N: A municipal inspector named Faida Hamdi confiscates the cart and the electronic scale. Witnesses say she slaps him. He goes inside to complain. They refuse to see him.
N: At eleven thirty in the morning, he stands in front of the building, douses himself in paint thinner, and strikes a match.
N: He is taken to a hospital in Sfax. He dies on January the fourth.
N: Protests reach Kasserine, Thala, and Tunis within days. Zine El Abidine Ben Ali, who has ruled Tunisia for twenty three years, sends riot police and snipers. At least three hundred thirty eight people are killed.
N: On January the fourteenth, twenty eight days after Bouazizi struck the match, Ben Ali boards a plane for Jeddah. He never comes back.

## DOCUMENT | Chant, attributed to Tunisian demonstrators | December 2010
N: The same three words are attributed to crowds within weeks, in Tunis, in Cairo's Tahrir Square, in Tripoli, Sanaa, Manama, and Damascus.
F: Ash-sha'b yurid isqat an-nizam. The people want the fall of the regime.

## ASIDE
N: Three words, and no leader, no party, and no manifesto behind them beyond that. It is the only thing every uprising in this story agrees on, and it is the last thing they agree on.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | The square
N: Egypt follows. On January the twenty fifth, 2011, tens of thousands march in Cairo, Alexandria, and Suez.
N: By the Day of Rage, January the twenty eighth, the police have been overrun and the army has deployed. By February the first, over a million people fill Tahrir Square.

## DOCUMENT | Unnamed protester, Tahrir Square | Battle of the Camel footage, broadcast by Al Jazeera | February 2, 2011
N: On February the second, pro Mubarak men on horses and camels charge into the crowd. An unnamed protester in the square, unnamed in the record, is filmed saying this.
F: We are peaceful. We are peaceful. They are killing us.

N: Hosni Mubarak, who has ruled Egypt for thirty years, addresses the nation on February the tenth and refuses to resign. The crowd watches on a giant screen and erupts in fury. The next day, his vice president appears on television for thirty seconds to say Mubarak has decided to step down. Mubarak is eighty two.

## SCENE 3 | The pipe
N: Libya's uprising begins February the fifteenth. Muammar Gaddafi, in power for forty two years, promises to hunt protesters alley by alley, house by house.

## DOCUMENT | Muammar Gaddafi | Televised address from Bab al-Azizia, Tripoli | February 22, 2011
N: A week later, Gaddafi says this, on television, from his own compound.
M: I will fight on to the last drop of my blood. Muammar Gaddafi is the leader of the revolution. I am the glory of Libya, I am the glory of Africa.
N: NATO intervenes on March the nineteenth. Seven thousand seven hundred air strikes follow over seven months. On October the twentieth, rebel fighters drag Gaddafi from a drainage pipe outside Sirte, beat him, and shoot him.
N: Three dictators. Two hundred eighty one days.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | Everyone else
N: The other three uprisings never get a Sirte moment.
N: In Bahrain, a Shia majority marches for political rights against a Sunni monarchy. On March the fourteenth, twelve hundred Saudi troops cross the King Fahd Causeway to crush it.
N: In Yemen, a journalist named Tawakkol Karman has been organizing protests in Sanaa's Change Square since two thousand seven. That October she becomes the first Arab woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

## DOCUMENT | Tawakkol Karman | Nobel Peace Prize acceptance lecture, Oslo City Hall | December 10, 2011
N: In December, in Oslo, Karman says this.
F: History has proven that criminal individuals who have committed crimes against humanity have had the worst ends.

N: The following month, Yemen's president signs away power after eleven months of protests and defections. The country collapses into civil war within three years.
N: In Syria, Bashar al-Assad simply refuses to leave. By twenty twenty six the war that followed has killed more than five hundred thousand people and displaced six million six hundred thousand more as refugees.

## REST

## SCENE 5 | The fork
N: Tunisia holds free elections in October 2011.
N: Egypt holds its first competitive presidential election in June 2012. The Muslim Brotherhood's candidate wins it.
N: One year later, on July the third, 2013, General Sisi removes him in a military coup.
N: Libya fractures into rival governments and militia rule. Syria becomes a proxy war. Yemen becomes a famine. Morocco and Jordan survive through limited constitutional reform.
N: Of the six countries where a ruler was directly challenged, one becomes a democracy.

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of what those years meant, and none of them agree.

## PERSPECTIVE | The revolutionaries | revisionist
N: The first account belongs to the people who built it, and it begins six months before Bouazizi, in that internet cafe in Alexandria.
N: The photograph Khaled Said's family took at the morgue, jaw broken, skull fractured, teeth knocked out, became the Facebook page that had half a million followers within three months, and it is the page that posted the call to march on January the twenty fifth.
N: The revolutionaries had no vanguard party, no ideology, no armed wing. In Tunisia, a labor union with five hundred thousand members called the strikes that paralyzed the country. In Egypt, the square itself became a government: field hospitals, food committees, a newspaper printed on the pavement.
N: Here is Ghonim, from Tahrir, days after Mubarak fell.
M: The barrier of fear has been broken.

## PERSPECTIVE | The regime | bystander
N: The second account is the one every falling government was making at the time, in its own words, and Syria said it loudest.
N: On January the thirty first, 2011, Bashar al-Assad told the Wall Street Journal this.
M: Syria is stable. Why? Because you have to be very closely linked to the beliefs of the people.
N: Five weeks later his own security forces were firing on unarmed demonstrators in Daraa.
N: The argument had three parts. Order: Egypt's state employed five point six million people, and its army owned an estimated twenty five to forty percent of the economy. Identity: a state guaranteeing Alawites, Christians, and Druze against a Sunni majority. And the alternative: look at what happens when the state actually falls, in Libya.
N: Saudi Arabia spent one hundred thirty billion dollars on housing, unemployment benefits, and a new minimum wage, before a single protester reached its own streets.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Muslim Brotherhood | victor
N: The third account belongs to an organization that had been organizing since 1928, eighty three years before this moment.
N: By 2011 it had chapters in every Arab country and the deepest grassroots network in the region: clinics and schools that reached millions the state had abandoned. When Egypt held its first free parliamentary elections that November, its party took thirty seven and a half percent of the vote, and Islamist parties together held seventy three percent of parliament.
N: That was not a hijacking of the revolution. It was eighty years of organizing the secular opposition had not done.
N: Mohammed Morsi won the presidential runoff in June 2012 with fifty one point seven percent of the vote. Here he is, campaigning in Tahrir Square that same month.
M: We do not want anything from the revolution. We want what the people want.
N: His government lasted three hundred sixty eight days.

## PERSPECTIVE | The realists | revisionist
N: The fourth account says the revolution ended the day outside money noticed it, and it can put a date on that.
N: On March the fourteenth, 2011, twelve hundred Saudi troops crossed the King Fahd Causeway into Bahrain, to make sure a Shia majority near Saudi Arabia's own oil fields never got what Tunisia got.
N: Qatar funded the Brotherhood through Al Jazeera and roughly three billion dollars in direct support. Saudi Arabia deposited five billion dollars in Egypt's central bank within weeks of the coup that removed it. The revolution was fought with chants. The counter revolution was fought with checkbooks.
N: NATO's seven thousand seven hundred air strikes over Libya were authorized to protect civilians and ended by removing a government. Russia cites Libya as the reason it vetoed Syria resolutions seventeen times.
N: Here is what Hillary Clinton, the American secretary of state, said to CBS News when Gaddafi was found and killed.
F: We came, we saw, he died.

## PERSPECTIVE | Women's experience | vanquished
N: The fifth account starts less than a month after Mubarak fell, when the Egyptian military detained seventeen women from Tahrir Square and forced them to submit to what soldiers called virginity tests, examinations conducted by a male military doctor while soldiers watched.
N: A protester named Samira Ibrahim sued the military over it. She won. The doctor was acquitted on appeal.
N: Women had been in this from day one. On January the eighteenth, a twenty six year old named Asmaa Mahfouz posted a video calling Egyptians to march.
F: I, a girl, am going down to Tahrir Square, and I will stand alone. And I will hold up a banner. Perhaps people will show some honor.
N: Tunisia guaranteed gender equality in its 2014 constitution and equalized inheritance rights. Egypt's post coup constitution had fewer protections for women than the one it replaced. Human Rights Watch counted at least one hundred eighty six cases of mob sexual assault in Tahrir Square between 2012 and 2014.

## REST | short

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what each account cannot afford to say.
N: The revolutionaries call having no vanguard their virtue, and say less about the cost. Egypt's first free parliament gave Islamist parties seventy three percent of the seats.
N: The regime's account counts the jobs and the sects it protected, and says almost nothing about the corruption and the police brutality that put people in the street to begin with.
N: The Brotherhood counts its votes, and says little about how it governed with them: a constitutional declaration Morsi granted himself, and fuel shortages that vanished within forty eight hours of the coup that removed him.
N: The realist account explains the money, and reduces everyone underneath it to a pawn. Millions of people had genuine grievances that no foreign treasury created.
N: And the women's account is the best documented of the five, and still the smallest audience. Tunisia is the one place any of it held, and even there it took a labor union.
N: Look past all five accounts, and the same three words produced a democracy, a military dictatorship, a failed state, a civil war, a famine, and two monarchies that bought their way out of hearing them.
