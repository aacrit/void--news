## OPEN
N: Berlin, March nineteen twenty one. A young man walks up to a middle aged Turkish exile on a residential street, shoots him once in the head, and waits to be arrested.
N: He is tried for murder in a German court. He does not deny doing it. The jury acquits him in two days.
N: A law student in Poland read the transcript and could not get past one question. How is it that killing one man is a crime with a courtroom and a jury, and killing a million people has no name at all?
N: He spent the next twenty three years building the word.

## TITLE
N: The Armenian genocide. Nineteen fifteen to nineteen twenty three.

## SCENE 1 | The twenty fourth of April
N: Constantinople, the night of the twenty fourth of April, nineteen fifteen.
N: Ottoman police arrest two hundred and thirty five Armenians in the capital. Not soldiers. Writers, clergy, doctors, lawyers, editors, the men who ran the community's institutions.
N: By the end of May the number is six hundred. They are sent inland to two towns in central Anatolia.
N: Almost none of them come back.
N: That is the date Armenians mark every year, and it is worth noticing what it marks. Not the first killing. The removal of the people who could have organised a response.

## SCENE 2 | Disarmed first
N: Two months before that, the war minister had ordered every Armenian soldier in the Ottoman army disarmed.
N: Somewhere between a hundred thousand and two hundred thousand conscripts were taken out of their units and put into labour battalions, building roads and digging trenches.
N: By the spring they were being marched out in groups of fifty to a hundred and shot.
N: Take those two facts together and the shape is clear. The men of military age were already in custody and already unarmed. The leadership was already gone.
N: What was left in the eastern provinces was women, children and the old.

## SCENE 3 | The law and the road
N: On the twenty seventh of May a deportation law was passed, authorising the military to relocate any population considered a security risk.
N: What it authorised in practice was columns of people walking south into the Syrian desert, escorted by gendarmes and irregular cavalry, along routes that ran to the Euphrates.
N: At set points along the way the columns were stopped, stripped of what they still carried, separated by sex, and attacked. Tens of thousands of women and girls were taken into households, married by force, or sold.

## DOCUMENT | Jesse B. Jackson | Dispatch to the American Embassy | September 29, 1915
N: Jesse Jackson was the American consul at Aleppo, watching the columns arrive from the north.
M: I have the honor to report to Your Excellency that the deportation of the Armenians from the eastern provinces and their massacre en route continues. A more fiendish or diabolical scheme to exterminate a people has never been devised.

## SCENE 4 | The end of the road
N: At Deir ez-Zor, where the routes ended, the survivors were held in open desert. No shelter, no food, no water supply.
N: In nineteen sixteen the administration there was replaced and the new man's job was to empty the camps. By August they had been emptied, by mass shooting and by burning.
N: The Euphrates carried bodies downstream for months. People living along the river reported it for the rest of their lives.

## DOCUMENT | Talat Pasha | Conversation recorded in 1915
N: And this is the Ottoman interior minister, Talat Pasha, the man who signed the orders, talking about his own results.
M: We have already disposed of three quarters of the Armenians. There are none at all left in Bitlis, Van, and Erzeroum.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts, and the same thing has to be said here as in the other episode of this kind.
N: These are not five positions on whether this happened. There is no serious argument about that, and this programme is not going to stage one. They are five vantage points, and one of them is the account the perpetrators gave at the time, which is evidence rather than a competing version of events.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Ottoman government | victor
N: The first is the case the government made while it was doing it.
N: The Ottoman Empire in nineteen fifteen was fighting on three fronts and losing on most of them. In January the war minister had taken an army into the Caucasus in winter and lost somewhere between sixty and ninety thousand men in a matter of weeks.
N: Armenian communities were concentrated in six eastern provinces along the Russian border. Some Armenians had joined Russian volunteer units. The government's position was that a population sitting on the invasion route, with co-religionists on the other side of it, was a military risk.
N: So the word used in the law was relocation, and the official framing was wartime security. This is also the foundation of the denial that came afterwards, which is why it is worth hearing in its original form.

## PERSPECTIVE | The survivors and the diaspora | vanquished
N: The second account is testimony, and one witness shows what became of it.
N: Aurora Mardiganian was fourteen when the gendarmes came to her family's house. She saw her father and her brother killed. She was marched south, her mother died on the road, she was sold twice, held in a harem, escaped, and reached Tiflis disguised as a Kurdish boy.
N: Her memoir came out in nineteen eighteen. The following year it was made into an American film, and she played herself in it. She was eighteen, re-enacting her own abduction and the deaths of her family for a camera, to raise relief money.
N: That is the shape of the whole archive. Survivor testimony collected, organised and published by a scattered people with no state to keep records for them.
N: There are now about seven million Armenians in the diaspora and under three million in Armenia. The dispersion is not a side effect of the killing. It is the killing's remaining form.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The witnesses | bystander
N: The third account belongs to the foreigners who were there and wrote it down.
N: The American ambassador in Constantinople, Henry Morgenthau, had direct access to the men running it, and he used it. He met the interior minister repeatedly through nineteen fifteen and recorded what was said to him.
M: When the Turkish authorities gave the orders for these deportations, they were merely giving the death warrant to a whole race; they understood this well, and in their conversations with me they made no particular attempt to conceal the fact.
N: His cables reached Washington in July. The United States was not at war with the Ottoman Empire and never declared war on it.
N: Nothing followed. German and Austrian diplomats, allied to the Ottomans and therefore in an even better position to see, filed similar reports to Berlin and Vienna. Those archives are now among the strongest evidence there is.

## PERSPECTIVE | Genocide studies | revisionist
N: The fourth account is the scholarly one, and its work is mostly the work of answering the first.
N: The security argument has a sequence problem. The soldiers were disarmed in February, before any deportation was ordered. The men arrested in the capital on the twenty fourth of April were doctors and editors living hundreds of miles from the Russian front.
N: And the government's own minister described the outcome as the disposal of three quarters of a people, in a private conversation, while it was happening.
N: What the field adds beyond that is the afterwards. Impunity is a finding too.

## DOCUMENT | Adolf Hitler | Remark to his commanders | August 22, 1939
N: A week before Germany invaded Poland, Hitler is recorded telling his commanders one sentence that is quoted in almost every history of both events.
M: Who, after all, speaks today of the annihilation of the Armenians?

## PERSPECTIVE | The Convention | academic
N: The fifth account is the one the law took, and it starts back on that Berlin street.
N: The man who was acquitted had lost his family in the deportations. The man he shot was the interior minister.
N: Raphael Lemkin, reading the transcript, went looking for the legal category and found there wasn't one. He proposed two new crimes at an international conference in nineteen thirty three, destruction of a group and destruction of its culture, and was turned down. His own foreign ministry pressured him to withdraw the paper.
N: Eleven years later, having lost most of his own family in the Holocaust, he published the word. Genocide. Greek for a people, Latin for killing.
N: The United Nations convention against it was adopted in nineteen forty eight and it is a direct product of his drafting.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each account leaves out.
N: The government's account cannot survive its own timeline. It explains a border province and not a round-up of editors in the capital, and it is contradicted in the words of the minister who ordered it.
N: The survivors' account is the indispensable one and it was shaped by its conditions. The best known testimony was produced for American audiences by organisations raising money, which does not make it false and does mean the archive we have was formed partly by what could be published.
N: The witnesses' account is a record of reporting rather than of acting. The cables went, and were read, and nothing followed, and a story in which the horrified diplomat is the main character is a comfortable one for the countries he was writing to.
N: Genocide studies grew out of advocacy and still carries it. Counting how many parliaments have voted to use the word is a political scoreboard, not a historical finding, and it invites the reply that the history is a matter of votes.
N: And the account of the law has the weakest record of all of them. The convention has been invoked far more often than it has been enforced, and the thing that set it in motion was a murder that a jury declined to punish.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: Between nineteen twenty and nineteen twenty two, Armenian operatives tracked down and killed the three men most responsible, in Berlin, in Tbilisi and in Rome. That is what justice looked like when there was no court.
N: Turkey has denied that this was a genocide for over a century. Thirty four countries have formally recognised it.
N: The word itself did not exist until nineteen forty four, which means that for twenty nine years the thing that had been done to the Armenians could only be described, never named.
N: And the date on the calendar every year is not the day the killing began. It is the night two hundred and thirty five people were taken from their houses in one city, because they were the ones who might have organised an answer.
