## OPEN
N: You picture this as an assembly line. Guards, a queue, a single shot.
N: The tribunal that spent sixteen years counting the dead found something else. Of the one and a half million to two million who died, about eight hundred thousand died of starvation and disease. About five hundred thousand were executed.
N: And execution rarely meant a bullet. At the killing fields outside Phnom Penh, guards killed with iron bars, bamboo sticks, machetes, and sharpened palm leaves. A bullet cost money the regime did not want to spend.

## TITLE
N: The Cambodian Genocide. Nineteen seventy five to nineteen seventy nine.

## SCENE 1 | Forty eight hours
N: The morning of April the seventeenth, nineteen seventy five. Soldiers in black uniforms walk into Phnom Penh and order the city emptied.
N: Hospital patients are wheeled out of their wards, still in their beds. Monks in saffron robes join the column. Women in labor are marched at gunpoint.
N: The soldiers say it is temporary. Three days, maybe a week, until the Americans stop bombing.
N: The Americans have already stopped bombing.
N: Within forty eight hours, two million people are on the road out of the capital. None of them are coming back.

## ASIDE
N: The regime called itself Democratic Kampuchea. Its leader, Saloth Sar, went by the name Pol Pot. He had studied radio electronics in Paris in the early nineteen fifties and joined the French Communist Party there.
N: His plan for Cambodia was Year Zero. No money. No markets. No schools. No religion. No cities. Anyone who had worn the old society, teachers, doctors, monks, anyone who wore glasses or spoke a foreign language, was an enemy to be, in the party's own word, smashed.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | A dam with no engineers
N: At Trapeang Thma, in Battambang Province, ten thousand workers built an irrigation dam with hand tools, to a design drawn by cadres with no engineering training.
N: About four thousand of them died there, of exhaustion, starvation, and execution.
N: The dam failed.
N: Across the cooperatives, cadres reported rice yields of three tons a hectare. The actual harvest was one ton. The surplus, the fictional two tons, was exported to China anyway, for weapons.
N: The famine that followed killed hundreds of thousands of people.

## DOCUMENT | Loung Ung | First They Killed My Father | 2000
N: Loung Ung was five years old when the soldiers came. This is what she wrote about her sister.
F: My little sister was beaten to death with a hoe in front of my eyes for stealing a handful of rice. She was six years old.

## SCENE 3 | Fourteen thousand faces
N: A former high school in Phnom Penh was redesignated Security Prison Twenty One. Between nineteen seventy five and nineteen seventy nine it processed an estimated fourteen thousand to seventeen thousand prisoners.
N: Every arrival was photographed before interrogation. The commander, Kaing Guek Eav, went by the name Duch, and he kept meticulous logs: intake, confession, execution schedule.
N: Of the thousands who entered, between seven and twelve came out alive. One of them, a painter named Vann Nath, was kept alive to paint portraits of Pol Pot.

## DOCUMENT | Khmer Rouge slogan | Documented by survivors and displayed at the Tuol Sleng Genocide Museum | 1975-1979
N: The line painted on the wall inside that prison, the Khmer Rouge's own words for what was happening in it.
M: To keep you is no benefit. To destroy you is no loss.

## SCENE 4 | Thirteen days
N: December the twenty fifth, nineteen seventy eight. One hundred fifty thousand Vietnamese troops cross the Cambodian border.
N: By January the seventh, nineteen seventy nine, they hold Phnom Penh. The Khmer Rouge flee west to the Thai border and keep fighting as a guerrilla force for another twenty years.
N: Three years, eight months, and twenty days after it started, the killing is over.

## DOCUMENT | Dith Pran | Testimony to the ECCC | 2009
N: Dith Pran was a Cambodian journalist for the New York Times who lived through all of it. This is what he told the tribunal, thirty years later.
M: They told us to leave the city for three days. Three days became three years, eight months, and twenty days.

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of who did this, who stopped it, and who was allowed to keep its seat at the United Nations for twelve years afterward.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Khmer Rouge | victor
N: The party's own case starts with a war it did not choose. Between nineteen sixty nine and nineteen seventy three, American bombers dropped two point seven million tons of ordnance on Cambodia, more than the Allies dropped on Japan in the whole of the Second World War. An estimated one hundred thousand to one hundred fifty thousand Cambodian civilians died in it, and the Khmer Rouge recruited heavily from the villages it hit.
N: To the party, the cities were parasitic, filled with merchants and bureaucrats who produced nothing, while eighty five percent of Cambodians already lived on the land the revolution needed. The evacuation of Phnom Penh was framed as economic necessity, not cruelty.
N: The purges that followed were framed the same way, as defense against enemies inside the party working for the CIA or for Vietnam. Pol Pot never accepted that the killing had a different scale than that.
N: He gave one interview in his life, twenty years after the fact, to a journalist named Nate Thayer.
M: I came to carry out the struggle, not to kill people. Even now, and you can look at me, am I a savage person? My conscience is clear.
N: He died in a bamboo shack near the Thai border in April nineteen ninety eight, without ever facing a court.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Survivors | vanquished
N: The account with no interest in framing anything is the one that lived it. Loung Ung's father was taken by the cadres and never came back. Her mother was taken next. Two of her sisters disappeared. Loung herself was put in a children's work brigade digging irrigation ditches twelve hours a day on a single bowl of watered rice.
N: Starvation, not execution, was what killed most people. The regime exported rice to China while its own population starved, and anyone caught foraging for leaves, insects, or bark faced execution for what the cadres called individualism.
N: Wearing glasses could be a death sentence. The regime read them as a mark of the educated class it had already decided to eliminate.
N: Haing Ngor was a Cambodian doctor who survived by never admitting what he was.
M: I could not tell them I was a doctor. To be a doctor was to be dead.
N: He watched his pregnant wife die of starvation and untreated illness rather than say the word that would have gotten them both killed.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Vietnamese Liberation | bystander
N: Vietnam's own account starts at its border, not Cambodia's capital. From nineteen seventy seven, Khmer Rouge units raided Vietnamese villages across the frontier, and the party's Eastern Zone purge, aimed at cadres it suspected of Vietnamese sympathies, killed about one hundred thousand of its own people and drove the survivors, including a commander named Heng Samrin, to defect across that same border.
N: The Vietnamese government framed the invasion that followed as something international law did not yet have a name for.
M: We could not stand by while our people were being massacred across the border and a genocide was being committed against the Cambodian people.
N: One hundred fifty thousand Vietnamese troops crossed on December the twenty fifth, nineteen seventy eight, and Phnom Penh fell within two weeks. Soldiers entering the city found it empty, the National Bank dynamited, currency scattered in the streets. A Vietnamese journalist, Ho Van Tay, was among the first to walk into Tuol Sleng and find the prison's own photographs.
N: A doctrine allowing exactly this kind of intervention was adopted by the United Nations in two thousand five. Vietnam was condemned for doing it thirty years early.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Tribunal | academic
N: The Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia opened its first trial in two thousand nine, thirty years after the killing ended. It convicted three people. Duch, in two thousand ten, later extended to life on appeal. Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan, both sentenced to life in two thousand eighteen. It cost three hundred thirty seven million dollars and took sixteen years, longer than the regime itself lasted.
N: The tribunal's historians put a shape on the number. Of Cambodia's four hundred thousand to five hundred thousand ethnic Vietnamese, almost all were killed or expelled. Of two hundred fifty thousand ethnic Chinese, fifty percent died. Of the country's two hundred thousand Cham Muslims, thirty six percent were killed, their mosques destroyed and their language banned. Of sixty six thousand Buddhist monks in nineteen seventy five, fewer than three thousand survived.
N: The regime documented almost all of it itself. Six thousand of the Tuol Sleng photographs survive today. They were taken by a Khmer Rouge cadre named Nhem En, who was fifteen years old when he got the job.
M: I took the photos. That was my job.
N: He said it decades later, to a reporter, without apparent difficulty.

## PERSPECTIVE | Regional Intervention | bystander
N: A separate account puts the invasion inside a bigger board. In April nineteen seventy seven, Khmer Rouge forces struck the Vietnamese village of Ba Chuc and killed three thousand one hundred fifty seven civilians over two weeks. Vietnam still maintains a memorial there.
N: China, the Khmer Rouge's patron, answered Vietnam's invasion by invading Vietnam itself in February nineteen seventy nine, a punitive war that killed an estimated twenty thousand soldiers in twenty nine days. The United States, which had bombed Cambodia harder than it bombed Japan in the whole of the Second World War, opposed Vietnam's invasion anyway, because Vietnam was a Soviet ally.
N: Carter's national security adviser, Zbigniew Brzezinski, said what the policy actually was.
M: I encouraged the Chinese to support Pol Pot. Pol Pot was an abomination. We could never support him, but China could.
N: Britain trained Khmer Rouge aligned fighters through the nineteen eighties. The deposed regime kept Cambodia's seat at the United Nations until nineteen ninety one, twelve years after it fell.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each account is not carrying.
N: The party's account explains where the anger came from and says nothing about the ethnic Vietnamese, Chinese, and Cham Muslims it targeted for who they were, or the party's own cadres tortured for confessions inside its own prison.
N: The survivors' account has no interest in the party's reasoning, and it says nothing about the base people, the peasants who lived under the same system and sometimes ran it.
N: The Vietnamese liberation account does not linger on its own interest in the government it installed, or the ten years its occupying troops then stayed.
N: The tribunal's account is built from confession archives and casualty counts, and it has no room in it for what any one survivor actually felt.
N: And the account of the wider Cold War board has nothing to say about what Vietnam's own decade of occupation cost the Cambodians living under it.

## CLOSE
N: In nineteen seventy nine, after the killing stopped, Cambodia had fewer than fifty doctors left in the entire country.
N: The median age in Cambodia today is twenty six. More than half the country was born after it ended.
