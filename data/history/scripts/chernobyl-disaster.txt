## OPEN
N: On the morning of the twenty eighth of April, nineteen eighty six, a routine radiation monitor at a nuclear plant in Sweden went off as a worker walked in from outside.
N: They checked their own reactor first, and found nothing wrong with it. Then they worked out that the contamination was on his shoes, that it had come in from the air, and that the wind that morning was coming from the southeast.
N: That is how the world found out. Two days after the explosion, from a shoe, in a different country.

## TITLE
N: Chernobyl. The twenty sixth of April, nineteen eighty six.

## SCENE 1 | Twenty three minutes past one
N: One twenty three and forty seconds in the morning. Reactor number four at the Chernobyl plant in Soviet Ukraine.
N: The operators are running a safety test. It is a rehearsal for a blackout: they want to know whether the spinning turbine can power the cooling pumps in the seconds before the diesel generators start.
N: The test has already been delayed for hours, and has been handed to a night shift that did not plan it and did not expect to run it.
N: They press the button marked A Z five. It is the emergency shutdown. It drives every control rod into the core at once, and it is the thing you press when you want the reaction to stop.
N: Because of how the rods were built, with graphite on their tips, the first thing they did on the way in was displace water and briefly increase the reaction rather than stop it.
N: Within seconds the core was at more than a hundred times its rated power.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | The roof
N: Two explosions lifted the upper shield of the reactor, a disc of steel and concrete weighing a thousand tonnes, and dropped it back at an angle. The reactor hall was open to the sky.
N: Burning graphite from the core was thrown across the site and onto the roof of the neighbouring reactor building.
N: Firefighters arrived five minutes later. They had no protective clothing, no dosimeters worth the name, and no reason to believe this was anything other than a fire.
N: They scraped glowing chunks of reactor graphite off the roof with their boots. Some described a metallic taste. Several of them were dead within weeks.

## DOCUMENT | Lyudmilla Ignatenko | Voices from Chernobyl, oral history collected by Svetlana Alexievich | 1997
N: One of those firefighters was Vasily Ignatenko. His wife Lyudmilla followed him to the hospital in Moscow and stayed with him while he died of acute radiation syndrome.
F: We didn't know that death could be so beautiful. He was radiating.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | Thirty six hours
N: Three kilometres away was Pripyat: a city of forty nine thousand people, built in nineteen seventy to house the plant's workers. The average age was twenty six.
N: On the morning of the twenty sixth, children went to school. People fished on the cooling channel. There were weddings that afternoon.
N: Above them an open reactor core was burning in the air.
N: The evacuation came on the afternoon of the twenty seventh, roughly thirty six hours after the explosion. Twelve hundred buses came from Kyiv. Residents were told to bring documents and enough for three days.
N: They were never allowed back. The city is still there, and still empty.

## DOCUMENT | TASS | first public announcement, read on Soviet television | April 28, 1986
N: The Soviet state's first public statement came on the evening news of the twenty eighth, after Sweden had already told the world.
M: An accident has occurred at the Chernobyl nuclear power plant. One of the reactors was damaged. Measures are being taken to eliminate the consequences.

## ASIDE
N: That is the entire announcement. Two and a half days, four sentences, no evacuation notice, and no mention that the fire was still burning.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | The liquidators
N: Roughly six hundred thousand people were eventually sent into the zone to clean it. They were called liquidators.
N: On the most contaminated section of roof, robots failed. The radiation destroyed their electronics. So men were sent instead, in lead aprons, running out to shovel graphite for ninety seconds at a time before running back.
N: Many were conscripts. Many were never told their real dose.
N: What most of them received afterwards was a certificate confirming they had been there.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Chernobyl is not really disputed as a sequence of events. What is disputed is who it belongs to, and how many people it killed.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Soviet state | victor
N: The first account is the official one, and its logic is that this was a secret before it was an emergency.
N: Classification governed the response. Radiation figures released domestically were a fraction of the measured values. Evacuation was delayed while the decision went up the chain and back.
N: And when an explanation was finally required, it was operator error: the men in the control room had broken procedure. This had the advantage of protecting the reactor design, and the entire Soviet nuclear programme built on it.
M: The reactor was not the problem. The people who operated it were the problem.

## PERSPECTIVE | The liquidators and the evacuated | vanquished
N: The second account rejects the arithmetic entirely.
N: Its argument is that a death toll is the wrong instrument: that people were kept in their ordinary routines for thirty six hours under an open radiological fire, that men worked in shifts measured in seconds without being told what they were absorbing, and that what happened to them is individual rather than statistical.
N: One liquidator's testimony puts the compensation in proportion.
M: They gave us certificates. A certificate that says I was there. As if that could unburn what was burned.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The epidemiologists | academic
N: The third account is a genuine scientific disagreement, and the gap in it is enormous.
N: The confirmed immediate deaths number about thirty one. The World Health Organisation's Chernobyl Forum projected up to four thousand among the most exposed. Modelled European studies produce figures between sixteen and sixty thousand. Greenpeace published ninety three thousand and above.
N: The clearly attributable population effect is thyroid cancer in children exposed to contaminated milk: more than six thousand cases.
N: The disagreement is about method. Applying a linear model of low dose risk across millions of lightly exposed people generates very large numbers from very small individual risks, and whether that is legitimate is itself contested.
M: The number of deaths will never be known with precision. The cancers are real but statistically invisible against the background rate.
N: The counter argument is about what invisibility is used for.
M: To dismiss the low-dose deaths because they cannot be individually identified is to hide a catastrophe inside a statistic.

## PERSPECTIVE | The reactor engineers | academic
N: The fourth account arrived six years late and reversed the verdict.
N: In nineteen ninety two, a second international report, INSAG seven, moved the blame from the operators to the design. The RBMK reactor had a positive void coefficient, meaning that as its cooling water boiled away the reaction sped up rather than slowed.
N: And the control rods were tipped with graphite, so the emergency shutdown briefly added power before removing it.
N: That flaw was known. It had appeared at another plant in nineteen eighty three. It was not corrected across the fleet, and it was not disclosed to the men who operated them.
M: They pushed the button that was supposed to stop the reactor, and it blew up.

## PERSPECTIVE | Glasnost and the collapse | revisionist
N: The fifth account treats the disaster as a political event.
N: Gorbachev had been in office for thirteen months, promising openness. Then the state's reflex was to say nothing for two and a half days, while the fallout crossed borders and made the silence impossible to maintain.
N: The information monopoly did not survive it. Gorbachev himself later made the strongest version of the claim.
M: The Chernobyl accident, even more than my launch of perestroika, was perhaps the real cause of the collapse of the Soviet Union.

## REST

## TURN | The shape of the disagreement
N: Each account leaves out what would most complicate it.
N: The Soviet account leaves out the positive void coefficient, the thirty six hours, and the suppressed readings.
N: The liquidators' account leaves out that the containment they built actually worked, and that without them it would have been far worse.
N: The epidemiological account leaves out the testimonial reality that a confidence interval cannot hold, and it leaves out that the nuclear industry has an interest in the low number.
N: The engineers' account leaves out the people almost entirely. It is an account of a machine.
N: And the political account leaves out everything that is not political: the health effects, the emptied towns, and the other causes of Soviet collapse that had nothing to do with a reactor.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: The reactor is now inside a steel arch, a hundred and eight metres high, moved into place in twenty sixteen. It is designed to last a hundred years, which is not the same as being finished.
N: Pripyat is still standing. The school, the swimming pool, the fairground with a ferris wheel that was due to open five days after the explosion and never carried a passenger.
N: The exclusion zone is now, by some measures, one of the more successful wildlife reserves in Europe, which tells you what the main threat to wildlife in Europe usually is.
N: And the world still found out from a shoe.
