## OPEN
N: The story everybody gets is a tired woman on a bus.
N: Rosa Parks was forty two, she was not unusually tired that day, and she was not the first. Nine months earlier a fifteen year old in the same city had refused the same thing on the same bus line.
N: What made the first of December nineteen fifty five different was not the refusal. It was that the refusal had been waited for. Parks was the secretary of the local branch of the NAACP, the plan was already drafted, and the machine went off within hours.

## TITLE
N: The American civil rights movement. Nineteen fifty four to nineteen sixty eight.

## SCENE 1 | Fifty two thousand leaflets
N: The night of the arrest, a teacher named Jo Ann Robinson let herself into a college building in Montgomery and ran off fifty two thousand five hundred leaflets on a mimeograph.
N: They were on the streets of the Black neighbourhoods by morning.
N: Within forty eight hours the committee had three hundred volunteer drivers, running what amounted to a parallel transit system.
N: They picked a twenty six year old pastor to front it. He had been in Montgomery just over a year, which was the qualification. No history, no enemies, nobody's rival.
N: The boycott ran three hundred and eighty one days and took about sixty five per cent of the bus company's revenue with it. Then the Supreme Court ruled the segregated buses unconstitutional.

## SCENE 2 | The crack in the law
N: The legal ground had already moved. In nineteen fifty four, in Brown against Board of Education, the Court threw out a fifty seven year old doctrine in a unanimous opinion, and the line it turned on was that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.
N: The ruling was about schools. Its logic dissolved the legal basis of the whole system.
N: The response had a name. Massive resistance. In nineteen fifty six, a hundred and one members of Congress signed a manifesto calling the ruling an abuse of judicial power.
N: One Virginia county closed every public school it had, for five years, rather than integrate. White children went to private academies funded by state tuition grants. Black children had no school at all.
N: In nineteen fifty seven the governor of Arkansas put the National Guard across the door of a high school to keep nine students out, and the President sent the hundred and first Airborne to walk them in.

## SCENE 3 | Project C
N: Birmingham, Alabama, spring nineteen sixty three. The campaign there was called Project C, and the C stood for confrontation.
N: The city's commissioner of public safety answered it with fire hoses and police dogs, and the marchers included children. Some were six years old.
N: A photographer caught a German shepherd going at a fifteen year old boy. It ran on the front page of the New York Times and in papers around the world.
N: Kennedy told his advisers the picture made him sick. Within weeks he sent a civil rights bill to Congress.

## ASIDE
N: And that was the plan. Not the brutality, but the photograph of it.
N: The method was to go where the violence was certain, remain nonviolent in front of a camera, and let the country look at what its own laws were built on. Saying so out loud is not a criticism of the movement. It is what the movement said it was doing.

## SCENE 4 | Eighteen days
N: The twenty eighth of August, nineteen sixty three. A quarter of a million people at the Lincoln Memorial, and seventy million watching at home, which was the largest television audience the country had ever produced.

## DOCUMENT | Martin Luther King Jr. | Speech at the March on Washington | August 28, 1963
N: Martin Luther King spoke last, and the passage everybody knows was not in the prepared text.
M: I have a dream that my four little children will one day live in a nation where they will not be judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character.
N: Eighteen days later a bomb went off in the basement stairwell of a church in Birmingham on a Sunday morning.
N: Addie Mae Collins, fourteen. Cynthia Wesley, fourteen. Carole Robertson, fourteen. Carol Denise McNair, eleven.

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of fourteen years.

## PERSPECTIVE | The movement | victor
N: The first is the movement's own, and its claim is that this was a discipline, not a mood.
N: People were trained. There were workshops on how to be beaten without striking back, rehearsals of what to do when the dogs came, and a selection process for who would be arrested that weighed who had children and who could make bail.
N: Behind every famous day there is that kind of organisation, and a great deal of it was done by women who were not invited onto the platform. The leaflets in Montgomery, the drivers, the logistics.
N: And the case it makes for itself is that it worked. Three federal laws in four years took apart legal segregation in twenty one states, and Black voter registration in Mississippi went from under seven per cent to nearly sixty in three years.
N: Martin Luther King put the argument for staying with it in one sentence, on the road out of Selma in nineteen sixty five.
M: We shall overcome because the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice.

## PERSPECTIVE | Black Power | revisionist
N: The second account says the first one is describing a ceiling.
N: Three months after the March on Washington, Malcolm X stood up in Detroit and called it the farce on Washington. His argument was procedural and hard to answer. The route, the speakers and the tone had all been cleared with the administration in advance. The chairman of the student wing had his speech censored on the day. The line taken out of it asked which side the federal government was on.
N: The deeper objection is about who is asked to carry the cost. A strategy that requires the victim to absorb the blow on camera is a strategy that needs the violence to continue in order to work.
N: And the sharpest version of it came from inside the movement, not from outside. Fannie Lou Hamer had been beaten in a county jail in Mississippi for trying to register people to vote. In nineteen sixty four she testified on national television against her own party's refusal to seat her delegation.
F: We are tired of being beaten by policemen. We are tired of seeing our people locked up in jail over and over again.
N: The President of the United States called a press conference at the same hour specifically to get her off the air.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | White southern resistance | vanquished
N: The third account is the one that lost, and the thing to notice about it is who was in it.
N: The manifesto against Brown was signed by a hundred and one members of Congress. Only three southern senators declined to sign. This was not the fringe. It was the establishment position of an entire region, argued in the language of constitutional principle, local control and states' rights.
N: That language is worth taking seriously, because it is the language the argument is still conducted in.
N: And it is worth putting next to the result. In one Virginia county the principle of local control produced five years in which the local authority provided no education whatsoever to Black children, and paid white families to go elsewhere.

## PERSPECTIVE | The federal government | bystander
N: The fourth account is Washington's, and it is mostly a record of doing the minimum under pressure.
N: Eisenhower privately told the Chief Justice that white southerners were not bad people, they were simply concerned about their little girls having to sit beside big overgrown Negroes. When he sent troops to Little Rock, his own attorney general later confirmed it was to enforce a court's authority, not to integrate a school.
N: Kennedy telephoned Coretta Scott King when her husband was in jail in nineteen sixty, a gesture credited with moving a quarter of a million Black votes, and then appointed segregationist judges to southern federal courts.
N: The pattern holds across administrations. The federal government moved when not moving had become more expensive than moving, and what made it expensive was photographs.

## PERSPECTIVE | The global view | academic
N: The fifth account puts the whole thing on a bigger map.
N: In nineteen fifty five, delegates from twenty nine Asian and African countries met at Bandung in Indonesia and condemned racial discrimination as a means of cultural suppression. Between nineteen fifty six and nineteen sixty eight, forty African countries became independent.
N: The connections were direct, not poetic. King went to India in nineteen fifty nine to study how the noncooperation campaigns had actually been run. The chief organiser of the March on Washington had been to India in forty eight and to Ghana for its independence in fifty seven.
N: On this reading the sit-in and the independence movement are the same decade of the same argument, conducted by people who were writing to each other.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each one has to leave out.
N: The movement's account gets compressed into one man and about six days, and the people who actually built it, most of them women, most of them local, end up as logistics in someone else's story.
N: The Black Power critique is right that the laws stopped at the edge of the South and never touched a northern mortgage. It also never produced an alternative that put a statute on the books.
N: The southern account calls itself lawful and local, and it is the only one of the five that has to explain a county with no schools.
N: The federal account presents patience as prudence. Its own records show it moving on the arithmetic of votes and pictures, which is not the same as being persuaded.
N: And the global account is the widest and the least useful at close range. A declaration at Bandung did not desegregate a lunch counter in Greensboro. Four students sitting down did that, and within two months there were sit-ins in fifty four cities.

## CLOSE
N: Three acts of Congress, in sixty four, sixty five and sixty eight, ended legal segregation in twenty one states.
N: What they did not touch is still measurable, in who owns a house, who holds wealth, and who is in prison, and the movement said so at the time.
N: And the fifteen year old.
N: Nine months before Rosa Parks, Claudette Colvin refused to move on the same bus line in the same city, and was dragged off and arrested for it. The organisation looked at her case, and at her, and decided to wait for a better one.
