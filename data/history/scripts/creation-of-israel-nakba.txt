## OPEN
N: At four in the afternoon on the fourteenth of May, nineteen forty eight, a man read a declaration aloud in a museum in Tel Aviv. Eleven minutes later the United States recognised the state it created.
N: It is usually told from there. Two peoples, one piece of ground, an ancient claim on each side.
N: The papers from that year are full of a third thing. An empire that had promised the same territory to two peoples in one sentence and was leaving on a deadline. And a king whose army invaded the next morning, who had already agreed in private, twelve days before the United Nations voted, to divide the country with the people he was about to fight.

## TITLE
N: The creation of Israel and the Nakba. Nineteen forty seven to nineteen forty nine.

## SCENE 1 | Fifty six per cent
N: The twenty ninth of November, nineteen forty seven. The United Nations General Assembly votes to partition Mandatory Palestine.
N: The arithmetic is the argument, so here it is.
N: The Jewish state gets fifty six per cent of the land, including the Negev desert, and inside it live four hundred and ninety eight thousand Jews and four hundred and seven thousand Arabs.
N: The Arab state gets forty three per cent, with seven hundred and twenty five thousand Arabs and ten thousand Jews. Jerusalem and Bethlehem are to be international.
N: The Jewish Agency accepts. The Arab Higher Committee rejects it. The Arab League says it will resist by force.

## ASIDE
N: Look at the first of those two populations again. Four hundred and ninety eight thousand and four hundred and seven thousand.
N: A state that is forty five per cent the other people is not a solution on paper. It is a question about what happens next, and both sides understood that in nineteen forty seven.

## SCENE 2 | Before any army crossed a border
N: The war did not begin in May. It began the week after the vote, between communities that had been living in the same towns.
N: By March the Jewish paramilitaries were running a coordinated plan to hold the territory allotted to them and the roads between their population centres. Arab irregulars were attacking settlements and cutting the convoys to Jerusalem.
N: On the ninth of April, fighters from two of the smaller Jewish paramilitaries attacked the village of Deir Yassin, outside Jerusalem, and killed between a hundred and seven and two hundred and fifty four civilians. The number is still disputed. The effect is not. News of it moved across Palestine faster than any army and people began leaving villages that had not been attacked.
N: Four days later an Arab force ambushed a medical convoy on the road to Mount Scopus and killed seventy eight doctors, nurses and patients.
N: This is five months before the invasion, and the violence is already reciprocal and already aimed at civilians.

## SCENE 3 | Eleven minutes
N: The British mandate was due to expire at midnight on the fourteenth of May, and Britain had announced it was going regardless of what it left behind.

## DOCUMENT | David Ben-Gurion | Israeli Declaration of Independence | May 14, 1948
N: That afternoon David Ben-Gurion read the declaration in Tel Aviv.
M: We hereby declare the establishment of a Jewish state in Eretz-Israel, to be known as the State of Israel.
N: Washington recognised it eleven minutes later. Moscow followed three days after that.
N: The next morning the armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon crossed the borders.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | What the fighting settled
N: The armistice agreements were signed in nineteen forty nine, and the map they froze was not the map the United Nations had drawn.
N: Israel held seventy eight per cent of Mandatory Palestine, not fifty six. Transjordan held the West Bank and the old city of Jerusalem. Egypt held Gaza.
N: No Arab state was established in Palestine. The territory allotted to it was taken by Israel and by two Arab governments.
N: Between seven hundred thousand and seven hundred and fifty thousand Palestinians had left. Some fled the fighting. Some were expelled at gunpoint, which is documented at Lydda and Ramle and in villages in the Galilee. Some left expecting to come back within weeks.
N: Four hundred and eighteen villages were emptied. Two hundred and ninety three of them were destroyed afterwards. Buildings demolished, orchards uprooted, cemeteries bulldozed.
N: None of the refugees were permitted to return.

## DOCUMENT | Count Folke Bernadotte | Report to the United Nations | September 16, 1948
N: The United Nations mediator was a Swedish diplomat named Count Folke Bernadotte, who had spent the war negotiating prisoners out of German camps. This is from his report.
M: The most tragic aspect of the Palestine refugee problem is that the refugees themselves were the victims of a conflict which was not of their making.
N: He was shot dead in Jerusalem the following day by members of one of the Jewish paramilitaries.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts, and on this subject more than any other in this catalogue, each one is used as a weapon against the others. They are set out here in their own terms first.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Israeli account | victor
N: The first account begins in Europe, not in Palestine.
N: Its premise is that the Jewish position as a minority in other peoples' countries had been tested to destruction. Theodor Herzl argued in the eighteen nineties that European antisemitism was not a phase and could not be reformed away. Fifty years later, a third of the Jews in the world had been murdered.
N: On that reading, sovereignty is not a preference. It is the only arrangement under which the argument stops being about the goodwill of a host.
N: The practical case is the record between the wars. Land bought, immigration under British permit, and institutions built before there was a state: a governing agency, a labour federation, a university.
N: And the decisive fact of nineteen forty eight, from inside it, is that partition was accepted and then five armies crossed the border.
N: Chaim Weizmann had put the harder version of the argument a decade earlier, when Britain was proposing to cap Jewish immigration.
M: It would be a crime against humanity to reduce the number of Jews so that the Arabs may enjoy greater ease.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Palestinians | vanquished
N: The second account begins with a sentence written in London in nineteen seventeen.
N: At the time the Balfour Declaration promised a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine, about nine in ten people living there were Arabs, and none of them were consulted. Balfour said so himself two years later, in a memorandum, writing that in Palestine the British did not propose even to go through the form of consulting the wishes of the present inhabitants.
N: One government promising a second people the land of a third is where this account starts, and everything after is read through it.
N: The case put to the United Nations in nineteen forty seven was made by Jamal Husseini.
M: The case of the Arabs of Palestine was based on the principles of international justice. It was that of a people which desired to live in undisturbed possession of the country where Providence and history had placed them.
N: And the core of the account is not the war. It is what followed the war. Four hundred and eighteen villages emptied, most of them then demolished, and a permanent refusal of return that was a decision taken afterwards, in peacetime, by a government in control of the territory.
N: Their descendants now number about five point nine million registered refugees.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The British mandate | bystander
N: The third account is the one that handed the problem over, and its own documents are the evidence against it.
N: The Balfour Declaration contains two promises in a single sentence. A national home for the Jewish people, and nothing that prejudices the civil and religious rights of the existing non Jewish communities. Britain spent twenty eight years discovering that those cannot both be honoured.
N: The record is a sequence of commissions and white papers that each tilted one way and were withdrawn when the other side pushed back. A nineteen thirty paper proposed limiting Jewish immigration and land purchase and was abandoned under political pressure within months. A nineteen thirty seven commission proposed partition for the first time and went nowhere.
N: Then in nineteen forty seven Britain announced a date, handed the file to the United Nations, and left. The mandate's last act was to stop administering a conflict it had built the terms of.

## PERSPECTIVE | The new historians | revisionist
N: The fourth account came out of a filing cabinet.
N: In the late nineteen eighties, Israeli state and military archives from nineteen forty eight began to be declassified, and a group of Israeli scholars went through them.
N: What they found did not confirm either national story. Benny Morris's study of the refugee question documented several different causes operating at once: people fleeing an active war, people expelled at gunpoint by Israeli units, deliberate psychological pressure including the use of Deir Yassin's reputation as a warning, and the collapse of Palestinian civil administration.
N: His conclusion was that the refugee problem was born of war rather than of a plan, which satisfied nobody. Israelis objected to the documented expulsions. Palestinians objected to the absence of a master design.
N: And the scholarship kept moving after that. Some of the same historians later reached political conclusions their own earlier work is used to argue against, which is itself worth knowing when the findings are quoted.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Arab states | bystander
N: The fifth account is the one least often told, and it is documented mostly in private papers.
N: On the seventeenth of November nineteen forty seven, twelve days before the United Nations vote, the king of Transjordan met a senior figure from the Jewish Agency in secret and reached an understanding. Transjordan would take the Arab areas of Palestine. The Jewish Agency would not contest it.
N: He did not send his army in May to create a Palestinian state. He sent it to annex one, and it was commanded in the field by a British officer.
N: Egypt's decision was about Transjordan, not about Palestine. If the Hashemites took Arab Palestine, Cairo's standing in the Arab world shrank, so the Egyptian army went into the Negev and Gaza to stop that happening.
N: The five armies barely coordinated. Their public statements were a single position. Their instructions were five different ones.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what each of them needs to leave out.
N: The Israeli account is a true description of why a state was necessary and it stops at the border of what the state then did. Five armies invaded, and four hundred and eighteen villages were emptied, and the refusal to let anyone return was a policy made after the shooting stopped.
N: The Palestinian account is a true description of a dispossession and it is quietest about the choice its own leadership made in nineteen forty seven, and about the Arab governments whose armies arrived with their own maps in their pockets.
N: The British account presents twenty eight years of failure as an impossible balancing act, which lets an administration describe as tragedy a contradiction it wrote itself and enforced with troops.
N: The new historians have the strongest evidence and the weakest protection against being used. A finding about intent is not a finding about outcome, and both sides quote the half of the archive they need.
N: And the account of the Arab governments is the most cynical and among the best sourced, and it explains the armies rather than the people. Pointed the wrong way it becomes an argument that Palestinians were only ever betrayed by other Arabs, which is a way of saying nothing was done to them at all.

## CLOSE
N: There have been five major wars since, and the thing the nineteen forty nine armistice lines were supposed to be temporary about is now older than almost everyone living inside them.
N: Between seven hundred thousand and seven hundred and fifty thousand Palestinians were displaced and not allowed back.
N: Over the following quarter century, around eight hundred and fifty thousand Jews left or were driven out of Arab countries, most of them to Israel. Both of those numbers belong to this history, and neither one cancels the other.
N: The mediator who wrote that the refugees were victims of a conflict not of their making had that report on the record for one day.
