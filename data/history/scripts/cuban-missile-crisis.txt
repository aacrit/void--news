## OPEN
N: The story as it is usually told happens in two rooms. A cabinet room in Washington and an office in Moscow, thirteen days, two men, and a blink.
N: The moment that came closest to ending it was neither of those rooms. It was two hundred feet under the Sargasso Sea, in a submarine that had been out of contact with Moscow for days, and it turned on one officer refusing.
N: And the thing that makes the whole crisis look different is a number that Washington did not learn for forty years. There were already a hundred and sixty two nuclear warheads on the island.

## TITLE
N: The Cuban missile crisis. October, nineteen sixty two.

## SCENE 1 | Thirteen minutes
N: The fourteenth of October. A U-2 flies over western Cuba and photographs construction near San Cristobal.
N: The photographs show launch sites for Soviet medium range missiles. From there, a warhead reaches Washington in thirteen minutes.
N: The President is briefed before breakfast on the sixteenth. By midday fourteen men are sitting in the Cabinet Room with one instruction: find a way to get them out.
N: One of the first voices in the room argues for airstrikes immediately. The President's brother passes him a note saying he now understands how the Japanese commander felt planning Pearl Harbor.

## SCENE 2 | The word
N: They argue for six days before the public is told anything.
N: The Joint Chiefs are unanimous. Strike the sites from the air, then invade. Their chairman tells the President that anything less is appeasement, and uses Munich as the comparison, to a man whose father had been the ambassador in London during Munich.
N: Kennedy takes a middle path and a careful word. Not a blockade, because a blockade is an act of war in international law. A quarantine.
N: His own defence secretary makes the argument that the missiles change nothing strategically, because the Soviet Union can already reach American cities from home. What they change is the appearance of it, ninety miles offshore, and appearance is what the crisis is about.

## SCENE 3 | Black Saturday
N: On the twenty fourth the Soviet ships approaching the line stop or turn back, and that is the moment everyone remembers.
N: The problem is that the missiles already ashore keep going up. By the twenty sixth, American intelligence believes two dozen launchers are operational.
N: The twenty seventh is the day everything happens at once.
N: A U-2 is shot down over Cuba by a Soviet surface to air missile. The pilot, Rudolf Anderson, is killed. Under the rules the Americans set themselves, that is the trigger for striking back.
N: At the same time, in the Sargasso Sea, American destroyers are dropping practice depth charges on a Soviet submarine to force it up. The boat is B fifty nine. It has been submerged for days, the air is foul, the temperature inside is over fifty degrees, and it has heard nothing from Moscow.
N: It is carrying a torpedo with a nuclear warhead. The captain concludes that war has started above him and orders it armed.
N: Soviet regulations on that boat require three officers to agree. Two do. The third, the flotilla commander, refuses, and argues that surfacing and being humiliated is better than starting a nuclear war.
N: His name was Vasili Arkhipov. The submarine surfaced.

## ASIDE
N: Nobody in Washington knew that had happened. Nobody in Washington knew it for another forty years.
N: It came out at a conference in Havana, in two thousand and two, when the men who had been on that boat were in a room with the men who had been in that cabinet.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | The part that was not announced
N: That same evening the President's brother meets the Soviet ambassador privately at the Justice Department, and gives him two things.
N: A deadline. Remove the missiles or they will be removed by force.
N: And an offer. American Jupiter missiles will come out of Turkey within six months. But if the Soviet Union ever says that this was part of the bargain, the deal is off and the United States will deny it.
N: On the twenty eighth, Radio Moscow announces the missiles are going home. The public version of events is that the Soviet Union backed down and the United States conceded nothing.
N: The Jupiters came out of Turkey in April.

## DOCUMENT | John F. Kennedy | Remark to aide Kenny O'Donnell | October 27, 1962
N: John Kennedy said this to an aide on the worst day of it.
M: It is insane and very sad that two men sitting on opposite sides of the world should be able to decide to bring an end to civilization.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts, and the one that is almost always missing is the one whose territory this was.

## PERSPECTIVE | The American room | victor
N: The first account is the one that was written first and taught longest, and its subject is decision making under pressure.
N: A young president resists his own generals. He invents an option nobody had offered him. He holds his nerve for six days and then for six more, and the other side gives way.
N: The line that fixed it in the public mind came from the Secretary of State, Dean Rusk, on the twenty fourth, watching the Soviet ships stop.
M: We're eyeball to eyeball, and I think the other fellow just blinked.
N: It is a good line, it was said, and it describes about one day of a thirteen day crisis.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Soviet calculation | bystander
N: The second account starts from a map.
N: American Jupiter missiles were already in Turkey, fifteen minutes from Moscow. Khrushchev's question, asked out loud to his defence minister on a beach on the Black Sea, was why the Americans should have that right and the Soviet Union should not.
N: What followed was called Operation Anadyr, and it was enormous. Forty two thousand troops, three dozen medium range missiles, bombers, fighters, and those tactical warheads, moved across the Atlantic disguised as agricultural shipments.
N: Soldiers were issued winter clothing and told they were going somewhere cold. They unpacked it in Havana.
N: The whole plan depended on the missiles being operational before anyone noticed. He missed by about ten days.
M: I will tell you one thing. Nuclear war is out of the question. We're not lunatics.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The Cubans | vanquished
N: The third account belongs to the country the missiles were standing in, and it begins eighteen months earlier.
N: In April nineteen sixty one, fourteen hundred CIA trained exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs. The invasion collapsed in three days, and it confirmed for Castro the thing he had been saying since nineteen fifty nine.
N: What followed confirmed it further. Operation Mongoose ran dozens of plans for sabotage, economic strangulation and assassination, signed off at the top of the American government.
N: So accepting Soviet missiles was, from Havana, not adventurism. It was a small country taking the only deterrent on offer.
M: If they want this kind of confrontation, well, OK. We are ready.
N: And then the deal was made without them. Castro learned that the missiles were leaving from a radio broadcast. The man whose soldiers had spent two weeks digging trenches along the northern coast, whose people would have died first, was told by Radio Moscow at the same time as everybody else.
N: The crowds in Havana chanted at Khrushchev that what you give, you cannot take away.

## PERSPECTIVE | The deterrence theorists | academic
N: The fourth account turned the crisis into a discipline.
N: The influential reading is that deterrence did not hold because the two leaders were rational. It held because both of them became frightened of how little either of them controlled.
N: The useful phrase is the threat that leaves something to chance. Neither man could guarantee what his own forces would do next, and it was that, rather than any calculation, that moved them both.
N: Which points at the problem the theorists call always and never. A nuclear weapon has to be always available, or the threat is empty, and never usable by accident, or the threat kills you. On the twenty seventh both of those nearly failed inside the same afternoon.
N: One aircraft shot down by a local commander. One torpedo armed by a submarine captain who could not ask anyone.

## PERSPECTIVE | The declassified record | revisionist
N: The fifth account is what happened when the files opened, and it is the reason the other four had to be rewritten.
N: Kennedy had secretly recorded the Cabinet Room. When those tapes were released, the president on them was not the cool figure of the official story. He is uncertain. He doubles back. He pushes against the generals out of fear of where their plan ends, not out of serene judgement.
N: The committee is less deliberative too. Men repeat themselves, talk past each other, and work from intelligence that is wrong.
N: Soviet archives added the rest. The tactical warheads already on the island, which nobody in Washington had counted. The submarine. And the Turkish trade, which had been kept secret so successfully that for a generation the lesson taught from this crisis was that firmness works and concession is unnecessary.
N: The record says a concession was made. It was simply not announced.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And what each of them is leaving out.
N: The American account leaves out the deal. For thirty years it was a story about nerve, told by people who knew that a trade had been made and had agreed never to say so.
N: The Soviet account presents the deployment as symmetry, which it was, and skips how much of it was a gamble on not being seen, and what it meant to move that many warheads onto an island whose government did not have a say over their use.
N: The Cuban account is the most morally clear and the least in control. It is an argument about sovereignty made by the one party that was not at the table when the thing was settled.
N: The deterrence account is a theory that survives by absorbing its own near misses. A framework that has to count Arkhipov as evidence of stability is doing a lot of work.
N: And the declassified account has the advantage every later reader has. It knows how it came out. Nobody in those rooms did.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: The direct line between Washington and Moscow was installed the following summer, because the crisis had been conducted partly through public broadcasts and partly through a journalist.
N: A test ban treaty followed in nineteen sixty three, and a non proliferation treaty five years later.
N: Vasili Arkhipov went back to the Soviet navy, was promoted, retired as a vice admiral, and died in nineteen ninety eight, before almost anybody outside his own service knew what he had done.
N: He was not being brave. By his own account he was following the rules, which happened to require three signatures, and he did not give his.
