## OPEN
N: A clay cylinder sits in a case in the British Museum with a label calling it the first charter of human rights.
N: It is forty five lines of cuneiform, in a dead Mesopotamian language, and it does not contain the word right, or freedom, or law.
N: What it actually says, in the plainest reading, is that a god got angry at one king and chose another. It is a conquest text.
N: The charter claim was made two thousand five hundred years after the clay was fired, and it has outlasted three governments that needed it to be true.

## TITLE
N: The Cyrus Cylinder. Five hundred and thirty nine before the common era.

## SCENE 1 | Babylon, the twelfth of October
N: The Persian army enters Babylon without a fight. Cyrus's general Ugbaru leads the troops in, and Nabonidus, Babylon's own king, has already fled the city.
N: Seventeen days later Cyrus enters in person. Green branches are spread before him, and the priests of Marduk, Babylon's chief god, receive the invader as a guest.

## DOCUMENT | Nabonidus Chronicle | Babylonian chronicle tablet, BM 35382 | c. 539 BCE
N: This is the Nabonidus Chronicle, a record keeping tablet from the very administration Cyrus had just replaced.
M: Without fighting, he entered Babylon. The state of peace was imposed upon the city.

## ASIDE
N: Read that again. The deposed regime's own chronicle calls it peace, not conquest.
N: Nobody keeping records in Babylon that year describes a battle, because for the people who kept the records, there wasn't one.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | The Esagila foundations, 1879
N: Hormuzd Rassam, an Assyrian born archaeologist working for the British Museum, digs into the foundations of the Esagila, Marduk's own temple, in March eighteen seventy nine.
N: He finds a baked clay barrel, twenty two and a half centimeters long, inscribed with forty five lines of Akkadian cuneiform. The practice was standard: Babylonian kings had been burying building inscriptions in temple foundations for centuries.
N: It ships to London. Two fragments of the same text surface later at Yale, and are not joined to the main cylinder until two thousand and ten.
N: The cylinder itself was almost certainly written within months of the conquest, while the arrangement with Marduk's priests was still fresh.

## DOCUMENT | The Cyrus Cylinder | Akkadian inscription, BM 90920 | c. 539 BCE
N: This is the cylinder itself, opening the way Babylonian royal inscriptions had opened for generations, in Cyrus's own voice, in Akkadian, for a Babylonian priesthood, not a Persian one.
M: I am Cyrus, king of the world, great king, mighty king, king of Babylon, king of Sumer and Akkad, king of the four quarters of the world.

## ASIDE
N: Akkadian, not Persian. Addressed to Marduk's priests, in the theology they already used for their own kings.
N: The cylinder goes on to say Marduk searched every country and chose Cyrus. That is a Babylonian priest's explanation, applied to a foreign conqueror.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | What the stone claims
N: In Irving Finkel's translation for the British Museum, the cylinder says Cyrus returned displaced gods to their home cities and let deported peoples go home.
N: It does not name a people. The words Israel, Judah and Hebrew appear nowhere on the forty five lines.
N: The exiles it never names numbered an estimated forty thousand to fifty thousand people, out of a Neo Babylonian empire of one and a half to two million.
N: The Hebrew Bible tells a related story about the same king, composed centuries after the events it describes.

## DOCUMENT | Biblical text | Ezra 1:1-3, Hebrew Bible | c. 5th-4th century BCE
N: This is the biblical text, the book of Ezra.
M: In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, the Lord stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia, so that he made a proclamation throughout all his kingdom. Who is there among you of all his people? Let him go up to Jerusalem.

## ASIDE
N: Two texts, one king, and no line connecting them. The cylinder never confirms the decree Ezra describes, and Ezra never mentions a cylinder.
N: Both describe the same general policy. Neither proves the other.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | New York, 1971
N: The government of the Shah of Iran sends a copy of the cylinder to the United Nations, alongside a new translation it commissioned for the occasion.
N: The introduction the United Nations printed with that translation makes the claim outright, in the UN's own words.

## DOCUMENT | United Nations | Introductory text, 1971 translation | 1971
N: This is the United Nations, in its own printed introduction.
M: This cylinder has historically been considered the world's first charter of human rights.

## ASIDE
N: The stone had been sitting in London for ninety two years by then. The claim was new. The clay was not.

## REST | short

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five ways of reading forty five lines of cuneiform, and they cannot agree on what kind of document it even is.

## PERSPECTIVE | Persian Imperial Achaemenid case | victor
N: The first account is the empire's own, and it says the cylinder describes real policy, not decoration.
N: By five hundred and thirty nine before the common era, Cyrus had already absorbed the Median Empire, Lydia, and the Greek cities of Ionia. Babylon was the last major power left in western Asia.
N: Darius, his successor, governed twenty three subject peoples through local satraps and left their gods alone. On this reading, the cylinder is the first statement of that approach: accommodation, not forced conversion.
N: The cylinder's own boast supports it.
M: All the kings who sit upon thrones, from the upper to the lower sea, they all brought their heavy tribute before me, and in Babylon they kissed my feet.
N: Tribute, not conversion. Nobody paying it was made to abandon a god.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Assyriological revision | academic
N: The second account comes from the people who read the cuneiform for a living, and it says the first account mistakes a genre for an invention.
N: Marduk-apla-iddina, a Babylonian king two centuries before Cyrus, made the identical claims: restored cult statues, released oppressed peoples, credited the god who chose him. The formula already existed.
N: Amelie Kuhrt's nineteen eighty three article on the cylinder argued that the Marduk priesthood, not Cyrus, likely composed it, to justify their own collaboration with the man who had just replaced their king.
N: Kuhrt put it plainly.
F: The cylinder is not a declaration of human rights. It is a traditional Mesopotamian royal building inscription.
N: Matt Waters, in scholarship paraphrased here, makes the harder version of the same point: the text explains how a priesthood justified a regime change, and nothing about human rights.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Iranian nationalist case | revisionist
N: The third account says the correction is beside the point, because the cylinder's meaning to Iranians was never really about the cuneiform.
N: In October nineteen seventy one the Shah staged celebrations at Persepolis: sixty nine heads of state, a tent city with fifty air conditioned pavilions, an estimated two hundred million dollars spent while rural poverty in Iran ran above forty percent.
N: He addressed Cyrus directly, at the tomb.
M: Sleep in peace, for we are awake.
N: The Islamic Republic that overthrew him rejected almost everything the Shah stood for, and kept the cylinder anyway. In two thousand and ten, Mahmoud Ahmadinejad called it a source of pride for all Iranians.
N: For the diaspora, the cylinder argues that Iranian identity predates the Islamic Republic, predates the revolution, and belongs to nobody's government.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Babylonian population | vanquished
N: The fourth account belongs to the people the cylinder was actually addressed to, and it was not all of Babylon.
N: It was addressed to the priesthood of the Esagila, who controlled its land and its offerings, and who had specific grievances against Nabonidus. He had promoted the moon god Sin above Marduk, moved provincial cult statues into Babylon, and spent years at the Arabian oasis of Tayma instead of performing the New Year festival the priesthood needed him to perform.
N: A Babylonian poem composed after the conquest, likely by that same priesthood, states their case even harder.
M: He did not know the rites, he ruined the regular offerings. He did evil against Marduk's city.
N: For ordinary Babylonians, farmers and traders rather than priests, the record shows continuity: land sales and loan contracts kept being written the same way, with a new king's name at the top.
N: Continuity is not consent. Within two generations, under Xerxes, Babylonian revolts were crushed twice, and part of the Esagila itself was damaged.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Museum repatriation case | revisionist
N: The fifth account is about where the object sits now, not what it says.
N: Hormuzd Rassam excavated it in Ottoman controlled territory under a British Museum permit, and it went to London without anyone from Babylon, Baghdad, or Tehran being consulted.
N: Rassam was born in Mosul in eighteen twenty six to a Chaldean Catholic family, and had already excavated at Nimrud, Nineveh, and Sippar for the museum before he reached Babylon.
N: When Wallis Budge, the museum's Keeper of Egyptian and Assyrian Antiquities, published translations of tablets Rassam had excavated, he put his own name on them. Rassam sued for defamation in eighteen ninety three, and lost.
N: The British Museum's stated position, repeated in a two thousand and two declaration signed by eighteen institutions, is that such objects belong in collections that can preserve and study them for everyone.
M: Objects are safest in institutions that have the resources and expertise to preserve them.
N: Iran has asked for the cylinder back at least three times since nineteen seventy nine. Iraq, whose claim is arguably stronger, since the object is Babylonian rather than Persian in both language and genre, has not formally asked at all.
N: The museum has loaned the cylinder to Tehran twice, in nineteen seventy one and two thousand ten, and taken it back to London both times.

## REST | short

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And what none of them will say outright.
N: The Persian imperial account leaves out the military campaigns that preceded Babylon: the destruction of Sardis, the subjugation of Ionia, missing entirely from a text about a divinely gentle king.
N: The Assyriological account is correct about the genre, and that correctness lets it treat the deported peoples going home as a footnote to propaganda rather than a fact about their lives.
N: The Iranian nationalist account spent two hundred million dollars celebrating a text written in Akkadian, for a Babylonian audience, in the same year Iran's own secret police was arresting people for saying the wrong thing.
N: The Babylonian account can describe the priesthood's motives in exact detail, and it has nothing to say about whether Achaemenid rule was better, on balance, for the scribes and astronomers who kept working under it.
N: And the repatriation account has no answer for the argument it records itself: that handing the object to Iran would make a Mesopotamian object, made in Iraq, into the property of one nation's ideology rather than a shared inheritance.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: The cylinder sits today in room fifty two of the British Museum, catalogued as BM ninety thousand nine hundred and twenty.
N: It toured five American museums in two thousand thirteen and drew two million visitors, most of whom could not read a single line of it.
N: Cyrus died nine years after taking Babylon, in five hundred and thirty before the common era, fighting the Massagetae, a people on his eastern frontier who refused to be governed.
N: The clay is still in one piece. You can stand in front of the case and read all forty five lines yourself, in whichever translation the museum has printed that year, and decide.
