## OPEN
N: Nobody decided to open it.
N: There was no vote, no order, and no moment where a government concluded that the time had come. What there was, on the evening of the ninth of November nineteen eighty nine, was a spokesman reading from a note card about a policy he had not been briefed on, and then a middle ranking officer at a gate deciding not to start shooting.
N: The Cold War's most photographed night was an administrative accident and one man's refusal.

## TITLE
N: The fall of the Berlin Wall. The ninth of November, nineteen eighty nine.

## SCENE 1 | Six fifty three
N: An East German press conference, routine, winding down. The Politburo member running it, Gunter Schabowski, has been handed new travel regulations to announce and has not read them properly.
N: An Italian journalist asks the obvious question. When does this take effect?
N: Schabowski shuffles through his papers. There is nothing in them about timing, because the plan was for the rules to start the next day, with orderly processing at the checkpoints, and nobody told him.

## DOCUMENT | Gunter Schabowski | Press conference, East Berlin | November 9, 1989
N: So Gunter Schabowski improvised, on live television, and this is the sentence.
M: As far as I know, it takes effect immediately, without delay.

## SCENE 2 | Half past eleven
N: It is on West German television within the hour, which East Berliners watch, and by eight o'clock there are thousands of people at the Bornholmer Strasse crossing asking to be let through.
N: The senior officer on duty is a lieutenant colonel named Harald Jager. He telephones up the chain and gets no instruction anybody will put their name to.
N: His options are a crowd that is growing every minute, and a standing order about what to do with people trying to cross.
N: At half past eleven he lifts the barrier.
N: Within a few hours every crossing point in the city is open. People are on top of the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate. West Berliners are handing champagne across.

## DOCUMENT | Harald Jager | Interview | 2009
N: Twenty years later Harald Jager described his own night this way.
M: I was the one who opened the Berlin Wall. On November 9, 1989. The most beautiful day of my life.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | What he was standing in
N: The thing he opened had gone up overnight, twenty eight years earlier, when work crews laid barbed wire and blocks across ninety six miles of city.
N: By the late sixties it was not a wall. It was a system. Two concrete walls with a strip between them, raked smooth so that footprints would show. Three hundred and two watchtowers. Anti vehicle trenches. In places, automatic firearms triggered by trip wires. And an order to shoot.
N: The memorial that counts them documents at least a hundred and forty people killed trying to cross.
N: The first was shot eleven days after the wire went up.
N: The last was Chris Gueffroy, shot in February nineteen eighty nine, nine months before the gates opened.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | The year it was already losing
N: None of this began on the ninth of November.
N: In June, Poland held elections that were only partly free, and the opposition took ninety nine of the hundred seats it was allowed to contest.
N: In September, Hungary opened its border with Austria, which gave East Germans a way out through a third country. Thirteen thousand went in three days.
N: In Leipzig, the Monday demonstrations went from a thousand people at the start of September to three hundred thousand by the end of October.
N: The East German leader was forced out in the middle of that and replaced by a man who lasted forty four days.

## DOCUMENT | Mikhail Gorbachev | Remark in East Berlin | October 7, 1989
N: A month before it fell, Mikhail Gorbachev came to East Berlin for the state's fortieth anniversary and said something to his hosts that was immediately understood as a warning.
M: He who arrives too late is punished by life.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of one night and the year around it.

## PERSPECTIVE | The West German account | victor
N: The first is the one that became the official memory of the twentieth century's end, and its claim is that a forty year strategy worked.
N: On that reading the West did not win by force. It waited, contained, and let a system fail under its own arithmetic, exactly as the strategy had predicted at the beginning.
N: The public face of it is Ronald Reagan at the Brandenburg Gate two years earlier, delivering a line his own State Department and National Security Council had tried to cut from the draft, and which he kept in.
M: Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!
N: And then the speed. Nineteen days after the gates opened, the West German Chancellor put a ten point plan for reunification to parliament without consulting his coalition partners, or France, or Britain, or Washington. The British and French leaders were alarmed, and that is the polite version.

## PERSPECTIVE | The East German dissidents | vanquished
N: The second account belongs to the people who had been risking arrest for months before any of this was safe, and they did not get what they were asking for.
N: The citizens' movement wanted a reformed, democratic East Germany. The pastors opening churches for Monday prayers, the New Forum founders, the artists and writers, were not campaigning to be absorbed by the Federal Republic.
N: Their slogan on the Leipzig marches was Wir sind das Volk. We are the people. It was an assertion against a government that claimed to be the people's.
N: Within weeks of the Wall opening it had changed by one word. Wir sind ein Volk. We are one people.
N: That is the whole argument in one vowel, and the people who had started the marching were not the ones who changed it.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The Soviet view | bystander
N: The third account is Moscow's, and its central fact is a thing that did not happen.
N: There were three hundred and eighty thousand Soviet troops in East Germany that night. In nineteen fifty three, in nineteen fifty six and in nineteen sixty eight, that had been the answer to exactly this situation.
N: They did not move, and the reason is more uncomfortable than restraint. No order came. There was no decision in Moscow not to intervene so much as an absence where the decision used to be.
N: Gorbachev's reforms had been designed to save the system by making it legitimate. He expected the satellite parties to reform themselves and survive. What he had built instead was a machine that could no longer produce the order to shoot.

## PERSPECTIVE | The reunification critics | revisionist
N: The fourth account does not dispute that the Wall was a prison. It disputes what happened next.
N: Legally, East Germany did not merge with West Germany. Its parliament voted in August nineteen ninety to accede to the Federal Republic under an article of the West German constitution designed for territories joining an existing state.
N: East Germans did not negotiate the terms of their own incorporation. Their industry was assessed and largely sold or closed by a West German agency, their qualifications were revalued, their institutions replaced.
N: The bill for reunification has run past two trillion euros, and the gap in wages, wealth and ownership between east and west is still measurable a generation later.

## PERSPECTIVE | The global south | bystander
N: The fifth account puts a different date beside it.
N: Forty one days after the Wall opened, the United States invaded Panama. Somewhere between five hundred and four thousand Panamanians were killed, and the width of that range is itself the point, because nobody counted carefully.
N: In the same weeks that Berliners were being handed champagne on top of a wall, civilians from one Panama City neighbourhood were going into mass graves.
N: Both are events of the winter of nineteen eighty nine. Only one of them is part of what the year is taken to mean.
N: And for much of the world the end of the Cold War was not liberation. It was the end of a bidding war in which small countries had been able to play two superpowers against each other, and the arrival of a single set of terms.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each account has to leave out.
N: The Western account describes a strategy succeeding, and the mechanism on the actual night was a man who had not read his briefing notes and an officer who decided not to fire. Policy gets the credit for an accident.
N: The dissidents' account is right that they were absorbed rather than joined. It also has to sit with the fact that when East Germans voted freely, months later, they voted for the party offering the fastest currency union. The dissidents lost the argument to their own public.
N: The Soviet account takes credit for restraint. An absence of orders is not restraint, and the men at the gate that night were not told to stand down. They were not told anything.
N: The critics' account is accurate about the asymmetry and slides easily into nostalgia for a state that shot people at its own border and kept a file on nearly everyone.
N: And the account from the global south is a comparison, not an explanation. Panama tells you nothing about why the Wall opened. It tells you what the word nineteen eighty nine was allowed to cover.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: The Wall stood for twenty eight years and eighty eight days.
N: Germany was one country again eleven months later. The Soviet Union was gone twenty five months after that.
N: Harald Jager was a career officer in the service that ran the border. He had been on that gate for decades, and the most beautiful day of his life was the day he stopped doing his job.
N: And the last man shot trying to get over it died in February. Nine months before anyone could walk through.
