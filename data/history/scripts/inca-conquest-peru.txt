## TITLE
N: The Inca Empire and the Spanish Conquest. Fourteen thirty eight to fifteen seventy two.

## SCENE 1 | The empire built in a lifetime
N: Fourteen thirty eight. A regional kingdom around the highland city of Cusco gets a new ruler, the ninth Sapa Inca, a man named Pachacuti.
N: In thirty three years he turns it into Tawantinsuyu, the Four Quarters Together. Twelve million people, four thousand kilometers of the Andes, from what is now Colombia to what is now Chile.
N: His engineers lay forty thousand kilometers of road without the wheel, the horse, or one iron tool. Runners called chasqui relay messages along it, two thousand kilometers, in five to seven days.
N: There is no written script. Instead there is the quipu, knotted string read by trained specialists, tracking census and tax debts across the empire.

## DOCUMENT | Pachacuti (attributed) | Inca oral tradition recorded by Juan de Betanzos | c. 1551
N: A line attributed to Pachacuti himself, set down a century later by the chronicler Juan de Betanzos.
M: In the four quarters of the earth, there is no one who resists me. I do great things, moving across the earth.

## ASIDE
N: That is not a boast made after the fact. It is the empire's own idea of itself, at its height, before anyone in it had heard the word Spain.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | The plague that got there first
N: Fifteen twenty seven. Five years before one Spaniard reaches the highlands, smallpox arrives, moving along trade routes ahead of any army.
N: It kills the emperor, Huayna Capac. Within months it kills his chosen heir, a young man named Ninan Cuyochi, too.
N: With no clear successor, two surviving sons go to war. Atahualpa, based in Quito. Huascar, based in Cusco.
N: Atahualpa wins. By the time he does, his army has killed forty thousand of his brother's supporters, and the empire he now holds is a fractured one.

## SCENE 3 | Cajamarca
N: November sixteenth, fifteen thirty two. Atahualpa enters the plaza at Cajamarca with eighty thousand troops behind him. He carries no weapon.
N: One hundred sixty eight Spaniards are hidden in the buildings around that square.
N: A friar named Vicente de Valverde walks out holding a Bible and a cross. Through an interpreter he reads a document composed in Spanish, informing Atahualpa that the pope has granted all of the Americas to Spain, and that he must accept Christian rule or face war.
N: Atahualpa examines the book. He turns the pages. He holds it to his ear.

## DOCUMENT | Multiple Spanish accounts (compiled) | Accounts of the encounter at Cajamarca | 1532
N: Multiple Spanish accounts, compiled afterward, record what Atahualpa says next.
M: It tells me nothing. It does not speak to me.
N: He throws it to the ground.

## ASIDE
N: Valverde shouts to Pizarro. The cannon fires. Cavalry charges from three sides into a crowd that arrived in ceremony, not battle order. In under an hour, six to seven thousand Inca are dead. Not one Spaniard is killed. Atahualpa is pulled from his litter alive.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | The room
N: Pizarro offers a deal. Fill a room six point seven meters long, five point two meters wide, and two point seven meters high with gold. Fill two more rooms with silver. Atahualpa goes free.
N: The Inca comply. Over eight months, llama caravans carry gold from temples across the empire: six thousand kilograms of gold, twelve thousand kilograms of silver.
N: Pizarro melts every piece into bars. Temple vessels, ceremonial ornaments, the golden garden at Coricancha with its gold corn stalks and gold llamas.
N: Then Pizarro charges Atahualpa with treason, idolatry, and polygamy. On July twenty sixth, fifteen thirty three, he executes him by garrote. In Cusco, that date is still a day of mourning.

## REST | short

## SCENE 5 | The siege that did not end it
N: Manco Inca, installed by Pizarro as a puppet ruler in fifteen thirty three, escapes Spanish control three years later and raises an army of one hundred thousand.
N: For ten months his forces besiege Cusco. They hurl red hot stones from slings onto the thatched roofs below. They capture Spanish horses and learn to ride them within weeks.
N: The siege breaks not in battle but because Manco's army has to go home. It is planting season.
N: He retreats to Vilcabamba, deep in the cloud forest. A rump Inca state survives there for thirty six years.

## SCENE 6 | The plaza, again
N: Tupac Amaru the First is the last Sapa Inca. In fifteen seventy two he is captured in the jungle and marched into Cusco in chains.
N: He is executed in the city's main plaza. Fifteen thousand people are forced to watch.

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of one conquest, and the first one insists it was never naive.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Inca Record | vanquished
N: Tawantinsuyu governed twelve million people across four suyus, from a capital the Inca called the navel of the world. Its quipu system tracked census, tribute, and astronomical observation in knotted string. Decades later, a Spanish priest asked a khipukamayuq to demonstrate it, and the man reproduced from his cords a complete confession of sins going back decades.
N: Atahualpa's choice at Cajamarca was not a mistake. He had just won a civil war. He had eighty thousand troops surrounding a plaza holding one hundred sixty eight foreigners. Andean protocol required the superior party to arrive in ceremonial display, not battle order. He was demonstrating power under a grammar Pizarro understood well enough to use against him.
N: Gold, in Inca belief, was the sweat of the sun. Silver was the tears of the moon. Every object melted down at Cajamarca carried a meaning the men weighing it never saw.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Conquistadors' Case | victor
N: Francisco Pizarro never learned to read or write. His cousin and his secretary wrote the accounts that carry his name. Two earlier expeditions down this coast had failed. On the third, in fifteen thirty one, he landed with one hundred sixty eight men, sixty two horses, and four cannon. He was fifty three years old.
N: The document Valverde read at Cajamarca was a real legal instrument, drafted by a royal jurist two decades before. It told native peoples the pope had granted their land to Spain, and that refusal was grounds for war.
M: The horsemen rode among the Indians who were so amazed and terror stricken at the novel sight of horses and the sound of the guns that they thought only of flight to save their lives.
N: That is Francisco de Xerez, one of Pizarro's own men.
N: Atahualpa was tried under Spanish law on charges of treason, idolatry, and conspiracy. Moments before his execution he accepted baptism. He was garroted rather than burned, a mercy reserved for the baptized.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Resistance | indigenous
N: Manco Inca took the throne Pizarro offered him in fifteen thirty three because both his brothers were dead and the Spanish held Cusco. He calculated cooperation would preserve some Inca authority. He was wrong. The Spanish looted his palace and abused his household, and in April fifteen thirty six he escaped and raised one hundred thousand warriors.
N: At Sacsayhuaman, an Inca commander fought until he was the last man on the wall, then threw himself from the tower rather than be taken. Inca riders, perhaps twenty of them, fought mounted on captured horses at Ollantaytambo, the only major battlefield defeat the Spanish suffered in the whole conquest.
N: Manco is recorded telling Spanish friars this, attributed to him by the successor who ruled after him.
M: You have told me that your God was killed by the very people he had created. My God still lives and looks down on his children.
N: His line held Vilcabamba for thirty six years. In seventeen eighty, a man claiming descent from the last Sapa Inca took the name Tupac Amaru the Second and led the largest indigenous rebellion the colonial Americas had seen.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Epidemic That Beat Him There | academic
N: Smallpox reached Tawantinsuyu around fifteen twenty seven, years before any Spanish soldier, and it moved faster than any expedition could. The historian Noble David Cook traces exactly what that meant.
M: The disease spread so rapidly that before any Spaniard set foot in the highlands, the plague had killed the Inca and his heir, destroyed the succession, and plunged the empire into civil war.
N: Pizarro conquered an empire that was already collapsing. The population fell from nine to twelve million at contact to about one point three million by fifteen seventy, and six hundred thousand by sixteen twenty. Smallpox struck first, measles in the fifteen thirties, typhus and influenza in the fifteen forties and fifties, each wave landing on a population still weakened by the last one.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Andean Present | indigenous
N: Ten million people speak Quechua today. It was never written by its own speakers until the twentieth century, and it survived five hundred years of suppression because mothers spoke it to children in kitchens and fields the colonial bureaucracy could not reach.
N: The potato was domesticated in the Andes eight thousand years ago. Inca farmers grew more than three thousand varieties of it. It now feeds one point three billion people worldwide.
N: The Inti Raymi festival, the most sacred ceremony on the Inca calendar, was banned by Spain in fifteen seventy two. It was revived in Cusco in nineteen forty four and now draws fifty thousand visitors every year.
N: Rigoberta Menchu said this accepting the Nobel Peace Prize in nineteen ninety two, and it has been cited across indigenous movements far beyond her own country.
F: We are not historical ruins, curiosities for tourists. We are peoples and we want to be respected.

## REST | short

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each account has to leave out.
N: The Inca record leaves out the peoples it had itself conquered, and the civil war that had already split the empire before Pizarro landed.
N: The conquistadors' case leaves out disease as the real engine of collapse, and the indigenous allies who marched beside them against the Inca.
N: The resistance account is right that the siege nearly worked, and quieter about how small and isolated that resistance eventually became.
N: The epidemic account explains the collapse and says almost nothing about the massacres and the looting, choices no microbe made for anyone.
N: And the account of what survived says less than it might about the poverty indigenous communities in Peru and Bolivia still live inside, festivals and a Nobel speech notwithstanding.

N: The room where the ransom was piled still stands in Cajamarca. It is the only Inca building left standing in that city.
