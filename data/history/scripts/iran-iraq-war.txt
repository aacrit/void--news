## OPEN
N: Call this ancient sectarian hatred between Shia and Sunni, and the record disagrees.
N: That fault line hardened because of this war. It did not start it.
N: This war started over a river, a treaty five years old, and a government in Baghdad betting that a revolution next door had gutted its neighbor's army.
N: Both governments fought it with tanks and jets bought from the same outside powers.

## TITLE
N: The Iran-Iraq War. September twenty second, nineteen eighty, to August twentieth, nineteen eighty eight.

## SCENE 1 | Khuzestan, September 22, 1980
N: Five days earlier, on live television, Saddam Hussein tears up the nineteen seventy five Algiers Agreement, which had fixed the border down the middle of the Shatt al-Arab.
N: At dawn on September twenty second, Iraqi jets cross into Iranian airspace and strike ten airfields.
N: Iraqi columns roll into the oil province of Khuzestan. Baghdad expects the Arabs there to greet them and Tehran to fall within weeks.
N: The revolution that took power in Iran the year before had executed or exiled much of the Shah's officer corps. From Baghdad, that looks like an open door.

## ASIDE
N: Saddam opens with the same move Israel used in nineteen sixty seven: a surprise strike meant to destroy an enemy air force on the ground. Israel's worked. It won in six days.
N: Iraq's raids fail to cripple the Iranian air force, and the war Saddam starts that morning runs for nearly eight years.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | Khorramshahr, October 1980 - May 1982
N: Khorramshahr falls in late October after house to house fighting so bloody that Iranians rename it Khuninshahr, the City of Blood.
N: Tens of thousands of teenage Basij volunteers walk into Iraqi minefields and machine guns to clear paths for the Revolutionary Guard behind them, some wearing a plastic key that promises the gates of paradise.
N: By May nineteen eighty two, Iran has retaken Khorramshahr and pushed the Iraqis back to the border they crossed in nineteen eighty.
N: Saddam offers a ceasefire. Khomeini refuses, and sends his armies across the border into Iraq.

## SCENE 3 | The trenches, 1982-1987
N: The next six years are fought in trenches, marshes, and mountains, along a front that barely moves: the First World War re-staged with the weapons of the Cold War.
N: Iraq turns to chemistry. Its forces fire mustard gas and the nerve agents tabun and sarin directly at the human wave assaults Iran keeps sending across the line.
N: Neither government controls more ground at the end of these six years than the other did when they began.

## SCENE 4 | Halabja, March 16, 1988
N: In the war's final months, Iraqi aircraft fly over Halabja, a Kurdish town Iranian and Kurdish fighters have just taken, and drop a mix of mustard gas and the nerve agents sarin and tabun.
N: Roughly five thousand civilians die in an afternoon.
N: Halabja is one operation inside the wider Anfal campaign, which will raze some two thousand Kurdish villages before the year is out.
N: The man directing it is Saddam's cousin, Ali Hassan al-Majid. The Kurds call him Chemical Ali.

## DOCUMENT | Ali Hassan al-Majid | Tape recording of a Ba'ath Party meeting, recovered by Human Rights Watch | 1988
N: On one of the tapes Human Rights Watch later recovers, al-Majid answers his own rhetorical question.
M: I will kill them all with chemical weapons! Who is going to say anything? The international community?

## ASIDE
N: For years, the answer to that question is no one. Al-Majid is hanged in two thousand ten. An Iraqi court and several European parliaments later call the Anfal genocide.

## REST

## SCENE 5 | The Gulf, July 3, 1988
N: By nineteen eighty seven the war has reached the sea. Iraq and Iran are striking oil tankers in the Gulf, and the United States reflags Kuwaiti tankers and puts its navy between the two combatants.
N: On July third, nineteen eighty eight, the cruiser USS Vincennes is tracking what its crew believes is an attacking warplane.
N: It fires two missiles at Iran Air Flight six fifty five and kills all two hundred ninety people aboard. Sixty six of them are children.

## SCENE 6 | The ceasefire, July 1987 - August 1988
N: A year before the shootdown, the UN Security Council had already told both governments to stop.

## DOCUMENT | United Nations Security Council | Resolution 598, operative paragraph 1 | July 20, 1987
N: The Security Council's first paragraph is not a request.
M: Demands that, as a first step towards a negotiated settlement, the Islamic Republic of Iran and Iraq observe an immediate cease fire, discontinue all military actions on land, at sea and in the air, and withdraw all forces to the internationally recognized boundaries without delay.

## ASIDE
N: Khomeini waits another year. Two weeks after Iran Air six fifty five goes down, he finally accepts it.

## DOCUMENT | Ruhollah Khomeini | Message to the nation accepting UN Security Council Resolution 598 | July 20, 1988
N: Khomeini names what accepting it costs him.
M: Taking this decision is more deadly than drinking from a poisoned chalice. I submitted myself to Allah's will and took this drink for His satisfaction.

## ASIDE
N: The guns fall silent on August twentieth, nineteen eighty eight, along almost exactly the border where they opened fire in nineteen eighty. Between half a million and more than a million people are dead. Neither government has taken a village. Both declare victory.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of the same war, and two governments that each still call it a victory.

## PERSPECTIVE | Iranian Revolutionary / Sacred Defense | vanquished
N: In Iran this is not the Iran-Iraq War. It is the Sacred Defense against an Imposed War, and its founding fact is simple: Iraq crossed a recognized border while the revolution was barely a year old.
N: Khomeini frames resistance as a religious obligation, and the response is mass and voluntary. The Basij clear minefields so the Revolutionary Guard can advance behind them.
N: Khorramshahr, retaken in May nineteen eighty two after Iran renamed it the City of Blood, becomes proof that a barefoot revolution can throw back a Soviet equipped army.
N: The choice not to stop there is remembered as principle. Saddam has gassed Iranian conscripts and cannot be trusted to keep any ceasefire while he still rules.
N: The lasting grievance is abandonment: the Security Council will not name Iraq as the aggressor while Iranian soldiers are dying of nerve gas, and the lesson Iran draws is that it must arm itself.
M: War, war until victory.

## PERSPECTIVE | Iraqi Ba'athist | victor
N: Saddam Hussein names his war Qadisiyyat Saddam, after the seventh century battle where Arab armies broke the Persian empire: Iraq as the eastern shield of the Arab world against a Persian, Shia, revolutionary state.
N: The concrete grievance is the Shatt al-Arab. The nineteen seventy five Algiers Agreement moved the border to the middle of the waterway, a humiliation Iraq accepted only because it was weak at the time.
N: Once the Shah falls and Iran dissolves into revolutionary chaos, Saddam judges the humiliation reversible and abrogates Algiers.
N: When Iran carries the war onto Iraqi soil after nineteen eighty two, the story becomes national survival: Iraq holding a larger enemy at the gates of Basra.
N: When the ceasefire comes in nineteen eighty eight on the old border, Baghdad declares total victory. Its own account of why it invaded never wavers: the Shatt al-Arab was Iraqi, and Algiers had taken it away.

## PERSPECTIVE | Kurdish / Halabja and the Anfal | indigenous
N: For the Kurds of northern Iraq, the war within the war has its own name: the Anfal, the spoils, a term the Ba'ath borrows from the Quran for a campaign of extermination.
N: Kurdish peshmerga had allied at times with Iran against Baghdad, and Saddam's regime treats the entire rural Kurdish population as a fifth column to be erased.
N: Between February and September nineteen eighty eight, beyond Halabja alone, Iraqi forces destroy roughly two thousand villages, herd families into camps, and execute tens of thousands.
N: The Kurdish memory of this war is one of double abandonment: gassed by their own state, and denied acknowledgment for years by the powers courting Baghdad.
N: A Halabja survivor, not named in this record, is quoted describing the attack's first minutes.
F: They said it smelled of sweet apples, and then the birds fell out of the sky.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Great-Power Double-Dealing | bystander
N: This is the clearest case in the late Cold War of outside powers arming both sides of the same war, sometimes at once.
N: The Soviet Union is Iraq's largest supplier, providing tanks, jets, and the Scud missiles Saddam fires into Iranian cities. France sells Iraq Exocet missiles and lends it aircraft on credit.
N: Western firms sell the dual use chemical plants and precursors that feed Iraq's nerve agent program. When Iraq gasses Iranian troops and then Halabja, its suppliers issue statements and keep trading.
N: Washington runs the most contradictory policy of all: it tilts toward Baghdad with intelligence and diplomacy, even as elements of its own government secretly sell missiles to Tehran and use the proceeds to fund the Contras in Nicaragua.
N: When that secret sale becomes public in November nineteen eighty six, President Reagan has to explain it to the country himself.
M: A few months ago I told the American people I did not trade arms for hostages. My heart and my best intentions still tell me that's true, but the facts and the evidence tell me it is not.

## PERSPECTIVE | Regional Legacy / Later Scholarship | academic
N: Seen from later scholarship, this war is the hinge the modern Middle East turns on, and its verdict is that it settled nothing on the map while it changed everything around it.
N: After eight years, perhaps a million dead, and hundreds of billions of dollars, the border returns to the nineteen seventy five line. Neither side's own image of victory survives contact with the terrain.
N: What the war does change is the region's power structure. It bankrupts Iraq and arms it at the same time, a combination that points straight at Kuwait in nineteen ninety.
N: For Iran the war becomes foundational: the Revolutionary Guard emerges as an economic and political empire, and the memory of being gassed while the world watched becomes the standing justification for a missile program and, later, a nuclear hedge.
N: This is now the scholarly consensus: the war was modern and industrial, planned in ministries and fought with imported weapons, not an ancient sectarian one, and its true death toll remains an estimate because neither state has ever fully counted its own.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what each account leaves out.
N: Iran's account of a sacred, unwanted defense says little about its own decision to invade Iraq after nineteen eighty two, when the fighting could have ended, or about the teenage boys it sent across minefields to do it.
N: Iraq's account of a shield against Persian revolution says nothing about the scale of the chemical weapons campaign it ran, or about the war debt that would drive Saddam into Kuwait two years later.
N: The Kurdish account, entirely truthful about the gas, says little about the peshmerga's own alliance with the invading Iranian army, or about the divisions inside Kurdish politics that outsiders rarely see.
N: The great powers' account of double dealing has almost nothing to say about the two governments' own choice to fight for eight years, or about the Gulf monarchies that financed Iraq's half of it.
N: And the scholarship that spares no one else its judgment gives its longest chapters to ministries and missile programs, and comparatively few sentences to the people who were actually gassed, drafted, or drowned.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: Iraq comes out of this war owing more than eighty billion dollars, much of it to Kuwait and the Gulf states that financed its fight against Iran.
N: Two years later, Saddam invades Kuwait to erase that debt.
N: The war that follows brings a permanent American military base to Saudi Arabia. Osama bin Laden will later name that presence as his first grievance.
N: The border between Iran and Iraq, meanwhile, sits almost exactly where the jets crossed it on September twenty second, nineteen eighty.
