## OPEN
N: It started with a newspaper article. One smear piece, about a cleric almost nobody under thirty could remember, placed in a state paper on the order of a government ministry.
N: Thirteen months later a monarchy that traced itself back two and a half thousand years was gone.
N: The mechanism in between is the part worth knowing, and it is not political. It is a funeral custom.

## TITLE
N: The Iranian revolution. Nineteen seventy eight to nineteen seventy nine.

## SCENE 1 | Forty days
N: The seventh of January, nineteen seventy eight. The paper Ettela'at runs an article calling Ayatollah Khomeini, in exile for fourteen years, a British agent and an adventurer with an unknown past.
N: Two days later theology students march in Qom. Security forces fire into them. The government says two people died. The opposition says seventy two.
N: And now the custom. Shia mourning requires the community to gather on the fortieth day after a death.
N: Forty days later the mourners fill the streets of Tabriz, and the security forces fire again.
N: Forty days after that, the mourning is in Yazd, in Isfahan, and in thirty other cities at once.
N: Each massacre produced a mourning. Each mourning produced a massacre. The state could not stop shooting and could not stop the calendar, and the calendar was setting the pace.

## SCENE 2 | Where the money went
N: By September the rhythm breaks open. On the eighth, at Jaleh Square in Tehran, troops fire on a crowd. The government counts eighty four dead. The opposition says four thousand. The careful estimates land between eighty four and three hundred.
N: Martial law is declared and does not hold.
N: Then the oil workers walk out. Abadan, Ahvaz, Tehran. Iranian production falls from five point eight million barrels a day to four hundred thousand.
N: That is the hinge. The Shah has one of the best equipped armies in the world and no revenue to run it.
N: By December, somewhere between six and nine million people are in the streets. As a share of a national population, it is the largest protest anyone has recorded.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | A man with cassettes
N: The man they are marching for has not been in the country since nineteen sixty four. Turkey, then Najaf in Iraq, then a rented house in a village outside Paris.
N: He does not use television. His medium is the cassette tape. Sermons recorded abroad, copied in the thousands, carried in by hand and distributed through mosque networks, where the state has no way to jam them and no way to confiscate them all.
N: On the sixteenth of January, nineteen seventy nine, the Shah leaves. He tells the country he is going on holiday. He is fifty nine and he takes a box of Iranian soil with him.
N: He never comes back. Every country he asks for permanent asylum refuses him, and he dies in Cairo the following year.

## SCENE 4 | The eleven days
N: On the first of February, Khomeini lands at Mehrabad airport, and between three and five million people line the twenty six kilometres from the runway to the cemetery where he speaks.
N: There is still a government. There is still a prime minister, appointed by the Shah, sitting in his office.

## DOCUMENT | Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini | Speech at Behesht-e Zahra | February 1, 1979
N: Ayatollah Khomeini opened by dealing with that.
M: I shall kick their teeth in. I appoint the government. I appoint a government with the backing of this nation.
N: The government fell eleven days later.

## DOCUMENT | Revolutionary slogan | Late 1978 - early 1979
N: The slogan the crowds had been chanting all through it had three words in it, and the argument of the next forty years is about the order they came in.
M: Esteqlal, Azadi, Jomhuri-ye Eslami. Independence, freedom, Islamic republic.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of thirteen months.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Islamic Republic | victor
N: The first is the winner's, and its foundation is a piece of theology that was new.
N: The doctrine is velayat-e faqih, guardianship of the jurist. In the absence of the Twelfth Imam, who Shia tradition holds went into hiding in the ninth century and will return at the end of time, the most learned and just jurist should govern in his place.
N: For centuries the dominant position in Shia thought had been the opposite. Political authority was not legitimate until the Imam returned, so the clergy stayed out of it.
N: Senior ayatollahs in Najaf and Qom rejected the new doctrine, including the highest ranking Shia cleric in the world at the time.
N: Khomeini did not need their approval. He had the streets, and the constitution written that year built the state around the idea: an elected president and parliament, underneath a Supreme Leader nobody elects.

## PERSPECTIVE | The secular left | vanquished
N: The second account says the revolution was made by people who did not get it.
N: Count who was actually in the coalition. Marxist guerrillas who had been attacking police stations since nineteen seventy one. The communist party, which had organised the oil workers since the nineteen forties. A movement that fused Islam with Marxism and had been at armed struggle since sixty five. Liberal nationalists who wanted a parliament. And the bazaar merchants who financed the mosques.
N: Khomeini needed every one of them, and they all needed him, because he was the only figure the whole coalition could stand behind.
N: The first prime minister of the republic, Mehdi Bazargan, was a French educated engineer and a liberal, and his cabinet had no clerics in it. He lasted nine months, governing a country where every one of his decisions could be reversed by a revolutionary committee he had no authority over. This is how he described the job, the day he resigned.
M: No one listens to me. I am a knife without a blade.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The American account | vanquished
N: The third account starts twenty six years earlier.
N: In August nineteen fifty three the CIA and British intelligence overthrew Iran's prime minister, who had nationalised the oil industry, and restored the Shah to absolute power. The American station chief handed out cash to army officers and street crowds. The direct cost to the CIA was about sixty thousand dollars.
N: For the next twenty six years the Shah was Washington's man in the region, and Washington sold him twenty billion dollars of weapons in the last six years alone.
N: So when the revolution came, its central grievance was not abstract. It was that the country's sovereignty had been bought once already, in living memory.
N: And it ended with fifty two American diplomats held for four hundred and forty four days, which destroyed an American presidency and set the terms of a confrontation still running.

## PERSPECTIVE | The monarchists | vanquished
N: The fourth account belongs to the people who thought the country was being modernised, and it is not empty.
N: The land reform gave about three and a half million hectares to three million peasant families, roughly half the farmland in Iran. A literacy corps sent a hundred thousand school leavers into villages as teachers, and illiteracy fell from about eighty five per cent to somewhere between fifty and sixty.
N: The case against it is not that the reforms were fake. It is the method. The same state that redistributed the land banned every party, and ran a secret police that made opposition possible only inside a mosque, which is exactly where it went.
N: A moderniser who could not understand why the mosques were filling. Here is Mohammad Reza Pahlavi's own verdict on it, afterwards, in exile.
M: I should have been more severe from the start. This would not have happened.

## PERSPECTIVE | Women's rights, given and taken | vanquished
N: The fifth account has the shortest timeline in this whole catalogue. Twenty seven days.
N: Women were in the crowds all through nineteen seventy eight, in enormous numbers, including women who had never been political before.
N: On the sixth of March nineteen seventy nine, Khomeini announced that the hijab would be compulsory.
N: On the eighth, International Women's Day, somewhere between fifteen thousand and a hundred thousand women marched through Tehran against it. Some in chadors, some in headscarves, some bareheaded, which was itself the point.
N: Revolutionary guards and vigilantes went at them with knives and clubs. Many of the women being attacked had marched beside the men attacking them, four months earlier, against the same police.
N: And the law moved the other way fast. The family protection act, which had let a woman initiate divorce and required a court's permission for a man to take a second wife, was gone.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what each account cannot afford to say.
N: The republic's account presents itself as the restoration of tradition, and its founding doctrine was a break with the tradition. The senior clerics who said so are simply absent from the story it tells.
N: The left's account says the revolution was stolen, which is true and incomplete. Most of them backed Khomeini with their eyes open, believing that once the Shah was gone they could manage the cleric. They had years of evidence about his intentions. He had published them.
N: The American account explains the rage and not the shape. Nineteen fifty three tells you why Iranians were furious. It does not tell you why the mosque network beat the Marxists to the outcome, and a story in which Iran is only something done to it by Washington removes Iranians from their own revolution.
N: The monarchist account has real numbers behind it and the wrong conclusion drawn by its own protagonist. Told that his country had risen against him, he said he should have shot more people sooner.
N: And the women's account is the best documented and the least powerful. It is also the earliest warning anybody got. The decree came before the furniture of the old regime had been cleared out.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: The monarchy fell on the eleventh of February nineteen seventy nine. The Islamic Republic was proclaimed seven weeks later, after a referendum on the first of April, and it is governing Iran still.
N: Within twenty months it was at war with Iraq, for eight years, with chemical weapons used by both sides and somewhere between half a million and a million dead.
N: In nineteen eighty eight, thousands of political prisoners were hanged on the strength of a single order, and the state denied it for twenty eight years, until a recording of one of its own senior clerics objecting to it was leaked.
N: And the decree that fifteen thousand women marched against on the eighth of March, twenty five days after the monarchy fell, is still the law.
