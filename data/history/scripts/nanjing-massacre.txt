## OPEN
N: Picture Nanjing as a battle. A defending army meets an invading one, the defenders lose, and what follows is the massacre.
N: That is not what happened.
N: Days before Japanese troops reached the walls, the general commanding the city, Tang Shengzhi, swore publicly to hold Nanjing to the last man.
N: Then he crossed the Yangtze himself and left his own soldiers behind, with no orders and no way out.
N: Tens of thousands of them stripped off their uniforms and hid inside the walls, among two hundred thousand civilians who had nowhere else to go.
N: There was no army left to defeat. What the Japanese army spent the next six weeks doing was hunting for one, checkpoint by checkpoint.

## TITLE
N: The Nanjing Massacre. December the thirteenth, nineteen thirty seven, to February, nineteen thirty eight.

## SCENE 1 | The checkpoints
N: Inside the walls, Japanese units ran what they called mopping up operations, searching house by house for the soldiers who had disappeared into the crowd.
N: At checkpoints across the city, men were pulled aside and their hands were checked. A rifle leaves a callus on the skin. A callus was a death sentence.
N: Those men were marched in columns to the riverbank, to ponds outside the walls, to open ground, and shot or bayoneted in groups of hundreds and thousands.
N: Prince Yasuhiko Asaka, a member of the imperial family holding field command over the operation, was linked to an order to kill all captives.

## SCENE 2 | A newspaper kept score
N: Before the city had even fallen, a Japanese newspaper, the Tokyo Nichi Nichi Shimbun, began reporting a contest between two junior officers.
N: Second Lieutenants Toshiaki Mukai and Tsuyoshi Noda were racing to be first to kill one hundred people with a sword.
N: The paper ran the tally like a sports score. Eighty nine to seventy eight. Then one hundred and six to one hundred and five. It printed their photographs.
N: Both men survived the war. Both were tried by the Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal and executed by firing squad on the twenty eighth of January, nineteen forty eight.

## DOCUMENT | John Rabe | Diary of John Rabe, published as The Good Man of Nanking | December 17, 1937
N: John Rabe had run the Nanjing office of a German company for three decades and was the local head of the Nazi Party. In late November he had organized a safety zone for civilians. This is his diary, three days after the city fell.
M: Last night up to one thousand women and girls are said to have been raped, about one hundred girls at Ginling College alone. You hear nothing but rape. If husbands or brothers intervene, they're shot.

## ASIDE
N: The Tokyo tribunal later put the number of rapes in the first month at twenty thousand. Some historians, counting differently, reach eighty thousand. It happened in daylight, on the street, in front of families.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | The gate
N: At Ginling College, the American missionary Minnie Vautrin turned soldiers away at the gate for weeks and sheltered close to ten thousand women and girls inside the campus.
N: Every night she wrote down what she had not been able to stop.

## DOCUMENT | Minnie Vautrin | Diary of Minnie Vautrin, Ginling College | December 16, 1937
N: Minnie Vautrin wrote this entry on the sixteenth of December, four days after the city fell.
F: There probably is no crime that has not been committed in this city today. Thirty girls were taken from the language school last night, and today I have heard scores of heartbreaking stories of girls who were taken from their homes last night.

## ASIDE
N: She wrote that with soldiers still working the gate outside her own window. She kept writing, and kept turning them away, for the rest of the six weeks.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | The tribunals
N: Two tribunals eventually judged what had happened here. The Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal, run by the Republic of China, tried the men who had been on the ground, and produced the number three hundred thousand.
N: The International Military Tribunal for the Far East, sitting in Tokyo, reached higher up the chain of command. It found that burial societies alone had counted more than one hundred and fifty five thousand bodies, and put the total over two hundred thousand.
N: It convicted General Iwane Matsui, commander of the Central China Area Army, not for giving the order, but for failing to stop it. He was hanged on the twenty third of December, nineteen forty eight.
N: Prince Asaka, the imperial family member linked to the kill all captives order, was protected by the immunity General Douglas MacArthur extended to the imperial house. He was never charged.

## REST | short

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of the same six weeks, and one of them has to be introduced carefully first.
N: These are not five positions on whether this happened. Mainstream historians on every side of it, Chinese, Japanese, and international, agree that Japanese forces carried out mass killing and mass rape at Nanjing. One of the five accounts here quotes the position that denies it. That is not a competing history. It is presented as what was argued at the time, and as evidence of the argument, nothing more.

## PERSPECTIVE | Chinese National Memory | vanquished
N: The first account is the one carved in stone. In Nanjing the number is not treated as disputed. It is three hundred thousand, cut in ten foot characters into the black wall of the memorial hall, built on an excavated mass grave and opened in nineteen eighty five.
N: That figure comes from the nineteen forty seven tribunal, combined with burial society records and survivor testimony, and it sits inside a larger accounting. China counted roughly fourteen million dead across the whole war with Japan. Nanjing is that war's symbolic center: the capital sacked, surrendered soldiers shot at the riverbank, women violated in front of their families.
N: The writer Iris Chang, whose grandparents had escaped the city, brought this account to an audience in the West that had never heard it.
F: The Nanking massacre would be the forgotten Holocaust of World War II.
N: When she died in two thousand four, she was mourned in China as a national heroine. A statue of her now stands at the memorial hall.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Western Witnesses and the Safety Zone | bystander
N: The second account belongs to the roughly two dozen foreigners who chose to stay when the diplomats left.
N: They organized a demilitarized safety zone around the embassy quarter and the university, and packed it with somewhere between two hundred thousand and two hundred fifty thousand Chinese civilians.
N: Their chairman was John Rabe, the Nazi Party's local head in the city, who flew a swastika flag over his own property and wrote directly to Hitler asking him to intervene, gambling that Japanese soldiers might hesitate in front of an ally's flag.
M: You simply cannot conceive of the misery.
N: His diary was lost for half a century and resurfaced in nineteen ninety six. Minnie Vautrin's diary stands beside it, along with a surgeon's letters describing mutilation wounds, and sixteen millimeter film smuggled out of the city sewn into a coat lining. None of it was written by the Chinese state, and all of it says what the survivors say.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Japanese Historiography and Memory | academic
N: The third account is not one voice. Japanese scholarship on Nanjing has split for decades into three schools, and mainstream historians occupy two of them.
N: The historian Tokushi Kasahara counts the six counties around the city through March of nineteen thirty eight and reaches one hundred sixty to one hundred seventy thousand dead. Ikuhiko Hata counts only the walled city through early February and reaches roughly forty thousand, thirty thousand disarmed soldiers and ten thousand civilians. Both call what happened a massacre and a war crime. They disagree about how wide to draw the map, not about whether it happened.
N: The third position denies it happened at all. In nineteen ninety the Tokyo governor, Shintaro Ishihara, said this to an American magazine.
M: People say that the Japanese made a holocaust there, but that is not true. It is a story made up by the Chinese. It is a lie.
N: Mainstream Japanese historians, Hata included, reject that position outright. Textbook screening softened or removed the massacre for decades regardless, so that by the two thousands most Japanese textbooks named an incident at Nanjing with no death toll attached.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Survivor Testimony | vanquished
N: The fourth account is one household. On the thirteenth of December, nineteen thirty seven, Japanese soldiers entered the home of an eight year old girl named Xia Shuqin and killed her parents, her grandparents, and four of her sisters. Seven of the nine people in that house died.
N: She survived by hiding under a quilt with her four year old sister. The two children stayed alone with the bodies for fourteen days before neighbors found them.
N: In two thousand a Japanese author called her account a fabrication. She sued him in Tokyo.
F: Seven of my family were killed in front of me. How can they say it did not happen?
N: The district court ruled for her in two thousand seven. The Supreme Court of Japan upheld it in two thousand nine. A woman had to win a court case to prove that her own family had been killed.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The Legal Record and Command Responsibility | academic
N: The fifth account is the law's, and the law reached two different numbers because it looked from two different heights.
N: The Nanjing tribunal, working from the ground up, produced three hundred thousand. The Tokyo tribunal, working from the top of the chain of command down, produced over two hundred thousand, and convicted General Matsui for what he failed to do rather than what he ordered.
N: The same tribunal that hanged Matsui left Prince Asaka untouched, because the occupation authorities had already decided to protect the imperial family to keep the peace. A general who failed to stop the killing was executed. A prince linked to ordering it went free.
N: Neither tribunal could settle the number, because neither tribunal was asking the same question.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each account leaves out.
N: The Chinese account fixes a number that historians still disagree on, and treats disagreement with that number as an insult to the dead, which forecloses the argument rather than winning it.
N: The witnesses' account is the evidence every historian relies on, and it was gathered by two dozen foreigners who could shelter a quarter of the city and could not stop what happened to the rest of it.
N: The Japanese academic account is honest about its own divisions, and still names a denial position without quite explaining who in Japan still holds it, or why the textbooks kept softening anyway.
N: The survivor's account is the truest record of what one family lost, and by its own logic the number of families does not matter, which is also why the number of families is exactly what the other four accounts keep arguing about.
N: And the legal record proved the killing and hanged one general for it, then let the man who may have ordered it live until nineteen eighty one, because punishing him was judged less useful than keeping an emperor on his throne.

## CLOSE
N: The Nanjing memorial has begun casting the footprints of survivors in bronze, one by one, as the last of them die.
N: They were born before nineteen thirty. Most of them are gone now.
N: The bronze is there so that after the last footprint is cast, someone can still stand where they stood.
