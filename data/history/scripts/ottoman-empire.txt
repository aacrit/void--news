## OPEN
N: Say the Ottoman Empire and you probably think of Turks ruling for Turks.
N: For long stretches of six centuries, the men actually running the empire were not born Turkish, not born free, and not born Muslim.
N: They were Christian boys taken from their villages as children, forced to convert, and trained to administer the state that had taken them.
N: Some of the most powerful officials in its history never once chose to belong to it.

## TITLE
N: The Ottoman Empire. Twelve ninety nine to nineteen twenty two.

## SCENE 1 | The border smaller than Belgium
N: Twelve ninety nine, the frontier of Anatolia. A Turkmen tribal leader named Osman declares independence from the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum.
N: His territory is smaller than Belgium. His army is a loose confederation of ghazi raiders, men fighting for faith and plunder along the crumbling edge of the Byzantine Empire.
N: Osman will never see Constantinople in his lifetime. That city falls to his great-great-grandson.
N: What Osman starts on that border does not stop growing for six hundred and twenty three years.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | A capital of three faiths
N: By fourteen fifty three the great-great-grandson has the city, and instead of a Christian capital he sets out to build a different kind of city entirely.
N: He renames it Istanbul, calls himself Kayser i Rum, Caesar of Rome, appoints a Greek Orthodox patriarch, invites Jews back into the city, and resettles Muslims from across Anatolia.
N: By the time Mehmed the Second dies at forty nine, possibly poisoned by his own physician, the empire he leaves runs from the Danube to the Euphrates.

## DOCUMENT | Sultan Bayezid II | Attributed invitation to Sephardic Jews | c. 1492
N: Eleven years later, when Spain's Alhambra Decree orders every Jew in the country expelled, an invitation attributed to Sultan Bayezid the Second survives in the record.
M: You, the Jews, who have been expelled from Spain and Portugal, who have been persecuted by the Inquisition, come to us.
N: Sephardic communities resettle in Thessaloniki, Istanbul, Izmir, and Sarajevo. Within a century, Thessaloniki is a majority Jewish city, the only one in Europe.

## ASIDE
N: That invitation became policy, and the policy was not equality. Non Muslims paid the jizya, a poll tax, and could not bear arms.
N: It was still more structured pluralism than the Europe of the Inquisition had to offer.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | The sultan's death kept secret
N: Fifteen seventeen: Mehmed's grandson Selim the First conquers Mamluk Egypt, taking Mecca, Medina, and Cairo, and claims the caliphate, the leadership of Sunni Islam, for the dynasty.
N: His son Suleiman the First, called the Magnificent in Europe and Kanuni, the Lawgiver, by his own subjects, codifies that inheritance into the Kanunname, a legal code regulating taxation, land, and punishment across a population speaking dozens of languages.
N: In fifteen twenty nine his army besieges Vienna and fails, the furthest the empire ever reaches into central Europe.
N: When Suleiman dies in his own tent during the siege of Szigetvar in fifteen sixty six, his grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha hides the death for three weeks, so the army does not mutiny before the campaign ends.

## REST | short

## SCENE 4 | A sequence of amputations
N: The empire's final century is a list of losses. Greece wins independence in eighteen twenty nine, Serbia, Romania and Montenegro gain autonomy in eighteen seventy eight, Bulgaria breaks free in nineteen oh eight, Libya falls to Italy in nineteen twelve.
N: The Balkan Wars of nineteen twelve and nineteen thirteen reduce Ottoman Europe to a sliver of Thrace, and in November nineteen fourteen the empire enters World War One on Germany's side.
N: By nineteen eighteen the British have taken Jerusalem, Baghdad and Damascus. The Armistice of Mudros dissolves the Ottoman military that October, and the Treaty of Sevres proposes partitioning Anatolia itself.

## DOCUMENT | Mustafa Kemal Ataturk | Nutuk (The Great Speech) | October 15-20, 1927
N: A general born in Thessaloniki, Mustafa Kemal, refuses those terms, fights a three year war of independence, and drives the Greek army out of western Anatolia. Years later he explains it to his own party.
M: I am addressing the whole world! Sovereignty and sultanate are vested in the nation. The dynasty had seized sovereignty and sultanate by force.

## REST

## SCENE 5 | The suitcase and the warship
N: On the first of November, nineteen twenty two, the Grand National Assembly abolishes the sultanate.
N: The last Ottoman sultan, Mehmed the Sixth, leaves Istanbul on a British warship on the seventeenth of November.
N: The caliphate follows on the third of March, nineteen twenty four. The caliph, Abdulmecid the Second, is handed a suitcase and put on the Orient Express to Switzerland.
N: A state that lasted six hundred and twenty three years is gone in five.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of six centuries, and no two of them start the clock, or stop it, in the same place.

## PERSPECTIVE | Turkish Republican / Kemalist | academic
N: The Kemalist case starts with a suitcase. In March nineteen twenty four, the last Ottoman caliph is handed one and put on a train to Switzerland.
N: Two years earlier, delegates in Ankara had hesitated over abolishing the sultanate at all, until Mustafa Kemal told them plainly what sovereignty was.
M: Sovereignty is acquired by force, by power, and by violence. It was by violence that the sons of Osman seized the sovereignty and Sultanate of the Turkish nation.
N: His case rests on the empire's own historians. Halil Inalcik documented its best years, from Mehmed the Second through Suleiman, as a functional meritocracy, where Christian boys were converted, promoted, and given power on ability, not birth. Sokollu Mehmed Pasha was born an Orthodox Christian in Bosnia. Ibrahim Pasha was born Greek, in Parga. Koprulu Mehmed Pasha was born Albanian. All three ran the empire for sultans withdrawn behind the walls of Topkapi Palace.
N: The system worked, in this account, only while the sultan governed, and every later fix, the Tanzimat's constitution, the Young Turk revolution, arrived too late or too recklessly to save it.

## PERSPECTIVE | Arab Nationalist | vanquished
N: The Arab Nationalist case starts with a single rifle shot, fired at the Ottoman barracks in Mecca on the fifth of June, nineteen sixteen, by Sharif Hussein bin Ali, the city's Hashemite guardian. The Arab Revolt begins.
N: This account calls the four centuries before that shot an occupation, not a partnership. From nineteen oh eight the Committee of Union and Progress makes Turkish the only language of government, courts, and schools, reversing centuries of administration in the empire's many tongues.
N: Its clearest proof is Cemal Pasha, the Committee's governor of Syria, hanging twenty two Arab nationalists in Beirut's main square in nineteen fifteen, and seven more in Damascus the next year. Beirut still calls that square the Martyrs' Square.
N: Historians paraphrase the case George Antonius built for it this way.
M: The Arabs have been governed for four hundred years by a people who gave them neither rights nor representation, who drained their resources for wars in Europe, and who called this arrangement Islamic brotherhood.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Balkan / Orthodox Christian | vanquished
N: The Balkan Christian case starts with a battle both sides could claim to survive. At Kosovo Polje on the twenty eighth of June, thirteen eighty nine, both commanders die and Serbia survives another seventy years as a vassal state. None of that stopped the memory.
N: Serbian tradition turned that battle into the day a heavenly kingdom was chosen over an earthly one, the start of five centuries of servitude.
N: Its clearest evidence is the devshirme, the child levy. Every three to five years Ottoman officials entered Christian villages and took boys between eight and eighteen, converted them, and trained them as Janissaries or palace officials, for roughly three hundred years. Estimates of how many were taken run from five hundred thousand to one million. Mothers reportedly scarred their own sons to disqualify them.
N: In eighteen seventy six Ottoman irregulars killed an estimated twelve to thirty thousand Bulgarian civilians putting down an uprising. William Ewart Gladstone wrote a pamphlet about it that sold two hundred thousand copies in its first month.
M: Let the Turks now carry away their abuses in the only possible manner, namely, by carrying off themselves.

## PERSPECTIVE | Revisionist / Pluralist | revisionist
N: The pluralist case takes Bayezid's ships and asks what held them up for four centuries after the refugees landed.
N: The historian Karen Barkey reframes it. The millet system was not built as tolerance, it was an administrative tool for governing difference, but the effect held for centuries. A Jewish merchant in Istanbul settled a contract dispute in a rabbinical court under his own law. His Muslim neighbor used a sharia court. His Greek Orthodox neighbor used the Patriarch's court. The sultan adjudicated between the communities. He did not impose one law on all of them.
N: In eighteen fifty six an imperial edict declared every Ottoman subject equal regardless of religion, and the reform commission that followed produced a code of one thousand eight hundred and fifty one articles, built on Islamic law in the structure of the Napoleonic Code. Parts of it were still in force in Jordan, Palestine, and Israel in the middle of the twentieth century.
N: Karen Barkey's own description of the system, paraphrased, was blunt.
F: The empire was not a melting pot. It was a mosaic in which each tile kept its shape, its color, and its law.

## PERSPECTIVE | Postcolonial / Dissolution Consequences | revisionist
N: The dissolution case starts with a line on a map that nobody living beside it was shown. In nineteen sixteen a British diplomat and a French one divided the Ottoman Arab provinces between their two countries in secret, and that is its own broadcast. What concerns this one is what was built on top of it.
N: Britain combined three separately governed Ottoman provinces, a Kurdish and Turkmen north, a Sunni Arab center, a Shia Arab south, into one new state called Iraq, and gave it a king who had never set foot in the country. France cut a Lebanon out of Ottoman Syria with borders drawn to guarantee a Christian majority. Lebanon's civil war, decades later, followed exactly those lines.
N: The Treaty of Lausanne in nineteen twenty three then unmakes what centuries of Ottoman rule had mixed together: one point two million Orthodox Greeks expelled from Turkey, four hundred thousand Muslims expelled from Greece, all of it compulsory. Fridtjof Nansen, the League of Nations official who ran the exchange, called his own project this.
M: A thoroughly bad and vicious solution.
N: The same dissolution left thirty to forty five million Kurds, promised a state at Sevres in nineteen twenty and denied one at Lausanne three years later, spread across four countries whose borders were drawn on Ottoman maps.

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what none of the five fully accounts for.
N: The Kemalist case explains why the Republic replaced the empire, and says less about the Kurdish revolts the new Republic itself suppressed, or the forced assimilation that replaced the pluralism the millet system had allowed.
N: The Arab Nationalist case is right about Turkification and right about the gallows in Beirut, and it says less about the Arab elites who served and prospered inside that same system for centuries.
N: The Balkan Christian case is right that the devshirme took children by force, and it says less about the devshirme graduates who reached the highest offices of the state and sometimes aided the very communities that lost them.
N: The pluralist case can point to Bayezid's ships and a legal code running to one thousand eight hundred and fifty one articles, and it says less about the poll tax non Muslims paid or the weapons they were barred from carrying under that same law.
N: And the dissolution case blames two diplomats and a map for the borders, and says less about the Arab, Turkish, and Kurdish leaders who then fought each other over what those borders would become.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: In fourteen ninety two, Thessaloniki took in the ships Bayezid the Second sent for the Jews expelled from Spain. Four hundred and thirty years later, the empire that sent those ships was abolished by a general born in that same city.
