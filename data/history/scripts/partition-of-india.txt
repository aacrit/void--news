## OPEN
N: There is a border in South Asia that two nuclear armed states have fought four wars across, and are still contesting today.
N: You would assume a line like that was drawn carefully. Over years. By people who knew the ground.
N: It was drawn in five weeks, by a man who had never been to India, working from maps that were six years out of date.

## TITLE
N: The Partition of India. August, nineteen forty seven.

## SCENE 1 | The bungalow in Delhi
N: July, nineteen forty seven. A guarded bungalow in Delhi, with the curtains drawn.
N: Inside, a British barrister named Cyril Radcliffe is working from maps. He landed in India on the eighth of July. He has been given until the fifteenth of August.
N: That is five weeks to divide a hundred and seventy five thousand square miles.
N: He has census data from nineteen forty one, six years out of date. He has survey maps that do not show where the irrigation canals run.
N: He does not visit the Punjab. He does not visit Bengal. He has been told that impartiality requires him to know nothing about the place. So he knows nothing about the place.
N: The line he is drawing will cut through villages, through farms, through the canal system that waters the Punjab, and through the railways that connect it.

## DOCUMENT | Sir Cyril Radcliffe | private correspondence, quoted by the historians Read and Fisher | 1947
N: Radcliffe wrote about the job privately, that same year.
D: The division of India is, in my opinion, the greatest blunder in the whole history of the British Empire.

## ASIDE
N: He was not describing someone else's decision. He was describing his own work, while he was doing it.

## SCENE 2 | Seventy three days
N: The deadline had not always been August. The transfer of power was scheduled for June, nineteen forty eight. Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, moved it forward by ten months.
N: He said later that the reason was momentum.
N: Seventy three days. That was the notice given to four hundred million people that their country was about to become two countries.
N: And they were not told which one they lived in. The line was not published on the fifteenth of August. It was published on the seventeenth.
N: Independence came first. The border came two days later.

## DOCUMENT | Jawaharlal Nehru | Tryst with Destiny, to the Constituent Assembly | August 14, 1947
N: On the night of the fourteenth, in Delhi, Jawaharlal Nehru spoke.
D: At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom.

## ASIDE
N: In the Punjab, people were also awake. They did not yet know which country they were standing in.

## SCENE 3 | The columns
N: What followed was the largest migration in human history, and it took about four months.
N: Fourteen to fifteen million people crossed the new borders. Hindus and Sikhs moved east. Muslims moved west.
N: On some roads the columns stretched fifty miles. People walked in a hundred and ten degree heat. Cholera moved through the columns, and so did dysentery. Thousands who survived the violence died of thirst.
N: In Lahore, fires burned for weeks. In Amritsar, mobs blocked the roads against the refugee columns. Armed bands attacked the trains, in both directions.
N: Some trains reached their destinations carrying no living passengers at all.
N: The railway staff at Wagah had a term for those, and they wrote it in the log. Ghost trains.

## SCENE 4 | The wells
N: The violence was not only on the roads. It was inside villages where nothing had changed except the information.
N: In border districts, neighbours who had shared wells for generations turned on each other within hours of learning which side of the line they had fallen on.
N: Nothing else had moved. The fields were the same. The houses were the same. What arrived was a fact about a map, and it was enough.

## DOCUMENT | Muhammad Ali Jinnah | address to the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan | August 11, 1947
N: Three days before independence, in Karachi, Muhammad Ali Jinnah addressed the assembly of the country he had spent a decade arguing into existence.
D: Pakistan is a dream that has come true.

## ASIDE
N: He said that on the eleventh. The Punjab was already burning. He died of tuberculosis thirteen months later.

## SCENE 5 | The force that was supposed to hold
N: The army that was meant to prevent this was being divided at the same time it was needed.
N: Of the fifty five thousand troops in the Punjab, roughly half were being transferred to the new Pakistani military.
N: A neutral force was created to hold the province. The Punjab Boundary Force. Twenty five thousand men, under Major General Rees.
N: It was disbanded on the first of September, after less than a month. The reason given was that it had been overwhelmed.

## SCENE 6 | How many
N: Nobody knows how many people died.
N: The early British figures said two hundred thousand. Later scholarship, by historians including Gyanendra Pandey and Urvashi Butalia, put it closer to two million.
N: That is not a small disagreement. It is a factor of ten, inside living memory, about one of the largest killings of the twentieth century.
N: The reason is simple and it is bleak. Most of these deaths were never registered. There was no state on either side of the line with the capacity to count, because the state itself was what had just been divided.
N: Mass graves found in the Punjab and in Bengal, decades afterwards, have kept revising the number upward.
N: So the figure you will see, one to two million, is not a measurement. It is an estimate of an absence of records.

## DOCUMENT | Mahatma Gandhi | prayer meeting address, Calcutta | August 1947
N: In Calcutta that month, Gandhi was fasting against the killing. At a prayer meeting, he said this.
D: I do not agree with what my Muslim friends say, that we cannot live together.

## ASIDE
N: He was assassinated five months later, by a Hindu nationalist who believed he had conceded too much.

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Here is the part that does not resolve.
N: There are five standard accounts of Partition. They do not really disagree about the facts. They disagree about what is missing from them.
N: The British account is administrative. A difficult handover, under time pressure, accepted by both Indian parties. It leaves out how arbitrary the line was. It leaves out a hundred and ninety years in which British administration hardened Hindu and Muslim into census categories and separate electorates.
N: The Indian nationalist account holds that partition was a Muslim League demand, which Congress accepted to save independence. It leaves out Congress's own failure to accommodate the League, between nineteen thirty seven and nineteen forty six.
N: The Pakistani account holds that a separate state was the only protection for a minority that would otherwise be permanently outvoted. It leaves out how thin the League's organisation was before nineteen forty. It leaves out Direct Action Day.
N: The fourth account is not about states at all. It is about the fourteen million. From inside a refugee column, the constitutional achievements are not the story, and that account leaves them out entirely.
N: The fifth is about the seventy five thousand women who were abducted. And about what the two new governments agreed to do next, which was to recover them and return them across the border. In many cases to families who no longer wanted them. In some cases against their own stated wishes.
N: None of these five is a lie. Each one is a true account with a hole in it.
N: And the holes are not random. Each account omits the part that would cost its own side the most.

## CLOSE
N: The Radcliffe Line left a hundred and sixty two enclaves stranded on the wrong side of the border with what became Bangladesh. Villages inside a country they did not touch.
N: They were not resolved until the Land Boundary Agreement of twenty fifteen. Sixty eight years.
N: And the division did not stop dividing. In nineteen seventy one, Pakistan itself broke along the contradiction it had been founded on, and Bangladesh became a third country born from the first partition.
N: The Punjabi poet Amrita Pritam wrote the lines that the north still remembers Partition by. She addressed them to a poet who had been dead for two hundred years, and asked him to speak. She did not write about politics. She wrote about the rivers.
N: Both states hold nuclear weapons now. They have fought four wars. Kashmir is still contested.
N: A hundred and seventy five thousand square miles. Six year old maps. Five weeks.
