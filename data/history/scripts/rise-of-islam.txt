## OPEN
N: Ask when Islam began and most people will say six hundred ten, the night a merchant saw an angel in a cave outside Mecca. The people who wrote the calendar disagreed.
N: The Islamic calendar does not start with that night. It starts twelve years later, in six hundred twenty two, when that merchant and his followers left Mecca for a city two hundred seventy miles north.
N: The cave produced five verses and one believer, his own wife. The emigration produced a government. The calendar remembers the government.

## TITLE
N: The Rise of Islam. Six hundred ten to seven hundred fifty.

## SCENE 1 | The cave on Mount Hira
N: Six hundred ten. A forty year old merchant of the Quraysh tribe climbs to a cave on Mount Hira, above Mecca, the way he does most years, to think alone.
N: Islamic tradition says an angel, Jibril, seizes him, holding on until he can barely breathe. It repeats one command. Read.
N: Muhammad ibn Abdullah cannot read. He was never taught.
N: He comes down shaking and tells his wife, Khadijah, older than him, wealthier than him, and the woman who employed him before they married.
N: She does not tell him he imagined it. She covers him, and she believes him.

## DOCUMENT | The Quran | Surah Al-Alaq (96:1-5) | c. 610 CE
N: What Muhammad recited that night became the opening verses of the Quran.
M: Read, in the name of your Lord who created. Created man from a clinging substance. Read, and your Lord is the most Generous, who taught by the pen, taught man that which he knew not.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | Twelve years, and a road north
N: For twelve years Muhammad preaches in Mecca, and Mecca does not want to hear it. The Quraysh elite run the Kaaba, and a single god with no room for the others threatens their pilgrim income.
N: His followers stay in the dozens, then the low hundreds. In six hundred twenty two, facing an assassination plot, Muhammad leaves for a town called Yathrib, two hundred seventy miles north.
N: It renames itself Medina, the City of the Prophet.

## SCENE 3 | The empire with no successor
N: June eighth, six hundred thirty two. Muhammad dies in Medina at sixty two, having named no one to follow him.
N: He leaves roughly one hundred thousand Muslims, a city state controlling most of the Arabian Peninsula, and a Quran that exists only in memory, on palm leaves, and on the shoulder blades of camels. No dynasty. No manual for running any of it.
N: Senior companions choose Abu Bakr, Muhammad's father in law and closest companion, as the first caliph.

## DOCUMENT | Abu Bakr al-Siddiq | Inaugural address, recorded in Tabari's History | 632 CE
N: Abu Bakr's first words as caliph set the terms of the office.
M: O people, I have been given authority over you, but I am not the best of you. If I do well, help me, and if I do wrong, set me right.
N: Within two years he is fighting the Ridda Wars, putting down tribes that stopped paying their tax the moment the Prophet was gone.

## REST | short

## SCENE 4 | Fifteen years
N: What follows is the fastest imperial expansion in recorded history to that point. Within twelve years of Muhammad's death, Arab armies destroy the Sassanid Persian Empire, four hundred years old, and strip Byzantium of Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and Libya.
N: At Yarmouk, August six hundred thirty six, an Arab force of twenty five to forty thousand meets a Byzantine army of eighty to one hundred fifty thousand. Six days later the Levant is gone for good.
N: The same year at Qadisiyyah, the Sassanid army is routed. By six hundred fifty one the last Sassanid emperor, Yazdegerd the Third, is running east, begging local rulers for soldiers who will not come.

## REST

## SCENE 5 | Three funerals
N: The community Muhammad left behind did not stay peaceful.

## DOCUMENT | Umar ibn al-Khattab | Statement during his caliphate | c. 634-644 CE
N: Umar ibn al-Khattab, the second caliph, governs an empire from Libya to Iran out of a mud brick house, and describes the job like this.
M: I am not a king. I am only one of you. I eat as you eat and I sit as you sit.
N: In six hundred forty four, a Persian slave named Abu Lu'lu'a stabs Umar to death.
N: Uthman ibn Affan succeeds him and compiles the scattered Quran into one written text. In six hundred fifty six, soldiers from Egypt murder him in his own home.
N: Ali ibn Abi Talib, Muhammad's cousin and son in law, becomes the fourth caliph and inherits a civil war. At Siffin in six hundred fifty seven, Muawiya's soldiers, losing the field, raise pages of the Quran on their spears and force a ceasefire neither side can live with.
N: In six hundred sixty one, a Kharijite named Ibn Muljam kills Ali with a poisoned sword in the mosque at Kufa. Muawiya declares himself caliph. The office is now a dynasty, headquartered in Damascus.

## REST

## SCENE 6 | The largest empire the world had seen
N: The Umayyads build the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and the Great Mosque of Damascus, and under Caliph Hisham the caliphate stretches from the Pyrenees to the edge of China: sixty two million people, about twenty nine percent of everyone alive.
N: In seven hundred fifty, the Abbasid revolution overthrows the dynasty that built it.

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of those hundred forty years, and the fight over who tells it starts inside the religion itself, before it reaches the empires on the border.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Sunni tradition | victor
N: This account starts with a man who had no political ambition and no army: a merchant who walked away from Mecca's gods to think alone.
N: It reads the next twenty two years, persecution, emigration, the conquest of Mecca, as a plan unfolding, not a career. At Badr in six hundred twenty four, three hundred thirteen Muslims defeated roughly a thousand Quraysh, and the Quran credits the outcome to God, not the men who fought it.
N: The conquests, in this reading, freed people from empires that punished the wrong kind of Christian or Zoroastrian, offering lower taxes and wider tolerance instead.
N: Its proof is Umar, who ran the largest empire on earth from a mud brick house in patched clothes, holding himself to a standard he stated himself.
M: If a single old woman in Iraq went to bed hungry, Umar would be answerable to God for it.
N: Khalid ibn al-Walid, called the Sword of God, never lost a battle in fifty engagements. To this tradition, that record is not luck.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Shia tradition | vanquished
N: This account begins before Muhammad's body was washed. While Ali performed that duty, senior companions met without him at the Saqifa and chose Abu Bakr instead.
N: The case rests on one word. At Ghadir Khumm, Muhammad took Ali's hand and said: whoever I am his mawla, Ali is his mawla. Sunni tradition reads mawla as friend. Shia tradition reads it as master, overridden at the Saqifa.
N: Ali waited twenty four years for the office, through three caliphs, and inherited a civil war on arrival: Muawiya's refusal, the ambush at Siffin, an arbitration that treated a usurper as an equal.
N: The wound became permanent at Karbala in six hundred eighty. Ali's son Husayn, with seventy two fighters, was cut off from water for three days and killed by Yazid's army. He explained why he had come.
M: I did not rise up out of wickedness or arrogance, but to seek reform in the community of my grandfather. I want to command good and forbid evil.
N: Every year since, Ashura reenacts his death as the paradigm the tradition lives by.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Byzantium and Persia | vanquished
N: This account starts with exhaustion, not an army. Byzantium and Persia had just spent twenty six years trying to finish each other off. The Sassanids took Jerusalem and occupied Egypt; Heraclius mortgaged his own church's gold to fund the counterattack that drove them back.
N: Neither treasury recovered, and the plague did the rest. The Arab armies were not unstoppable. There was almost nothing left to stop them with.
N: Heraclius received the news of Yarmouk in Antioch, and is remembered for one line about it.
M: Farewell, Syria. What a beautiful land you are for the enemy.
N: For an empire that saw itself as chosen by God, losing the Holy Land was not only a defeat. It was a question with no comfortable answer.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Western revisionists | revisionist
N: This account questions the sources before the story. The earliest full biography of Muhammad was written around seven hundred sixty seven, more than a century after he died.
N: Patricia Crone and Michael Cook read the seventh century through outside sources instead: Greek, Syriac, Armenian. A Greek chronicle from six hundred thirty four describes an Arab prophet still alive, two years after tradition says he died.
N: Crone's position, across her work, was that the Islamic tradition is a monument to the faith of the believers rather than a transparent window onto the seventh century. She later retreated from the book's most provocative claims. The methodological point survived her.
N: Fred Donner's more moderate version puts it in one sentence.
M: The Believers' movement initially included righteous Christians and Jews as well as followers of Muhammad.
N: A sharply bounded Islamic identity, on this reading, hardened only later, under the Umayyads.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Islamicate civilization | revisionist
N: This account argues the conquest is the least interesting part of what happened next. Marshall Hodgson coined the word Islamicate for the civilization it produced: not a list of rulers, but a shared world from the Sahara to the edge of China.
N: The Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun built the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, where Christian, Jewish, and Muslim scholars translated Greek philosophy into Arabic, and al-Khwarizmi's treatise on algebra gave mathematics the word algorithm.
N: This account finds its own proof in the Quran itself.
M: There is no compulsion in religion. Truth stands out clear from error.
N: Umar in patched clothes, walking to hear the poor, belongs here too: a caliphate that argued with itself, not one that only conquered.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what none of the five will say about itself.
N: The Sunni account calls the succession pragmatic consensus, and leaves out that three of the first four caliphs were assassinated by their own community.
N: The Shia account rests everything on the meaning of one word, and says little about how much of Sunni tradition also honors Ali as a righteous caliph.
N: The Byzantine and Persian account explains the speed of the collapse, and says little about why so many of their own subjects were glad to see them go.
N: The revisionists question every source inconvenient to their argument, and say nothing of what the traditional account means to one point nine billion people for whom this is not academic.
N: And the Islamicate account, the most flattering of the five, is quiet about the dhimmi system's real restrictions, and the slavery its golden age also depended on.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: Yazdegerd the Third ran for nine years before he was killed by a miller in Merv, in six hundred fifty one. An empire that had fought Rome and Byzantium to a standstill for seven centuries ceased to exist in fifteen years.
N: A century later, when the Abbasids came for the Umayyads, one prince got out. Abd al-Rahman escaped to Spain and founded an independent emirate at Cordoba, on the far shore of the sea the conquest had never quite closed.
