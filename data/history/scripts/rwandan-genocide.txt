## OPEN
N: The word used at the time, in newsrooms and in foreign ministries, was tribal. Ancient hatreds. Something that had welled up and would burn itself out.
N: Here is what the record shows. Roadblocks were up across the capital within one hour of the plane coming down. The lists of who to kill had been typed in advance. The peacekeepers from one particular country were murdered first, because the planners had calculated that their government would then leave, and they were right.
N: And the document a militiaman at a roadblock asked you for, the one that decided whether you lived, had been designed by a Belgian colonial administration in the nineteen thirties.

## TITLE
N: The Rwandan genocide. April to July, nineteen ninety four.

## SCENE 1 | One hour
N: The evening of the sixth of April, nineteen ninety four. A Falcon 50 carrying the presidents of Rwanda and Burundi comes in to land at Kigali.
N: A surface to air missile takes it down. Everyone aboard is killed. Who fired it has never been settled, and both answers serve somebody.
N: Within the hour, roadblocks manned by the Interahamwe militia and the presidential guard are up across the city.
N: They have lists. Tutsi politicians, moderate Hutu officials, journalists, human rights workers, by name and address.
N: A killing that begins one hour after the trigger event is not a reaction to it.

## SCENE 2 | The prime minister
N: Agathe Uwilingiyimana was the prime minister. She was a moderate Hutu, and she was near the top of the list.
N: On the morning of the seventh she telephoned the headquarters of the United Nations mission.

## DOCUMENT | Agathe Uwilingiyimana | Phone call to UNAMIR headquarters | April 7, 1994
N: What Agathe Uwilingiyimana said survives because the force commander wrote it down afterwards, so this is his record, not a recording.
F: We are in a crisis of killings and political assassinations. My guards have been killed. I have no protection. Prepare an intervention.
N: She was found and killed that day.
N: The ten Belgian peacekeepers assigned to protect her were disarmed by Rwandan government soldiers, taken to a barracks, tortured and killed.
N: Belgium withdrew its contingent within days. That was the purpose of killing them, and it worked.
N: Two weeks later, on the twenty first of April, the United Nations Security Council voted. Not to reinforce the mission. To cut it, from two and a half thousand troops down to two hundred and seventy.

## SCENE 3 | A hundred days
N: The instrument was the machete, the club and the small arm, which is why the pace is so hard to take in.
N: A radio station broadcast the encouragement and a good deal of the targeting. Announcers called Tutsis cockroaches and snakes, and read names and addresses on air.
N: The organisation was administrative. Killing squads were raised at commune level by the local officials who ran everything else, and officials who refused to take part were killed themselves.
N: You can see that in one province. Butare was the one prefecture with a Tutsi prefect, and the killing there started ten days later than everywhere else. It started when the government removed him and sent militia down from the capital.
N: People went where people go. Ten thousand sheltered in the Catholic church at Nyamata, and were killed there with machetes and grenades over two days.
N: At a technical school at Murambi, local officials told fifty thousand Tutsis they would be protected if they gathered there. On the twenty first of April, soldiers and militia killed about forty thousand of them in one night.

## DOCUMENT | Jean Kambanda | Statement to ICTR investigators | 1997
N: Jean Kambanda was the prime minister of the interim government through those hundred days. He would later become the first head of a government anywhere to plead guilty to genocide. This is what he told investigators.
M: We, the members of the government, do not know how to stop the massacres.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of a hundred days.

## PERSPECTIVE | The survivors | vanquished
N: The first belongs to the people it was done to, and its first insistence is on the word neighbour.
N: This was not carried out by strangers who arrived. It was carried out at close range by people who knew the names of the children they were killing, in the parish, at the school, on the road between two houses.
N: Between two hundred and fifty and five hundred thousand women and girls were raped, as a method, deliberately. The tribunal that followed recognised something no court had recognised before, that rape had been used here as an instrument of genocide, and convicted on that basis.
N: The survivors were then left with the consequence. Health agencies later estimated that seven in ten of them were living with HIV.
N: And they were left living in the same villages, because there was nowhere else, often within sight of the families of the people who had done it.

## PERSPECTIVE | The peacekeepers | bystander
N: The second account is the mission on the ground, and it turns on a single fax.
N: On the eleventh of January, three months before any of this, the force commander, Romeo Dallaire, cabled New York. An informant inside the militia had told him three things. Weapons were being cached across the capital for distribution. Belgian peacekeepers would be attacked in order to force a withdrawal. And lists of every Tutsi in Kigali had been drawn up, in the informant's words, for their extermination.
N: The informant offered to show him the weapons. Dallaire asked permission to raid them.
N: The reply came the same day. The raid exceeded his mandate. He was told instead to pass the intelligence to the president of Rwanda, whose own circle was organising it.
N: Everything in that fax then happened, in order.
M: I know there is a God, because in Rwanda I shook hands with the devil. I have seen him, I have smelled him and I have touched him.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The United Nations | bystander
N: The third account is the political one, in New York and in Washington, and it is mostly about a word.
N: The Genocide Convention obliges the states that signed it to act. So the question of whether to use the word was not a semantic one. It was a legal trigger, and everybody in the room knew it.
N: So for weeks the language was acts of genocide, or tribal violence, or civil war. One American spokeswoman was asked directly how many acts of genocide it takes to make a genocide, and had no answer.
N: Meanwhile the Council had a mission on the ground that it chose to shrink, in the third week, with the killing at its height.
N: Four years later the Secretary-General, Kofi Annan, who had run the peacekeeping department that sent the reply to Dallaire, stood in the Rwandan parliament and said it plainly.
M: The international community failed Rwanda, and that must leave us always with a sense of bitter regret.

## PERSPECTIVE | The pan-African reading | revisionist
N: The fourth account says the clock started a century earlier.
N: Hutu and Tutsi existed before any European arrived. They were not what they became. They were fluid social categories, tied to cattle and to closeness to the royal court, and people moved between them.
N: What the Belgian administration did in the nineteen thirties was fix them. Every Rwandan was measured, classified and issued a card. Hutu, about eighty five in a hundred. Tutsi, fourteen. Twa, one. If you owned ten cows or more, you were Tutsi.
N: The classification was hereditary and could not be changed, and it was built on a racial theory imported from Europe which held that the sophistication of East African kingdoms must have come from somewhere further north.
N: Tutsis got the schools, the administrative posts and the church hierarchy. Hutus got the labour. Then, at independence, the colonial power changed sides and handed power to the majority it had spent forty years subordinating.
N: Nineteen ninety four was carried out with Rwandan hands. The instrument in the hand at the roadblock was printed in Brussels.

## PERSPECTIVE | Rwanda after | academic
N: The fifth account is about what was built on the ruins, and it does not resolve.
N: The courts first. No conventional legal system could try a million cases, so Rwanda adapted a traditional village hearing. About twelve thousand community courts processed one point nine six million cases in seven years. It is the largest exercise in transitional justice ever attempted, and it was conducted in public, by neighbours, about neighbours.
N: Then the state. Growth averaged seven and a half per cent a year for nearly two decades. Foreign aid fell from eighty six per cent of the national budget to forty. Kigali became a showcase. Rwanda has among the highest proportions of women in parliament anywhere in the world.
N: And the same government permits no real opposition, jails and silences journalists, and has been credibly linked to the killing of critics who fled abroad.
N: Scholars call it the Rwandan paradox. A country that will not have another nineteen ninety four, governed in a way that does not allow anyone to test that claim.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: What each account is not carrying.
N: The survivors' account is the one with the least power to shape policy, and the least incentive to be diplomatic about a government that also protects them.
N: The peacekeepers' account is about a warning ignored, which is a story with a clear villain in New York, and it quietly steps around how little two thousand lightly armed monitors could have done once it started.
N: The United Nations account is institutional, and institutions apologise in the passive voice. The international community failed is a sentence with no subject in it.
N: The colonial account explains the categories and the card. It does not pick up a machete. Explaining where a weapon came from is not the same as saying who used it, and the people who organised this were adults making decisions.
N: And the account of the Rwanda that followed leans on its own results, which is what every government that cannot be voted out eventually does.

## CLOSE
N: The killing stopped because the rebel army won, in July, and not because anybody intervened.
N: France's safe zone in the southwest, set up in the last weeks, also opened a road out. The organisers crossed it into what is now the Democratic Republic of Congo, rebuilt as armed groups, and the wars that followed there have killed several million more people.
N: The doctrine that states have a responsibility to protect populations from this was adopted by the United Nations in two thousand and five. It is invoked selectively.
N: A hundred days. Something near a million people. Five times the rate of the Nazi camps.
N: And of the fifty thousand who went to the school at Murambi because officials told them it was safe, thirty four were found alive the next morning, among the dead.
