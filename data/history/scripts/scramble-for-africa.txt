## OPEN
N: Most people picture the moment they mean by the Scramble for Africa. Fourteen nations leaning over a map in Berlin, dividing a continent with a ruler and a pen.
N: That meeting happened. It ran for one hundred and four days. It drew not one border on any map.
N: What came out of it was a rule. A flag, a garrison, and an administration in place would now beat an older claim made by treaty, or by simply getting there first. The actual taking of the continent took the next thirty years, and none of it was done with a pen.

## TITLE
N: The Scramble for Africa. Eighteen eighty four to nineteen fourteen.

## SCENE 1 | Berlin, November fifteenth, eighteen eighty four
N: Berlin, November the fifteenth, eighteen eighty four. Otto von Bismarck opens a conference at his own official residence.
N: Fourteen nations send delegates, including the United States, which holds no African colony and never will. No African ruler is in the room. No African diplomat. No African representative of any kind.
N: In eighteen seventy, one African in ten lived under a European flag. By nineteen fourteen it will be nine in ten.
N: What this room agrees on is not a map. It is a rule. The power that holds a flag, a garrison, and a working administration on the ground now outranks any older claim made by treaty, or by simply arriving first. The paperwork calls it effective occupation.

## DOCUMENT | Leopold II | Letter to his London ambassador, Baron Solvyns | 1876
N: Eight years before that room, Leopold the Second of Belgium already knows what he wants out of it, and writes to his ambassador in London, Baron Solvyns.
M: I do not want to miss a good chance of getting us a slice of this magnificent African cake.

## ASIDE
N: A slice of cake is a private appetite, not a state policy. And that is exactly what Leopold gets out of Berlin. Not a colony of Belgium, but a personal possession of his own, called the Congo Free State.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | The Congo Free State
N: The Congo Free State runs on rubber. Every village is set a quota, collected by the Force Publique, an army of African soldiers under European officers.
N: A village that misses its quota is punished with hostages, burned huts, mutilation. The chicotte, a whip cut from hippopotamus hide, is standard issue. Officers cut the hands from workers who fall short, or from corpses, to prove the bullets were not wasted on hunting instead.
N: A British shipping clerk named E.D. Morel notices the pattern in his own company's ships. They come back from the Congo loaded with rubber and ivory. They go out loaded with nothing but guns and ammunition.

## DOCUMENT | E.D. Morel | Red Rubber: The Story of the Rubber Slave Trade Flourishing on the Congo | 1906
N: Morel starts an international campaign, and in a book called Red Rubber, writes down what the trade actually cost.
M: He kept the left hands of all the corpses he had in his collection. These hands were smoked to keep them from putrefying.

## ASIDE
N: That collection was bookkeeping. A quota proved in body parts is still a quota. Morel's campaign, and a report from the British consul Roger Casement, forced Belgium's own parliament to take the Congo away from its king in nineteen oh eight.
N: By nineteen twenty, a population estimated at fifteen to thirty million in eighteen eighty five had fallen to roughly ten million.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | German South West Africa, October second, nineteen oh four
N: German South West Africa, October the second, nineteen oh four. The Herero and the Nama have risen against colonial rule. General Lothar von Trotha has an answer.
N: Von Trotha drives the Herero into the Omaheke Desert and has the waterholes poisoned and guarded.

## DOCUMENT | General Lothar von Trotha | Vernichtungsbefehl (Extermination Order), in the English translation published by German History in Documents and Images | October 2, 1904
N: Von Trotha puts it in writing, an order he calls the Vernichtungsbefehl, the extermination order. In an English translation:
M: Any Herero found within the German frontier, with or without a gun, with or without cattle, will be shot.

## ASIDE
N: Of an estimated eighty thousand Herero, sixty five thousand die. Survivors go into camps at Shark Island and at Swakopmund, where more than forty five in every hundred do not survive the camp either.
N: Germany called this a colonial war for one hundred and seventeen years. It recognized it as genocide in twenty twenty one.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts, and one fact before they start. One is the profiteer's own words, written as he went. Two are the words of people who fought back, on either side of the Sahara, in records that barely speak to each other. The last two, decades apart, argue about what the whole thing built, and who is still paying for it.

## PERSPECTIVE | The colonizers | victor
N: The first account is Europe's own case for what it was doing, resting on three legs: rivalry, economics, and duty.
N: Bismarck is refereeing France, Britain, and a newly unified Germany before their competition for territory turns into a war on European soil.
N: The economic case is raw material. The Second Industrial Revolution wants rubber, palm oil, copper, diamonds and gold, and tropical Africa has all five. One hundred and ten million Africans are also a market.
N: The moral case belongs to Rudyard Kipling.
M: Take up the White Man's burden.
N: The Berlin Conference's own general act commits its signers to watch over the preservation of the native tribes, and care for their moral and material well being.

## PERSPECTIVE | The kingdoms that resisted | vanquished
N: The second account is that Africa in eighteen eighty four was not empty ground waiting for a flag.
N: The Ashanti Empire fought the British to a standstill across four wars between eighteen twenty four and nineteen hundred. In eighteen seventy nine the Zulu Kingdom under Cetshwayo killed thirteen hundred British and colonial troops at Isandlwana, the worst defeat a European army suffered from an African force in the whole century.
N: In nineteen hundred the British governor demands the Golden Stool, the sacred symbol of Ashanti sovereignty. The male chiefs hesitate. Yaa Asantewaa, Queen Mother of Ejisu, does not.
F: If you, the men of Ashanti, will not go forward, then we will. I shall call upon my fellow women. We will fight the white men. We will fight till the last of us falls in the battlefields.
N: The war lasts a year. The British win it. They never find the stool.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Pan African critique | revisionist
N: The third account says the Scramble did not merely happen to Africa's economy. It built the economy that came after, on purpose.
N: Before eighteen eighty four, Africa had its own textile industries, ironworking, and long distance trade running from the Sahel to the Mediterranean and the Congo basin to the Atlantic. Colonial economies replaced all of it with a single cash crop apiece: cotton, cocoa, groundnuts, rubber, sent out raw and bought back as finished goods.
N: Frantz Fanon, writing in nineteen sixty one, names what held that system up.
M: Colonialism is not a thinking machine, nor a body endowed with reasoning faculties. It is violence in its natural state, and it will only yield when confronted with greater violence.
N: Kwame Nkrumah had a name for what happened after independence, when the borders stayed and the money kept flowing out. Neo colonialism.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The economic ledger | academic
N: The fourth account tracks the money through three commodities: rubber, diamonds, copper.
N: Leopold's rubber alone made him close to two hundred and twenty million francs between eighteen ninety six and nineteen oh five. The British consul Roger Casement went to investigate it for the Foreign Office, in what became one of the first human rights reports on record.
M: I shall never return to the Congo. Not because of the danger, but because I cannot bear what I have seen.
N: Economists Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson call institutions built only to extract, extractive, and argue they explain why the inequality outlasted the flags.

## PERSPECTIVE | North Africa's own war | vanquished
N: The fifth account is the half of the Scramble the word itself usually leaves out. North Africa was taken by the same powers, in the same decades, and met with a resistance that had its own tradition to draw on.
N: French Algeria's conquest killed an estimated eight hundred twenty five thousand people, close to a third of the population, over seventeen years against the emir Abd el Kader.
N: In Sudan, Muhammad Ahmad declared himself the Mahdi and built a state that destroyed a ten thousand man Egyptian force at Shaykan, held Khartoum, and killed General Gordon when the city fell. It governed for thirteen years before Kitchener's army ended it at Omdurman.
N: In Libya, Omar al Mukhtar fought Italian forces for twenty years, against concentration camps and poison gas, until his capture and execution at the age of seventy three.
M: We shall not surrender. We shall win or we shall die.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: Each of these accounts is carrying something, and leaving something behind.
N: The colonizers' account leaves out that its own civilizing mission provided cover for extraction, which is why almost no historian defends it today.
N: The resistance account leaves out that African kingdoms also held captives and fought each other, and that some rulers signed away land they did not understand they were signing away.
N: The Pan African critique leaves out that it measures African achievement on scales Europe chose, in square kilometers of territory and tonnes of ore, and that some of what it condemns has continued under African governments since independence.
N: The economic ledger leaves out everyone who was not a price. Read only the ledger and the Scramble is a transaction, not sixty five thousand Herero dead in a desert.
N: And North Africa's own account leaves out that some of its own elites collaborated with the same powers it fought, and that its resistance is remembered in a story with almost no room in it for women.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: Ethiopia and Liberia are the only two African states still independent by nineteen fourteen.
N: Forty four of Africa's fifty four nations now sit inside borders drawn at that conference table, or in the treaties that followed it, borders that put at least one hundred seventy seven language and ethnic groups on the wrong side of a line somebody else drew.
N: The rifle that did most of the work fired six hundred rounds a minute. Its name was the Maxim.
