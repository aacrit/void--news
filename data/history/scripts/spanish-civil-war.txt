## OPEN
N: You know the postcard version. Young idealists crossing oceans to fight fascism in Spain, Hemingway and Orwell and the Lincoln Battalion, thirty five thousand volunteers from fifty countries.
N: The other side had foreigners too, and more of them. Germany rotated nineteen thousand men of its Condor Legion through Spain. Italy sent as many as seventy five thousand troops.
N: For Germany this was not a romantic cause. It was a laboratory, testing the dive bomber and the fighter plane and the bombing of towns for a war that would start five months after this one ended.
N: The war remembered as the last great cause was also, on the record, somebody else's rehearsal.

## TITLE
N: The Spanish Civil War. July seventeenth, nineteen thirty six, to April first, nineteen thirty nine.

## SCENE 1 | Madrid, July 19, 1936
N: Two days earlier the garrisons of Spanish Morocco rose against the Republic, and General Francisco Franco flew from the Canary Islands to take command of the Army of Africa.
N: By the nineteenth the rising has reached the capital. Trade unionists and loyal Assault Guards storm the barracks before the army can decide.
N: In a Madrid radio studio that same day, a Communist orator known across Spain as La Pasionaria goes on the air.

## DOCUMENT | Dolores Ibarruri (La Pasionaria) | Radio address, Madrid | July 19, 1936
N: Dolores Ibarruri tells the city what to do with the soldiers coming for it.
F: No pasaran. They shall not pass.

## ASIDE
N: They did not, not in Madrid. Within a week the coup has half succeeded and half failed. The plotters hold Seville, Burgos, and the rural west and north, while Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, and Bilbao crush the rising outright. A country of twenty five million is cut in two.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | The powers arrive, 1936
N: What the generals expected to finish in days is turning into years, and the rest of Europe arrives before Spain can catch its breath.
N: Hitler's Junkers transports have already airlifted some twenty thousand troops of the Army of Africa across the Strait of Gibraltar, one of the first large scale military airlifts in history.
N: Stalin answers with tanks, aircraft, and advisers.
N: Britain and France convene a Non-Intervention Committee of twenty seven nations. Germany, Italy, and the Soviet Union all sign it, and all three break it.

## SCENE 3 | The University of Salamanca, October 12, 1936
N: Jose Millan-Astray, who founded the Spanish Legion, stands inside the university and gives the rising its own war cry.

## DOCUMENT | Jose Millan-Astray | War cry, University of Salamanca | October 12, 1936
N: Millan-Astray shouts three words that will follow the movement for the rest of the war.
M: Long live death.

## DOCUMENT | Miguel de Unamuno | Rebuke to Millan-Astray, University of Salamanca | October 12, 1936
N: Miguel de Unamuno answers him from inside the same room.
M: You will win, but you will not convince.

## ASIDE
N: The regime already has a name for its own certainty. It calls the war a Cruzada, a crusade for Christian civilization. Unamuno, in one sentence, told the men running it that winning a war and being right are not the same thing.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | Guernica, April 26, 1937
N: A market day afternoon in the Basque town of Guernica. The German Condor Legion and the Italian Aviazione Legionaria bomb it for more than three hours. Roughly eighty five percent of the buildings burn.
N: The dead have never been agreed on. Estimates run from about one hundred fifty to more than one thousand six hundred.
N: George Steer of The Times files a dispatch that circles the world. Franco's press answers that the Basques dynamited their own town.

## DOCUMENT | Jose Antonio Aguirre | Statement, Basque government | April 1937
N: Jose Antonio Aguirre, the Catholic president of the Basque government, answers Franco's press directly.
M: Before God and history, which shall judge us, I affirm that for three and one half hours German planes bombed the defenseless civilian population of Guernica.

## ASIDE
N: Pablo Picasso turns Steer's dispatch into a mural for the Paris Exposition that same year. The propaganda war has opened alongside the shooting war, and it will outlast it by decades.

## REST

## SCENE 5 | Barcelona, May 3 to May 8, 1937
N: Government forces and Communists fight the anarchist CNT-FAI and the anti-Stalinist POUM in the streets. About five hundred die.
N: The POUM is outlawed. Its leader, Andreu Nin, is seized and killed by Soviet NKVD agents.
N: George Orwell had fought with the POUM militia and been shot through the throat at the Aragon front. He flees Spain and writes Homage to Catalonia.

## REST

## SCENE 6 | The end, October 1938 to April 1939
N: The International Brigades are withdrawn that autumn, a failed bid to force German and Italian withdrawal from the other side. Dolores Ibarruri addresses them one last time in Barcelona.

## DOCUMENT | Dolores Ibarruri (La Pasionaria) | Farewell address to the International Brigades, Barcelona | October 28, 1938
N: Ibarruri tells thirty five thousand foreign volunteers what they are leaving behind.
F: You can go proudly. You are history. You are legend.

## ASIDE
N: The Ebro offensive that follows bleeds the Republic white. Barcelona falls in January nineteen thirty nine, and some five hundred thousand refugees walk into France in an exodus called La Retirada.
N: Madrid, besieged since November nineteen thirty six under the cry No pasaran, falls on March twenty eighth, nineteen thirty nine. Four days later, Francisco Franco issues his final communique.

## DOCUMENT | Francisco Franco | Final war communique | April 1, 1939
N: Franco tells Spain, in two sentences, that it is over.
M: Today, with the Red Army captured and disarmed, the national forces have reached their final military objectives. The war is over.

## ASIDE
N: His dictatorship lasts until his death on November twentieth, nineteen seventy five. Thirty six years, for a war the generals expected to finish in days.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of the same war, and the argument over which one is right did not end when the shooting did.

## PERSPECTIVE | Republican Spain | vanquished
N: The Republic called itself the lawful government of Spain. The Popular Front had won the elections five months before the rising, and July was a coup against a ballot box.
N: The Republic held roughly two thirds of the population, the capital, the industrial belts of Catalonia and the Basque Country, and the gold reserve. It called this democracy against fascism, a year before the rest of the world used those words for a war of its own.
N: It argues it was not defeated so much as abandoned. The Non-Intervention Committee starved the one side that needed to buy weapons, and Munich in September nineteen thirty eight told the Republic that Britain and France would appease Hitler.
N: George Orwell fought for that Republic and then watched part of it turn on itself. He wrote down what that taught him.
M: I saw, in fact, history being written not in terms of what happened, but of what ought to have happened according to various party lines.

## PERSPECTIVE | Nationalist Spain | victor
N: This account does not begin with the coup. It begins with the chaos the coup claims to answer, strikes, land seizures, and church burnings through the first half of nineteen thirty six, and the killing of the monarchist leader Jose Calvo Sotelo by Assault Guards on July thirteenth.
N: The rising was a coalition of the right, the professional army, the fascist Falange founded by Jose Antonio Primo de Rivera, the traditionalist Carlist requetes, monarchists, and Catholic conservatives. Franco welded them into one state party in April nineteen thirty seven and made himself Caudillo.
N: The regime called the war a Cruzada, a crusade to defend Christian civilization against an atheist and Bolshevik conspiracy, and the Spanish bishops endorsed the cause in writing the following year.
N: Victory bought stability, in this account. Franco kept Spain out of the Second World War, and aligned with the United States in nineteen fifty three in exchange for military bases.

## PERSPECTIVE | International Volunteers and the Great Powers | bystander
N: To much of the watching world this looked like a foreign story about foreigners. The International Brigades held the Lincoln Battalion of about two thousand eight hundred Americans, of whom some seven hundred fifty died, the British Battalion, and the German Thaelmann and Italian Garibaldi units of exiles who could not fight in their own countries.
N: Above all of it sat the fiction of Non-Intervention. Twenty seven nations met in London while Germany, Italy, and the Soviet Union armed their own clients and lied about it. Stalin extracted over five hundred tonnes of Spain's gold reserve to pay for the aid, and the NKVD came with the advisers.
N: Hermann Goering, testifying at Nuremberg after the next war was over, said plainly why Germany had come.
M: I urged the Fuehrer to give support to Franco under all circumstances, first to prevent the further spread of communism, and second to test my young Luftwaffe.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Catholic Church and Rural Spain | revisionist
N: The Church remembers exact numbers. Thirteen bishops, four thousand one hundred seventy two priests and seminarians, two thousand three hundred sixty four monks and friars, and two hundred eighty three nuns, six thousand eight hundred thirty two clergy killed, most of them in the summer and autumn of nineteen thirty six.
N: To the Church this was the culmination of a Republic that had disestablished it in nineteen thirty one and secularized schools and marriage. Forty three Spanish bishops said in writing, in July nineteen thirty seven, that the rising was self defense.
N: But Catholic Spain was not one thing. The Basque Country was devout and Republican, and Franco's forces shot fourteen to sixteen Basque priests for it. The war set the rosary against the rosary.
N: Beneath the language of faith ran an older war over land. In Andalusia and Extremadura landless laborers had seized fields in nineteen thirty six, and the Nationalist reconquest of the countryside was as much a landlord's revenge as a religious crusade.

## PERSPECTIVE | Memory and the Historians' War | academic
N: When Franco died in nineteen seventy five, Spain chose silence. The nineteen seventy seven Amnesty Law, approved by ninety three percent of the new parliament, closed the door on prosecutions in both directions, a pact of forgetting.
N: The silence broke from below. In two thousand a man named Emilio Silva dug his grandfather out of a roadside grave and started an exhumation movement. An estimated one hundred fourteen thousand people still lie in unmarked mass graves, a number the United Nations ranks second in the world only to Cambodia.
N: The historians went to war over it. Paul Preston documented around two hundred thousand executions behind the lines. Revisionists led by Pio Moa argued the Republic had started the war.
N: Paloma Aguilar named what the whole transition had rested on.
F: The transition to democracy was built on a collective and voluntary decision to forget.

## REST | short

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what each account cannot afford to say.
N: The Republican account says little about the Red Terror it ran alongside its own defense: six thousand eight hundred thirty two clergy killed, the executions at Paracuellos, and the killing of Andreu Nin, its own dissident leader.
N: The Nationalist account calls the repression regrettable surgery and passes quickly over the true scale of it, roughly one hundred fifty thousand executed, over Guernica, and over the German and Italian armies that made the victory possible.
N: The international account tells a story of great powers and has little room for the Spanish causes of a Spanish war, or for the Communist International's control of the Brigades it organized.
N: The Church's account counts its own dead precisely and says less about the decades it spent as the dictatorship's ally, or about the landless laborers shot at Badajoz.
N: And the historians' account, which spares no one else its scrutiny, has the least patience for the lived immediacy of the killing years, or for those who still defend the pact of forgetting as the price of peace.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: Picasso set one condition on the mural's return to Spain: not until public liberties and democratic institutions were restored.
N: He died in nineteen seventy three. Franco died two years later. The painting reached Spain in nineteen eighty one, after democracy, and neither man was alive to see it arrive.
