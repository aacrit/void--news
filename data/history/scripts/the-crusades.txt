## OPEN
N: The Fourth Crusade never reached Jerusalem. It never even turned south. In twelve hundred and four it sacked Constantinople, the largest Christian city in the world, and looted it for three days.
N: And the First Crusade started killing eight hundred miles from Jerusalem, in the Rhine valley, in the spring before it crossed into Asia. The people it killed there were not Muslims.
N: Two centuries of war are filed under a single word, and a great deal of what the word covers was aimed at other Christians, and at Jews, and at nobody in the Holy Land at all.

## TITLE
N: The Crusades. Ten ninety five to twelve ninety one.

## SCENE 1 | God wills it
N: The twenty seventh of November, ten ninety five. A field outside Clermont, in central France, because no church is big enough.
N: Pope Urban the Second tells the crowd that the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem is in the hands of the Seljuk Turks, and that the men in front of him should go and take it back.
N: The crowd answers with two words. Deus vult. God wills it.
N: What he had been asked for, by the emperor in Constantinople, was mercenaries. A few thousand professional soldiers, on contract, to help push the Turks back out of Anatolia.
N: What he launched was something nobody could steer.

## SCENE 2 | Speyer, Worms, Mainz
N: The first crusader violence happens in the spring of ten ninety six, in German cities on the Rhine, before any army has left Europe.
N: A minor count named Emicho leads an unofficial crusade through the Rhineland with an argument his followers find airtight. Why march two thousand miles to avenge the death of Christ, when the people we hold responsible for it live here?
N: In Speyer the bishop takes the Jewish community inside his own palace. Nine are caught outside and killed. In Worms the bishop tries the same thing and the crusaders break down his gates.
N: In Mainz, in May, more than a thousand Jews are killed in a single day.
N: Some communities, cornered, chose what the Hebrew sources call sanctification of the name. Rather than accept forced baptism, parents killed their children and then themselves.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | The fifteenth of July, ten ninety nine
N: Four years later the armies are at the walls of Jerusalem, and they get over them.

## DOCUMENT | Anonymous | Gesta Francorum | c. 1101
N: What follows is three days. This is a Latin chronicler travelling with the army, writing about his own side, with approval.
M: They were killing and slaying even to the Temple of Solomon, where the slaughter was so great that our men waded in blood up to their ankles.

## DOCUMENT | Ibn al-Athir | Al-Kamil fi'l-Tarikh | c. 1231
N: And this is Ibn al-Athir, a Muslim historian in Mosul, writing a century later from the accounts that reached him.
M: The population of the holy city was put to the sword, and the Franks spent a week massacring Muslims. In the Masjid al-Aqsa the Franks slaughtered more than seventy thousand people.
N: Modern estimates put the dead between forty and seventy thousand, which is close to everyone who lived there. The synagogue where the city's Jews had taken refuge was burned with them inside.
N: Then the crusaders took off their shoes and walked to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre to give thanks.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | Eighty eight years later
N: They held it for eighty eight years, and lost it in an afternoon.
N: On the fourth of July, eleven eighty seven, at Hattin, above the Sea of Galilee, Saladin destroyed the entire field army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem. Twenty thousand men killed or taken, the king among them.
N: Jerusalem surrendered in October. And Saladin did not do what had been done in ten ninety nine.
N: He set a ransom. Ten dinars for a man, five for a woman, one for a child. Those who could not pay were enslaved, about fifteen thousand of them, and his own brother bought a thousand of those and set them free as a gift.
N: The comparison between the two conquests has been doing political work in both directions ever since.

## SCENE 5 | The wrong city
N: The Fourth Crusade sails in twelve hundred and two, deep in debt to Venice for ships it cannot pay for.
N: Venice proposes the army work off the debt. First by taking a Christian port on the Adriatic for Venice, which gets the whole expedition excommunicated. Then, with a claimant to the Byzantine throne offering money it does not have, by sailing on Constantinople.
N: In April twelve hundred and four they take the city and sack it for three days. The relics go to churches across western Europe, where many of them still are.

## DOCUMENT | Nicetas Choniates | Annals | c. 1206
N: Nicetas Choniates was a Byzantine official who watched it and got out.
M: Even the Saracens are merciful and kind compared with these men who bear the Cross of Christ on their shoulders.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts, and the first thing to notice is that the two sides in the title are only two of them.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Latin crusaders | vanquished
N: The first is the crusaders' own, and the modern version of it is not the one you expect.
N: For a long time the assumption was that these were younger sons with no land, going east to get some. The records do not support it. Crusading was catastrophically expensive. Men mortgaged estates, sold rights, and beggared their families to go, and the great majority came home poorer, if they came home.
N: What Urban offered was not land. It was an indulgence, remission of the penance owed for sin, for anyone who went in the right spirit. To a man who believed that literally, the arithmetic was obvious.
N: And they built real states on it. Four of them, defended by the new military orders, the Templars and the Hospitallers, and financed by Venice, Genoa and Pisa, who took commercial privileges in every port as their price.
N: A French speaking aristocracy governing Arabic speaking Muslims, Christians and Jews, for two hundred years, which is a colony however it described itself.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Muslim world | victor
N: The second account starts with a fact that complicates the whole story. The initial response was not a holy war. It was confusion, and division.
N: The Seljuk empire was fragmenting. Local rulers made and broke alliances with the newcomers, sometimes against other Muslim rulers, for decades.
N: Saladin, a Kurd who had made himself sultan of Egypt and Syria, spent as much of his career unifying Muslim territory as fighting Franks, because the first was the precondition for the second.
N: And the Franks were not only enemies. Usama ibn Munqidh, a Syrian nobleman who spent decades among them, wrote a book of observations that is by turns furious, condescending and warm. He kept Frankish friends. He also recorded this.
M: Among the Franks, God damn them, there are none more wretched than the knights. They are the great ones and the fighters, but everyone else is merely a servant to them.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Byzantium | bystander
N: The third account is the one that asked for this and could not stop it.
N: The Byzantine emperor Alexios had lost most of Anatolia to the Turks a generation earlier. He wrote west asking for soldiers he could pay and command. What arrived first was an unarmed mob, and then armies under lords who refused to take his orders and wanted his territory.
N: His daughter Anna Comnena wrote the history, which makes her one of very few women writing history anywhere in the medieval world. She found the Franks unwashed, greedy, oath breaking and dangerous, and she said so at length.
N: Her central example was a Norman prince who had attacked the empire before, swore to hand Antioch back to Alexios when it fell, took it in ten ninety eight, and kept it.
N: A century later the Fourth Crusade finished the argument. The empire never recovered from twelve hundred and four, and the city fell for good in fourteen fifty three, to the Ottomans.

## PERSPECTIVE | The modern historians | revisionist
N: The fourth account is about what later centuries did with all of this.
N: In eighteen twenty five Walter Scott wrote Richard the Lionheart and Saladin as chivalric equals, and Europe liked that version enormously. France published a vast collection of crusade sources as a monument to French achievement in the east. When France took its mandate over Syria and Lebanon after the First World War, over ground that had been the County of Tripoli and the Principality of Antioch, its administrators said the connection out loud.
N: Then Steven Runciman, who read Latin, Greek and Arabic, wrote the first major English history that used all three, and ended it by calling the whole enterprise a long act of intolerance in the name of God.
N: And the correction to Runciman came from inside the field. Jonathan Riley-Smith went through the charters and the debts and found the piety was real, which does not excuse anything, but does mean the cynical reading was also a story people wanted.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Rhineland | vanquished
N: The fifth account has the fewest survivors and no strategic importance at all, which is exactly why it keeps falling out.
N: A Latin chronicler recorded Emicho's followers explaining themselves, and recorded it without any apparent discomfort: we are going a long way to take vengeance, and here among us live the people whose forefathers killed him.
N: The Hebrew account written a generation later names communities, families and individual deaths, because that was the point of writing it.
N: What ten ninety six established was not a single atrocity. It was a pattern. Every subsequent crusade preaching in Europe was followed by attacks on Jewish communities, and the accusation that had been improvised in the Rhineland hardened into a permanent fixture of European life.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what each of them cannot account for.
N: The crusaders' account explains why men went. It does not explain what they did when they arrived, and it cannot explain the Fourth Crusade at all, which is why it usually skips it.
N: The Muslim account leans on the comparison between ten ninety nine and eleven eighty seven, and that comparison is true. It also compresses two centuries into two days, and it quietly passes over the decades when Muslim rulers allied with Franks against each other.
N: The Byzantine account is written fifty years after the fact by a daughter defending her father, about a disaster that began with her father's own request.
N: The modern account is two arguments pretending to be one. Whether the Crusades were sincere and whether they were justified are different questions, and Runciman's verdict is a moral judgement wearing the clothes of a history.
N: And the Rhineland account is left out of the other four because it changes nothing about who held Jerusalem. That is the whole reason it has to be included.

## REST

## CLOSE
N: Acre, the last crusader city, fell in twelve ninety one. Two hundred and ninety six years later Constantinople was Ottoman, and the Latin states were a memory that had lasted less time than the argument about them has.
N: The word is still live ammunition. An American president used it about a war in September two thousand and one and had to stop using it within days.
N: The relics taken out of Constantinople in twelve hundred and four are still in churches in Venice and France.
N: And in Mainz, in the memorial book, the names of the people killed in May ten ninety six are still read aloud in synagogue, nine hundred and thirty years later.
