## OPEN
N: The most famous sentence Martin Luther ever spoke, he did not speak.
N: Here I stand, I can do no other. That line is not recorded on the spot at the Diet of Worms. It was added into the earliest printed account of the day, after the fact.
N: What he did say, on the record, is that his conscience was captive to the Word of God, and that he would not recant.
N: And the man remembered for standing alone against an empire spent his career, three years later, telling princes to hunt down the people who took his own talk of freedom literally.

## TITLE
N: The Protestant Reformation. Fifteen seventeen to sixteen forty eight.

## SCENE 1 | The debate that got away
N: October thirty first, fifteen seventeen. Wittenberg. An Augustinian friar and professor named Martin Luther sends ninety five complaints about the sale of indulgences to the Archbishop of Mainz.
N: By tradition he also posts them on the door of the Castle Church, the university's own notice board, for other scholars to debate. In Latin.
N: He does not send them to a printer. Someone else does.
N: Within two weeks copies are moving across Germany. Within two months, across Europe. By the end of the year, editions are running in Leipzig, Nuremberg, and Basel, around three hundred copies at a time.
N: Luther later tells Pope Leo the Tenth it is a mystery to him how the theses traveled so far, so fast, when he never wanted them published at all.

## DOCUMENT | Martin Luther | Ninety-Five Theses, Thesis 86 | October 31, 1517
N: Thesis eighty six is the one where Luther asks the plainest question in the whole document.
M: Why does not the pope, whose wealth is today greater than the wealth of the richest Crassus, build this one basilica of St. Peter with his own money rather than with the money of poor believers?

## ASIDE
N: This isn't a call to arms. It's a receipt, held up to the light.
N: If the pope can build his church with his own money, the question is why he's building it with theirs.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | Worms
N: Pope Leo the Tenth excommunicates Luther in January, fifteen twenty one.
N: In April, the young Emperor Charles the Fifth summons him to the Imperial Diet at Worms and orders him to recant.

## DOCUMENT | Martin Luther | Address at the Diet of Worms | April 18, 1521
N: Luther, standing in front of the most powerful ruler in Europe, answers him directly.
M: Unless I am convinced by the testimony of the Scriptures or by clear reason, I am bound by the Scriptures I have quoted and my conscience is captive to the Word of God. I cannot and will not recant anything.

## ASIDE
N: Charles declares him an outlaw whom anyone can kill.
N: Frederick the Wise of Saxony has Luther kidnapped instead, to the Wartburg castle, for safekeeping. There, in eleven weeks, the outlaw translates the entire New Testament into German.

## REST

## SCENE 3 | Frankenhausen
N: Peasants read Luther's talk of Christian freedom and hear a promise about their own lives. In fifteen twenty four and fifteen twenty five, three hundred thousand of them rise.

## DOCUMENT | Martin Luther | Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants | 1525
N: Luther's answer to the people who took him at his word.
M: Let everyone who can, smite, slay, and stab, secretly or openly, remembering that nothing can be more poisonous, hurtful, or devilish than a rebel.

## ASIDE
N: The princes oblige. Roughly one hundred thousand peasants are killed.
N: The man who told an emperor his conscience was captive to nothing but scripture had just told princes exactly how far his reformation went, and no further.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | The fracture
N: Ulrich Zwingli leads the Swiss Reformation from Zurich, and dies in battle in fifteen thirty one.
N: John Calvin builds a disciplined Reformed Geneva.
N: In England, the king breaks with Rome over a divorce, not a doctrine.
N: Western Christianity is no longer one thing. It is several, and each one thinks the others are wrong.

## SCENE 5 | The treaty and the war
N: The Peace of Augsburg, in fifteen fifty five, lets each German prince choose his own territory's faith.
N: The truce collapses into the Thirty Years' War.
N: By the time the Peace of Westphalia ends it, in sixteen forty eight, an estimated four to eight million people are dead. In parts of the Holy Roman Empire, roughly a third of the population.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of what happened, and they don't even agree on what the argument was about.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Lutheran Reformers | victor
N: The reformers call this a recovery, not a revolution. The church of fifteen seventeen had buried the gospel of grace under a trade in salvation, and Pope Leo the Tenth, a Medici who needed money to finish St. Peter's Basilica, had licensed the sale.
N: Martin Luther's claim was theological, not political. Salvation comes by faith alone, a free gift, and it cannot be purchased.
N: What made the protest unstoppable was the press, and Luther understood it before any reformer had. A printed page reaches a thousand towns at once and outlives its author.
N: He argued that every believer stood equal before God without need of a priestly caste, and that scripture alone, not popes or councils, held authority.
M: When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said Repent, he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance.
N: Worms, in this telling, is the birth of the modern conscience. Ordered to recant on pain of death, Luther refused, and bound himself to scripture over an emperor.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The Catholic Church | vanquished
N: From Rome, Luther was not a reformer. He was a heresiarch who tore the seamless robe of Christ, and the Church had answered dissenters before.
N: What was different in fifteen seventeen wasn't the doctrine. Much of it echoed older heresies. It was the machine. One friar in a provincial town could flood a continent with pamphlets faster than any bishop could refute them.
N: The Catholic case is that Luther's slogans, faith alone, scripture alone, were dangerous precisely because they were simple. If every plowman interprets scripture for himself, the result isn't liberty. It's a thousand contradictory sects, each certain it alone has the Word.
N: The pope's own bull put the accusation in a single image.
M: Arise, O Lord, and judge thy cause. A wild boar has invaded thy vineyard.
N: And Rome's proof is fifteen twenty five. Luther's own talk of freedom lit the fuse, and when peasants took him literally, he called for their massacre. The Church, meanwhile, reformed itself on its own terms, and rebuilt.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Radical Reformation and the Peasants | vanquished
N: The people who read Luther most literally were the ones he disowned. When he wrote that a Christian was a free lord subject to none, peasants ground down by rising rents heard a promise.
N: In fifteen twenty five they wrote it down themselves, in the Twelve Articles of the Peasants of Swabia, printed and circulated in twenty five thousand copies within two months. They asked that serfdom end, because Christ had redeemed all people with his blood.
M: It has been the custom hitherto for men to hold us as their own property, which is pitiable enough, considering that Christ has redeemed and purchased us all with the precious shedding of his blood.
N: At Frankenhausen, the radical preacher Thomas Muntzer led the army gathered under him.
M: On, on, on! Do not let the sword of the saints grow cold. Strike a cling-clang on the anvils of Nimrod.
N: Muntzer was captured, tortured, and beheaded. What this account insists on is that the reformation had two faces, and the one that demanded freedom in this life, from serfdom, from tithes, lost because it had no army.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | The Princes and Sovereignty | victor
N: For the rulers of roughly three hundred states inside the Holy Roman Empire, the Reformation was an opportunity. Luther's revolt handed princes a doctrine that let them seize the wealth and jurisdiction of the Church inside their own borders and call it piety.
N: Frederick the Wise never professed Luther's faith publicly, but protecting him meant more independence from both emperor and pope, and other rulers followed for a mix of conviction and calculation.
N: The struggle was settled by treaty, not theology. The Peace of Augsburg states its own terms plainly.
M: That the Estates adhering to the Augsburg Confession shall be left in the peaceable enjoyment of their religion, faith, and ceremonies.
N: A prince now decided his territory's religion. When that settlement collapsed into the Thirty Years' War, the Peace of Westphalia extended the same logic further. The princes didn't set out to invent sovereignty. They set out to keep their lands, and the Reformation gave them the means.

## PERSPECTIVE | Print and the Modern World | academic
N: Later scholarship asks a different question. Not why Luther's theology won, but why this protest, unlike a century of earlier ones, could not be contained.
N: Wycliffe and Hus made arguments close to Luther's, and both were suppressed. Luther survived because, by fifteen seventeen, decades of Gutenberg's press had already built an infrastructure of print shops and a reading public across German cities.
N: The historian Elizabeth Eisenstein argued that print didn't merely publicize the Reformation. It made a permanent schism possible for the first time, because once thousands of identical copies exist in private hands across many jurisdictions, no authority can recall them all.
F: A wholesale religious upheaval could not be launched or sustained without the new powers of the press.
N: The second order effects reshaped the modern world. To read the Bible yourself, you had to read, so Protestant regions pushed literacy. This account doesn't deny the faith. It just notices that the monk mattered, and the machine mattered more.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: And here is what each of them doesn't say.
N: The reformers leave out Luther's own endorsement of the princes' slaughter of the peasants, and his later, virulent writing against the Jews.
N: The Catholic account leaves out the scale of the corruption that made Luther's protest resonate in the first place, and the Church's own use of force against dissent.
N: The peasants' and radicals' account leaves out the violence and the millenarian extremism inside their own movement, at Munster among others.
N: The princes' account leaves out the believers who had no say at all in their ruler's choice of faith, and the war that made the treaties necessary.
N: And the historians' account leaves out the conviction. It explains why an idea could spread. It has nothing to say about why anyone thought it was true.
