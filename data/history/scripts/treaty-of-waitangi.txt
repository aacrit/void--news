## OPEN
N: Most museums that hold the Treaty of Waitangi keep two documents in the case, in two languages.
N: Ask which one nearly all the Maori chiefs who signed it actually read, and the honest answer is the version that never uses the Maori word for supreme authority.
N: The English text ceded the Crown all the rights and powers of sovereignty. The Maori text, translated overnight by a missionary, ceded only te kawanatanga katoa: governance.
N: Nearly every chief signed the Maori text. The two treaties do not say the same thing.

## TITLE
N: The Treaty of Waitangi and the New Zealand Wars. The sixth of February, eighteen forty, to eighteen seventy two.

## SCENE 1 | Waitangi, the sixth of February, eighteen forty
N: At the house of the British Resident, James Busby, in the Bay of Islands, about five hundred Maori chiefs have gathered. The Maori population is about eighty thousand. The European settler population is about two thousand.
N: Captain William Hobson, representing the Crown, has brought a document from London that he says will protect Maori rights while establishing British authority.
N: The missionary Henry Williams and his son Edward spent the night before translating it into Te Reo Maori.
N: By the end of this day, forty three chiefs sign. Over the months that follow, the treaty travels the country, and more than five hundred eventually sign it. Some refuse. Some are never asked.

## DOCUMENT | Treaty of Waitangi | English text, Article 2 | February 6, 1840
N: The Treaty of Waitangi's English text promised the Chiefs and Tribes of New Zealand this, in Article two.
M: Her Majesty the Queen of England confirms and guarantees to the Chiefs and Tribes of New Zealand the full exclusive and undisturbed possession of their Lands and Estates, Forests, Fisheries, and other properties.

## DOCUMENT | Te Tiriti o Waitangi | Maori text, Article 2 | February 6, 1840
N: Article two of Te Tiriti o Waitangi, the Maori text nearly every chief actually signed, promised something else.
M: Ko te Kuini o Ingarani ka wakarite ka wakaae ki nga Rangitira ki nga hapu, ki nga tangata katoa o Nu Tirani te tino rangatiratanga o o ratou wenua o ratou kainga me o ratou taonga katoa.

## ASIDE
N: Kawanatanga was not a Maori word. Henry Williams built it from the English word governor. Mana, the Maori word for supreme authority, already existed and was not used.
N: Rangatiratanga meant full chieftainship over land, forests, fisheries, and taonga, treasured possessions. The chiefs who signed believed they were granting a governor power over British settlers, not over their own people.
N: The British believed they had acquired sovereignty over the whole territory.

## REST

## SCENE 2 | The debate, the day before
N: The chiefs debate the document for five hours before anyone signs it.
N: Te Kemara of Ngapuhi rejects it outright: a free people, he says, who are not the governor's people.
N: Rewa of Ngapuhi warns that Maori and Pakeha cannot live together.
N: Tamati Waka Nene argues the opposite: sign, and gain a governor with power over the whalers, traders, and convicts already destabilizing the Bay of Islands, not power over Maori.

## SCENE 3 | Kororareka, eighteen forty four to eighteen forty five
N: Hone Heke, a Ngapuhi chief and one of the first to sign the treaty, is the first to declare it broken.
N: In July eighteen forty four, he cuts down the British flagpole at Kororareka, now Russell. The British re-erect it. He cuts it down again.
N: They raise it a third time, encased in iron, guarded by a blockhouse. On the eleventh of March, eighteen forty five, Heke cuts it down a fourth time, while his ally Kawiti attacks the town.

## DOCUMENT | Hone Heke | Statement to Tamati Waka Nene before the fourth cutting of the flagpole | March 1845
N: Heke told Tamati Waka Nene exactly what the flagpole meant to him.
M: What I object to is the flagstaff. It is the flagstaff that is the cause of the trouble.

## ASIDE
N: The flagpole stood for mana, not the kawanatanga Heke believed he had signed for. He was never captured, and never accepted that the treaty transferred sovereignty.
N: The Northern War that followed, the first of the New Zealand Wars, ran until eighteen forty six and ended with no clear winner.

## REST

## SCENE 4 | The Waikato, eighteen sixty three
N: Governor George Grey invades the Waikato with twelve thousand British and colonial troops, the largest campaign of the New Zealand Wars.
N: The target is the Kingitanga, the King Movement, formed in eighteen fifty eight when Maori leaders elected Potatau Te Wherowhero as the first Maori king, a pan tribal authority built to match the Crown's own claim.
N: The Crown calls it rebellion. The invasion confiscates one point two million acres of Kingitanga land.

## SCENE 5 | The Settlements Act, eighteen sixty three to eighteen seventy two
N: The New Zealand Settlements Act of eighteen sixty three lets the government seize the land of any tribe it judges to be in rebellion. It draws no line between the tribes that fought and the tribes that simply lived in the wrong district.
N: The Taranaki Wars and the campaigns against Te Kooti Arikirangi Te Turuki grind on with intervals for another nine years.
N: By eighteen seventy two, when the last campaign ends, the Crown has confiscated about three point four million acres of Maori land.

## REST

## TURN | Five accounts
N: Five accounts of the same thirty two years.

## PERSPECTIVE | Maori Chiefs / Iwi | indigenous
N: The chiefs at Waitangi were not naive. The Bay of Islands had been a trading hub for decades, and chiefs including Hone Heke and Tamati Waka Nene read and wrote in both Te Reo Maori and English.
N: Hongi Hika, another Ngapuhi chief, had already sailed to England in eighteen twenty and met King George the Fourth.
N: Chiefs who signed te Tiriti understood exactly what they were granting: kawanatanga, governance over British subjects, not mana, supreme authority over Maori.
N: Nopera Panakareao, signing at Kaitaia in April eighteen forty, put the reading precisely.
M: The shadow of the land will go to the Queen, but the substance will remain with us.
N: Read that way, the two articles describe power sharing, not surrender. The Kingitanga, and Hone Heke's four cuttings of the flagpole, were later assertions of that same reading.

## PERSPECTIVE | British Crown / Colonial Administration | victor
N: London's problem was not conscience alone. A private company was already shipping settlers to Wellington and buying land through agents at a fraction of its value, and the Colonial Office feared a settler war like the ones already consuming the Cape Colony and New South Wales.
N: Lord Normanby, the Colonial Secretary, set the standard in his instructions to Hobson.
M: All dealings with the Aborigines for their Lands must be conducted on the same principles of sincerity, justice, and good faith as must govern your transactions with them for any other purpose.
N: The Crown's own reading is direct: Article one cedes sovereignty, Article two protects property, and its pre-emption clause gives the government the sole right to buy Maori land, meant to keep speculators from underpaying chiefs directly.
N: Henry Williams, seventeen years among Maori and fluent in Te Reo, chose the closest words he had.
N: Governor George Grey enforced that reading with force, and also built native schools and collected Maori oral tradition. He saw no contradiction between the two.

## REST | short

## PERSPECTIVE | Settler / Pakeha | bystander
N: Most settlers came for farms, not conquest. The New Zealand Company advertised an orderly transplant of English society. The settlers who landed at Wellington in eighteen forty found contested land, unfinished surveys, and titles that might belong to somebody else.
N: They depended on Maori for wheat, potatoes, pigs, and flax, and competed with Maori for the one resource that mattered: land.
N: The Crown's pre-emption system frustrated settlers who wanted to buy land directly from willing Maori sellers.
N: An unnamed settler in New Plymouth put the experience in four words, in a letter that survives only because a historian quoted it in a nineteen fifty nine history of New Zealand.
M: We came to better ourselves, not to fight.
N: At Gate Pa in April eighteen sixty four, two hundred thirty Ngai Te Rangi warriors held a fortified pa against seventeen hundred British troops, inflicted over a hundred casualties, and slipped away undetected at night. Historian James Belich later showed that British accounts routinely called such defeats victories.

## PERSPECTIVE | Contemporary Treaty Jurisprudence / Waitangi Tribunal | revisionist
N: The Waitangi Tribunal, created in nineteen seventy five and given jurisdiction back to eighteen forty in nineteen eighty five, applies a rule from international law: where two language texts of a treaty diverge, the meaning that favors the Indigenous signatories prevails.
N: Its reports now number over one hundred. The Waikato Tainui report on the eighteen sixty three confiscation found the seizure disproportionate to any military threat, and aimed at land rather than security.
N: The nineteen ninety five settlement paid one hundred seventy million New Zealand dollars for one point two million confiscated acres, with a formal Crown apology.
N: Queen Elizabeth the Second signed that apology into law herself.
F: The Crown has not honoured the Treaty. We have failed in our duty to the Maori people.
N: Settlements since nineteen ninety two have transferred over two point two four billion New Zealand dollars in total.

## PERSPECTIVE | Colonial Settler / Pakeha Progressive | bystander
N: Not every Pakeha endorsed what the Crown did in their name.
N: Octavius Hadfield, an Anglican missionary on the Kapiti Coast, wrote to the Colonial Office in eighteen sixty to say the Taranaki land purchases fueling the wars were fraudulent.
M: The natives have been plundered of their land under forms of law devised for that purpose.
N: James Busby, who had helped draft the treaty himself, wrote to the governor protesting that Crown actions violated the very document he had helped create. His letters were filed and ignored.
N: Judge Fenton, the first Chief Judge of the Native Land Court, resigned in eighteen eighty two, concluding that the court he ran was an instrument of dispossession, not justice.
N: None of it stopped the confiscations. It left the documentary record the Waitangi Tribunal would use a century later to prove the Crown knew, and did it anyway.

## REST

## TURN | What each one leaves out
N: The Maori account is quieter about the intertribal warfare that predated Europeans, and about the chiefs who sold land willingly rather than lost it to confiscation.
N: The Crown's account leaves out that it was both the only legal buyer of Maori land and the regulator of that market at once, and that it confiscated three point four million acres, non combatant tribes included.
N: The settler account leaves out that settlers themselves pressured the Crown for more land, and gained directly from a Native Land Court built to make that land easier to sell.
N: The Tribunal's account is quieter about the Maori who sold land by choice, and about a backlash from Pakeha who call the settlements an unfair privilege.
N: The progressive account is honest that dissent existed. It is quieter about how rarely that dissent changed an outcome, and its own protection of Maori still stopped short of recognizing Maori sovereignty.

## CLOSE
N: The Waikato Tainui settlement paid, for the one point two million acres the Crown confiscated in eighteen sixty three, roughly fourteen cents an acre.
N: The original treaty, both texts, signed the same week in eighteen forty, is still held at Archives New Zealand in Wellington.
N: Two documents. Two languages. One case.
