## SCENE 1 | Twenty five seconds
# MOOD: rupture
N: July, nineteen sixty nine. The United States and the Soviet Union, the two superpowers of the Cold War, have raced each other into space since nineteen fifty seven, when the Soviets launched the first satellite.
N: The Soviets led that race for a decade. Now an American rocket has carried three men to the Moon: Neil Armstrong, the commander, Buzz Aldrin, the lunar module pilot, and Michael Collins, who will stay in orbit alone.
N: The twentieth of July. Two hundred and thirty nine thousand miles from Mission Control in Houston, Armstrong and Aldrin are falling toward the Moon in the lunar module, the landing craft they call Eagle, and its computer is complaining.
N: The alarm code is twelve oh two. It means the guidance computer is being asked to do more than it can do at once, and is dropping what matters least.
N: The automatic system is steering them toward a field of boulders. Armstrong takes the controls and flies the last of the descent by hand, looking for somewhere flat, while the fuel runs down.
N: They land with about twenty five seconds of fuel remaining.

## TURN | Five accounts
# MOOD: reckoning
N: What that was worth is where the accounts separate.
N: This disagreement is not about what happened. It is about whether it should have been done first.
N: Five accounts, each argued from what this programme could read: two speeches of Kennedy, the transcripts of the flight, the plaque, and one engineer's recollection. The books behind them could not be read here. Each gets its own case first.

## PERSPECTIVE | American triumph | victor
# MOOD: procedure
N: The first account is that a democracy did this in the open, and that the openness is the point.
N: It announced a deadline in nineteen sixty one, in public, and had to meet it in public: televised launches, public failures, a congressional inquiry into the Apollo one fire.
N: It did that without a command economy, by coordinating twenty thousand private firms toward one date.
N: The Saturn, the rocket that carried it, never lost a crew in flight.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Soviet space programme | vanquished
# MOOD: reckoning
N: The second account is Soviet, and it starts by pointing out that they were winning.
N: Sputnik, the first satellite, in October nineteen fifty seven, beeping over American cities every ninety six minutes. Then the first man in orbit, the first woman, the first spacewalk.
N: What they lost was not a race of engineers but one man. Sergei Korolev, the chief designer, died on the operating table in January nineteen sixty six; he alone had held the feuding design bureaus together.
N: Valentin Glushko, one of those feuding designers, refused to build engines for the N one, the giant rocket of the Soviet Moon effort, so it flew on thirty smaller ones, under analogue control.
N: It flew four times and failed every time, once falling back onto its own pad weeks before Apollo eleven.
N: Even in defeat the Soviets raced. Luna fifteen, an unmanned probe, launched three days ahead of Apollo eleven to bring lunar soil home first, and struck a mountainside hours before Eagle lifted off.
N: Afterwards the official line held that the Soviet Union had never intended to send people to the Moon. The N one was cancelled in nineteen seventy four, and its existence denied until the Soviet collapse.

## PERSPECTIVE | The domestic critique | revisionist
# MOOD: testimony
N: The third account was made at the launch gate itself, the day before liftoff.
N: On the fifteenth of July, Ralph Abernathy, a civil rights leader who had succeeded Martin Luther King, led several hundred marchers and four mule drawn wagons to the gates of Cape Kennedy, where the rocket stood.
N: Thomas Paine, the administrator of NASA, met him at the gate. Abernathy said his Poor People's Campaign had come not to stop the launch but to protest a country's distorted priorities, and asked its scientists to turn their genius on hunger and poverty.
N: Then he watched the rocket rise and, by his own account, prayed for the astronauts.
N: In nineteen seventy the poet Gil Scott-Heron recorded a spoken word piece that set a Black family's medical debt, rat bites and rising rent against the spectacle overhead. Its title is the whole argument: Whitey on the Moon.
N: The objection was about sequence, not science. The United States was fighting in Vietnam, cities had burned in nineteen sixty seven and again after King was killed, and the median Black family earned about sixty percent of what white families did.
N: The critics did not deny the achievement. They asked why it had been chosen first, and by whom, and who had paid for it while going without.

## PERSPECTIVE | The engineers | academic
# MOOD: reckoning
N: The fourth account is made in laboratories, and its claim is that the spending bought more than a flag.
N: The guidance computer ran on integrated circuits when the chip was a laboratory curiosity. By nineteen sixty three the space programme was buying a large share of all the chips made in the United States, and the price fell.
N: The line from Apollo runs through that purchase to the microprocessor.
N: The missions returned three hundred and eighty two kilograms of rock and soil, and their analysis produced the theory of how the Moon formed: a body the size of Mars striking the early Earth.
N: And the landing itself was saved by software design. Margaret Hamilton led the software for the guidance computer at the university laboratory that built it, and coined the term software engineering to force her discipline to be taken seriously.
N: She was in a monitoring room there when the alarms came. Her software had been written to triage under overload: near its limit the computer cleared its queue and ran only the highest priority tasks until the landing was complete.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Global South | revisionist
# MOOD: grief
N: The fifth account came from what is called the Global South, the nations of Africa, Asia and Latin America, much of it independent for less than a decade.
N: India, Nigeria, Indonesia and dozens of other states were building their first universities, fighting famine, and choosing sides in a Cold War not of their making.
N: In this view the money both powers spent above the planet was misallocation on a global scale.
N: In nineteen seventy, Sister Mary Jucunda, a nun in Zambia, wrote to a NASA scientist asking how he could justify spending billions on a voyage to Mars while children on Earth were starving.
N: Ernst Stuhlinger, the scientist, replied in print: basic research had always, indirectly, fed the hungry by advancing the tools of civilization. Both were sincere, which is what makes it the hardest version of the question.
N: The deeper objection was about authorship. The plaque said for all mankind, and mankind had not been consulted, had not funded the effort, and had not shared in its direction.
N: For much of the Global South, Apollo was neither villain nor miracle but evidence: the resources to end poverty existed, and this is what they were spent on.

## TURN | The shape of the disagreement
# MOOD: reckoning
N: Each of those five is true, and each leaves something out.
N: The triumph account leaves out the poverty and the civil rights struggle in the same month, and Wernher von Braun, who designed the Saturn and had built the V two rocket for the Nazi state with slave labour.
N: The Soviet account leaves out the scale of the effort that beat them, and two decades spent concealing that the N one had failed at all.
N: The domestic critique leaves out the jobs the programme created, including for Black engineers and mathematicians, and the spinoffs that later reached ordinary people.
N: The account of the engineers leaves out that Cold War prestige paid for the science, and that crewed flights to the Moon stopped after nineteen seventy two.
N: The Global South account leaves out the inspiration Apollo gave to the programmes India and Brazil later built, and the samples shared openly.
N: The holes are not random. Each account omits the part that would most complicate its own conclusion.

# MOOD: reckoning

## CLOSE
# MOOD: grief
N: Two Americans, Armstrong and Aldrin, landed on the Moon on the twentieth of July, nineteen sixty nine, eight years after a president asked Congress to pay for it; Collins waited in orbit.
N: The Soviet Union, which had led the Space Race since nineteen fifty seven, came second. The last people left the Moon in December nineteen seventy two.
N: What stayed was less obvious than a flag. The integrated circuit found its first buyer at scale, and the industry that grew from it is the one you are listening on.
N: And there was a photograph: the Earth seen from lunar distance, a blue marble in black vacuum, which became the emblem of the environmental movement founded in the same decade.
N: The largest peacetime engineering project ever attempted is best remembered for what it showed when it looked back.
N: Eight years, four hundred thousand people, and twenty five seconds of fuel.
