## SCENE 1 | Twenty five seconds
# MOOD: rupture
N: The twentieth of July. Two hundred and thirty nine thousand miles from Houston, a machine of aluminium and foil is falling toward a plain of basalt, and its computer is complaining.
N: The alarm code is twelve oh two. It means the guidance computer is being asked to do more than it can do at once, and is dropping what matters least.
N: The automatic system is steering them toward a field of boulders. Neil Armstrong takes the controls and flies the last of the descent by hand, looking for somewhere flat, while the fuel runs down.
N: They land with about twenty five seconds of fuel remaining.

## TURN | Five accounts
# MOOD: reckoning
N: What that was worth is where the accounts separate.
N: This disagreement is not about what happened. Everyone agrees what happened. It is about whether it should have been done first.
N: Five accounts, each given its own case first.

## PERSPECTIVE | American triumph | victor
# MOOD: procedure
N: The first account is that a democracy did this in the open, and that the openness is the point.
N: The United States announced a deadline in nineteen sixty one, in public, and then had to meet it in public: televised launches, public failures, a congressional inquiry into the Apollo one fire.
N: It did that without a command economy, by coordinating twenty thousand private firms toward one date.
N: The Saturn rocket that carried it never lost a crew in flight.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Soviet space programme | vanquished
# MOOD: reckoning
N: The second account is Soviet, and it starts by pointing out that they were winning.
N: Sputnik, in October nineteen fifty seven: a hundred and eighty four pounds, beeping over American cities every ninety six minutes. Then the first animal in orbit, the first man, the first woman, the first spacewalk. Each first was announced only after it succeeded.
N: What they lost was not a race of engineers but one man. Sergei Korolev, the chief designer, died on the operating table in January nineteen sixty six, aged fifty nine, and he alone had held the feuding design bureaus together.
N: Valentin Glushko refused to build engines for the giant N one rocket, so it flew on thirty smaller ones, under analogue control. It flew four times and failed every time, once falling back onto its own pad weeks before Apollo eleven.
N: Even in defeat the Soviets raced. Luna fifteen, an unmanned probe, launched three days ahead of Apollo eleven to bring lunar soil home first, and struck a mountainside in the Sea of Crises hours before Eagle lifted off.
N: Afterwards the official line held that the Soviet Union had never intended to send people to the Moon at all. The N one was cancelled in nineteen seventy four, and its existence denied until the Soviet collapse.

## PERSPECTIVE | The domestic critique | revisionist
# MOOD: testimony
N: The third account was made at the launch gate itself, the day before liftoff.
N: On the fifteenth of July, Ralph Abernathy, who had succeeded Martin Luther King at the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led several hundred marchers and four mule drawn wagons to the gates of Cape Kennedy.
N: The NASA administrator, Thomas Paine, met him at the gate. Abernathy said his Poor People's Campaign had come not to stop the launch but to protest a country's distorted priorities, and asked the scientists of NASA to turn their genius on hunger and poverty.
N: Then he watched the rocket rise and, by his own account, prayed for the astronauts.
N: In nineteen seventy the poet Gil Scott-Heron recorded a spoken word piece that set a Black family's medical debt, rat bites and rising rent against the spectacle overhead. Its title is the whole argument: Whitey on the Moon.
N: The objection was about sequence, not science. The United States was fighting in Vietnam, cities had burned in nineteen sixty seven and again after King was killed, and the median Black family earned about sixty percent of what white families did.
N: The critics did not deny the achievement. They asked why it had been chosen first, and by whom, and who had paid for it while going without.

## PERSPECTIVE | The engineers | academic
# MOOD: reckoning
N: The fourth account is the one that gets made in laboratories, and its claim is that the spending bought more than a flag.
N: The guidance computer ran on integrated circuits when the chip was a laboratory curiosity. By nineteen sixty three the space programme was buying a large share of all the integrated circuits made in the United States, and the price fell.
N: The line from Apollo runs through that purchase to the microprocessor.
N: The Apollo missions returned three hundred and eighty two kilograms of rock and soil, and the analysis produced the theory of how the Moon formed: a body about the size of Mars striking the early Earth.
N: And the landing itself was saved by software design. Margaret Hamilton was in a monitoring room at the laboratory when the alarms interrupted the normal displays.
N: Her software had been written to triage under overload. Each time the computer neared its limit it cleared its queue, restarted, and ran only the highest priority tasks until the landing was complete.

## PERSPECTIVE | The Global South | revisionist
# MOOD: grief
N: The fifth account came from outside both superpowers, from nations independent for less than a decade. India, Nigeria, Indonesia and dozens of other states were building their first universities, fighting famine, and choosing sides in a Cold War not of their making.
N: In this view the money both powers spent above the planet was misallocation on a global scale.
N: It had dissenters within the Global South itself. India and Brazil would later build space programmes framed around weather, communication and development rather than prestige.
N: The tension was captured in a letter. In nineteen seventy, Sister Mary Jucunda, a nun in Zambia, wrote to a NASA scientist asking how he could justify spending billions on a voyage to Mars while children on Earth were starving.
N: Ernst Stuhlinger published a reply arguing that basic research had always, indirectly, fed the hungry by advancing the tools of civilization. Both of them were arguing in good faith, and that is what makes it the hardest version of the question.
N: The deeper objection was about authorship. The plaque said for all mankind, and mankind had not been consulted, had not funded the effort, and had not shared in its direction.
N: For much of the Global South, Apollo was neither villain nor miracle but evidence: the resources to end poverty existed, and this is what they were spent on.

## TURN | The shape of the disagreement
# MOOD: reckoning
N: Each of those five is true, and each leaves something out.
N: The triumph account leaves out the poverty and the civil rights struggle in the same month. It leaves out Wernher von Braun, who designed the rocket, and who had built the V two for the Nazi state with slave labour.
N: The Soviet account leaves out the scale and the consistency of the effort that beat them, and the two decades spent concealing that the N one had failed at all.
N: The domestic critique leaves out the jobs the programme created, including for Black engineers and mathematicians, and the spinoffs that later reached ordinary people.
N: The engineers' account leaves out that Cold War prestige is what paid for the science, and that the last crew left the Moon in December nineteen seventy two.
N: The Global South account leaves out the inspiration Apollo gave to the programmes India and Brazil later built, and the samples shared openly.
N: The holes are not random. Each account omits the part that would most complicate its own conclusion.
