## TITLE
# MOOD: dread
N: The Cuban Missile Crisis. October, nineteen sixty two.

## SCENE 1 | The photographs
# MOOD: procedure
N: October, nineteen sixty two. The United States and the Soviet Union, the two superpowers of the Cold War, each hold nuclear weapons that can reach the other.
N: Cuba is an island ninety miles by sea from the United States, allied to Moscow and led by Fidel Castro, its Prime Minister.
N: On the sixteenth of October, in Washington, President John F. Kennedy is shown photographs from a U two, a reconnaissance plane: Soviet medium range missile sites being built on the island.
N: Once built, the missiles could reach Washington. In September the Soviet government had said in public that its arms in Cuba were for defence only.
N: For six days the President meets in secret with advisers the record calls ExComm, the Executive Committee of the National Security Council, choosing between an air strike and a blockade.

## DOCUMENT | Robert F. Kennedy | the Attorney General's objection to an air strike, as Robert McNamara's notes of the meeting record it | October 21, 1962
# extract: src-frus11-d36.secitem9
# MOOD: procedure
N: On the twenty first, the President asks his brother Robert Kennedy, the Attorney General, about a strike. Robert McNamara, the Secretary of Defense, wrote down the answer.
M: It would be a Pearl Harbor type of attack.
N: He said to start with the blockade. The next day the President gave his own reasons: a strike could not be certain of every missile, and would end in invasion.

## SCENE 3 | The line
# MOOD: procedure
N: The twenty fourth of October, at sea. The quarantine takes effect at the hour the Proclamation names, and the Soviet ships carrying missiles never reach it.
N: Aides later claimed the navies met eyeball to eyeball; the records put the missile ships at least five hundred nautical miles off, turned for home.

M: You, Mr. President, are not declaring a quarantine, but rather are setting forth an ultimatum and threatening that if we do not give in to your demands you will use force.

N: The summary the Library wrote for the day says he urged a nuclear first strike. The letter names no Soviet act, and Castro said years later he meant the Americans must not strike first.

## SCENE 4 | Saturday
# MOOD: rupture
N: Saturday, the twenty seventh of October, Washington. Moscow radio broadcasts a new message from Khrushchev, with a condition the earlier letter did not carry: the American missiles in Turkey.
N: Turkey borders the Soviet Union, and the United States keeps missiles there called Jupiters, placed by a decision of the NATO Council, the alliance it leads.
N: Kennedy tells the morning meeting the Jupiters are obsolete, and that he is now risking war over them.

## DOCUMENT | Nikita Khrushchev | Message to President Kennedy, Moscow, published, in the Department of State's translation | October 27, 1962
# extract: src-frus11-d91.secturkey
# MOOD: reckoning
N: Khrushchev's public message set the two countries side by side.
M: You are disturbed over Cuba. You say that this disturbs you because it is 90 miles by sea from the coast of the United States of America. But Turkey adjoins us; our sentries patrol back and forth and see each other.

## SCENE 5 | The afternoon
# MOOD: dread
N: Four in the afternoon, the same Saturday, Washington. The meeting is told a U two has been shot down over Cuba and its pilot killed: Major Rudolf Anderson, the one death of the crisis.
N: McNamara said invasion had become almost inevitable. Who ordered the shot is argued: Castro said later a Soviet missile commander most probably decided it; Mark Kramer, studying Soviet nuclear operations, writes that Moscow thought Castro had ordered it.
N: The same afternoon, at the quarantine line, American destroyers drop signalling depth charges, the size of hand grenades, on a Soviet submarine, B fifty nine, to make it surface.
N: The Navy did not know it carried a nuclear torpedo, usable under its orders if the hull was holed, with a yield of fifteen kilotons, about the Hiroshima bomb.
N: What happened inside is one account. Vadim Orlov, a signals officer aboard, recalled the captain, Valentin Savitsky, out of contact with Moscow, ordering the torpedo readied, and Vasili Arkhipov, the deputy brigade commander, calming him until they surfaced.
N: The Archive that published the account calls it controversial; the other submarine commanders do not believe Savitsky gave the order. B fifty nine surfaced on the twenty seventh.
N: Thirteen days. One man killed. The rest is what did not happen.

## TURN | Five accounts
# MOOD: reckoning
N: Whether anyone won is where the accounts part.
N: Five accounts, argued from what this programme could read: the letters between the three governments, the American minutes, one ambassador's cables, Castro's broadcasts, and the documents the National Security Archive published at the fortieth anniversary. The memoirs could not be read here.

## PERSPECTIVE | American | victor
# MOOD: procedure
N: The first account is American, and it is about restraint under pressure.
N: The President was advised to strike and chose to stop ships instead, in public, so the Soviet leader could answer in public too.
N: The Soviet Union had placed the missiles in secret after assuring Washington in September that its arms were defensive; Kennedy read that assurance aloud and called it false.
N: At the United Nations on the twenty fifth, Adlai Stevenson, the American ambassador, asked the Soviet representative whether the missiles existed, yes or no, and said he would wait for his answer until Hell froze over.

## PERSPECTIVE | Soviet | bystander
# MOOD: reckoning
N: The second account is Soviet, and it begins in Turkey.
N: American missiles stood on the Soviet border before any Soviet missile reached Cuba.
N: In this account the weapons were defensive: the island had been invaded once already, at Playa Giron the year before, and Khrushchev's letter of the twenty third affirmed the arms were solely for defence.
N: The outcome was an exchange: the missiles left Cuba, the Americans pledged not to invade, and the Jupiters left Turkey the following spring.

## PERSPECTIVE | Cuban | vanquished
# MOOD: testimony
N: The third account is Cuban, and in Cuba the event has another name: the October Crisis.

## DOCUMENT | Fidel Castro | Television and radio address, Havana, in the Foreign Broadcast Information Service's translation | October 23, 1962
# extract: src-fbis-castro-19621023.secoffensive
# MOOD: testimony
N: On the twenty third, Castro reminded his listeners which rifles had been landed at Playa Giron, and which rifles had met them.
M: The thing that determines the offensive or defensive character of the weapons is not their shape but their use, their employment.
N: Cuba sat at no table. In November Castro told Anastas Mikoyan, for the Soviet side, that Cuba had learned of Turkey from Soviet radio; the Soviet side recorded the conversation.

## PERSPECTIVE | Nuclear Deterrence Theory | academic
# MOOD: reckoning
N: The fourth account belongs to the theorists of deterrence, whose books this programme could not read; their position is narrated from the event record.
N: In that account the crisis is the founding case: neither leader controlled every variable, and fear of an accident moved both to retreat.
N: The twenty seventh is its evidence: a missile fired on an order Moscow had not given, a torpedo whose captain could not reach Moscow, five hundred sorties prepared for a strike the President had not ordered.

N: The doctrine itself was stated on the twenty second, in the third step. The recording of Kennedy, once more.
M: Third: It shall be the policy of this Nation to regard any nuclear missile launched from Cuba against any nation in the Western Hemisphere as an attack by the Soviet Union on the United States, requiring a full retaliatory response upon the Soviet Union.

## PERSPECTIVE | Declassified Revisionist | revisionist
# MOOD: reckoning
N: The fifth account is built from documents declassified since, and it holds that the crisis was closer and longer than thirteen days.
N: American intelligence never located the nuclear warheads, and found only thirty three of the medium range missiles that later photography showed.
N: Tactical nuclear weapons, for use against an invasion force, were on the island too. General Anatoly Gribkov, who planned the deployment, revealed them at an earlier Havana conference; documents released in two thousand and two show they stayed into late November.
N: The Archive's essay and its chronology count those warheads differently, so this programme reads no total aloud.
N: Its second finding is Turkey: a timetable in Dobrynin's cable, a denial in Robert Kennedy's memorandum.

## TURN | The shape of the disagreement
# MOOD: reckoning
N: Each of the five is true, and each leaves something out.
N: The American account leaves out Turkey, and the crossed-out sentence.
N: The Soviet account leaves out the September assurance Kennedy called false, and the ally it told by radio.
N: The Cuban account leaves out the letter of the twenty sixth, and the warheads on the island that Cuba did not control.
N: The theorists' account leaves out that both arsenals grew after the crisis.
N: The documentary account leaves out the letters between Havana and Moscow that could not be read here.

N: Thirteen days. One man killed. The rest is what did not happen.
