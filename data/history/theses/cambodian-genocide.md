## The question

::: episode chapter=0

The tribunal that tried the regime's surviving leaders wrote that the estimates of the dead “range from 600,000 to three million” and that “Experts accept estimates falling between 1.5 and two million excess deaths as the most probable”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para297] The same court, in its first case, found that “no fewer than 12,273 individuals were detained at S-21”.[^src-eccc-001-tj para141] Before the tribunal held in Phnom Penh after the regime fell, the prosecution charged that “about 3 million innocent people have been killed”.[^src-prt-1979 secprosecution] The United Nations' experts, asked whether the regime committed genocide against its own majority, wrote that the question “turns on complex interpretive issues” and took no position.[^src-un-goe-1999 para65]

This thesis asks how many died, who counts as a victim of the genocide, and what the free record settles about the prison whose registers the court read. It makes three claims. First, that every count of the dead in the record lies between 600,000 and 3,000,000, that the ceiling coincides across producers and the floor is the court's, and that the counts are stated here with their producers and never as one number. Second, that the court found no fewer than 12,273 detained and no fewer than 12,272 executed at S-21, that 4,492 of those entries record neither a sex nor an age, and that the commandant's own figure was above 12,300. Third, that the word genocide was given three answers by three producers, the whole people, then three groups and other minorities with the majority left open, then two minorities, and that the court's own judge wrote that the narrow charge was never explained.

## The record

Every primary document this thesis holds was fetched from the court in Phnom Penh, from the ICC Legal Tools Database where the court's own copy of its longest judgment answered a missing page, from the United Nations document system, from the Documentation Center of Cambodia and from the Internet Archive, and every passage below was sliced from that copy rather than retyped.{i} The judgments, the transcripts and the resolutions are held with a paragraph or a page number; the scholarship is held only as the passages cited; the court's summary of its second judgment, the one Khmer court text fetched with a text layer, is held as that layer prints it beside the court's own English.{i} What the record does not hold is listed under the gaps, by name, with what was tried; the largest gaps are the regime's own words, which reach this page only through a Council record and an interview, the court's demographic report, whose scanned pages carry no text, and the standard monographs, which sit in controlled lending.{i} The court's own statement of the limits of its count comes first.{i} It found that “the absence of relevant and reliable statistical data for the purposes of assessing a precise number of deaths attributable to the CPK leads to inherent uncertainty surrounding the use of demographic evidence”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para297]

## The argument

### 1. The count

The court's survey of the estimates lists them from Vickery's 740,800 in 1984 and Ponchaud's 800,000 in 1986, through the “1.4 million” the CIA was estimating and Heuveline's “1.5 to 2 million”, to the ceiling Chandler gave the court as the consensus, “between 1.5 and 3 million”.[^src-eccc-002-01-tj fn523] The court's demographers, as the same footnote reports them, found that “earlier estimates heavily underestimated the scale of actual killing by the Khmer Rouge”.[^src-eccc-002-01-tj fn523] The court concluded that “between 600,000 and 3 million died as a result of Khmer Rouge policies and actions” and that experts accept “between 1.5 and 2 million excess deaths as the most accurate”.[^src-eccc-002-01-tj para174] The report the footnote relies on was fetched in this session and could not be read: its pages are images with no text layer.{i}

The Phnom Penh tribunal counted differently.{i} Its Act of Prosecution put the dead at “about 3 million innocent people”, and its foreword, in the later edition, at “more than three million”.[^src-prt-1979 secprosecution][^src-prt-1979 secforeword] The Act names its method: “a simple survey of the population formerly living in Phnom Penh and at a commune in Siemreap province” and “data collected on the spot by Australian journalist W. Burchett in three provinces”.[^src-prt-1979 secconsequences] The Soviet delegate had told the Security Council, seven months before the tribunal sat, that the rulers had destroyed, “according to statistics reported in, among others, the Western press, from 2 to 3 million people” in “a country with a population of 8 million”.[^src-un-spv2108 para149] The United Nations' experts wrote that scholars had “separately arrived at figures of 1.5 million and nearly 1.7 million”, that the estimates point to “approximately 20 per cent of the April 1975 population of 7.3 to 7.9 million people”, and that historians of Cambodia have “rejected the figure of 2 to 3 million” used by the governments in Cambodia since the regime fell.[^src-un-goe-1999 para35]

::: exhibit ex-prt-foreword

The commandant of S-21 told the court that he was “appalled by the fact that more than 1 million Cambodians lost their lives” and “morally responsible for the loss of more than one million lives, now and forever”.[^src-eccc-duch-apology p7] The demographer whose earlier reconstruction the court cites published a second, which turned the count into a distribution: a “95-percent simulation interval (1.2 to 2.8 million excess deaths)” with a median of “1.9 million excess deaths”, which “represents 21 percent of the population at risk”.[^src-heuveline-2015 abstract] By 2008 the Documentation Center of Cambodia “had identified an estimated 1.3 million human remains in 390 mass grave sites”, as the court records it, and the Center's own mapping page counts “19,733 mass burial pits, 196 prisons that operated during the Democratic Kampuchea (DK) period, and 81 memorials”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para297][^src-dccam-mapping whole]

### 2. The prison

::: episode chapter=3

The list the court read is the prison's own.{i} The revised list was “a collation by the Office of the Co-Prosecutors of previous lists compiled by DC-Cam based on the original prisoner lists and execution logs of S-21”, with 107 duplicate entries removed.[^src-eccc-001-tj fn240] It “contains 5,994 entries as men, 1,698 as women and 89 as children”; “5,609 entries are members of the RAK and 4,371 are DK cadres, while 1,751 are neither”; it describes “279 as either teachers, professors, students, doctors, lawyers or engineers” and “345 as Vietnamese, of whom 122 are described as Vietnamese soldiers, 144 as Vietnamese spies while for the remaining 79, who were presumably civilians, no description is provided”.[^src-eccc-001-tj para141] The court added that “Certain of the entries on the Revised S-21 Prisoner List contain minimal information”.[^src-eccc-001-tj para141]

::: exhibit ex-e188-141

::: analysis an-s21-list

The Vietnamese on the list were, the court found, “protected persons under the Geneva Conventions of 1949”, and the accused “stated that “spies” were classified as such on order of his superiors but were in fact either civilians or combatants”.[^src-eccc-001-tj para425] In its second judgment the court found that “Until late 1976, the Vietnamese were targeted for expulsion, from April 1977, for destruction as such”.[^src-eccc-002-02-summary para32]

::: analysis an-vietnamese-share

The court found the accused responsible for a system “that resulted in the execution of no fewer than 12,272 victims, the majority of whom were also systematically tortured”, and that “Only a very small number of those detained at S-21 survived”.[^src-eccc-001-tj para597][^src-eccc-001-tj para598] On appeal the Supreme Court Chamber wrote that he “commanded and operated this factory of death for more than three years” and “mercilessly terminated the lives of at least 12,272 individuals, including women and children”.[^src-eccc-001-aj para380] The accused's own count, given at trial, was of “that picture of me, I who killed more than 12,300 people”.[^src-eccc-duch-apology p7]

::: exhibit ex-s21-cellblock

Two men who left the prison alive testified before the court.{i} The painter Vann Nath told the court that “after we arrived into a room we were each photographed”, that “There were 20 or 30 of us in each row of those shackles”, and that the prisoners were given shorts “with nothing to tighten the waist because probably they were afraid that maybe we would take the string from the waist tightening to hang ourselves to commit suicide”.[^src-eccc-tr-20090629 p20] He was kept to paint: “Of course, the colour portrait could save my life. If I made the portrait attractive, then the Angkar would be pleased with the painting; then I would be spared from being executed.”[^src-eccc-tr-20090629 p26] On the last morning, he recalled, “there were woodcrafters, electricians, blacksmiths and there were about 13 of us”, and “there were no other prisoners remained in the prison”.[^src-eccc-tr-20090629 p52] His two children, “The eldest was five and the youngest was six months old”, had been left at the cooperative: “After I was freed and after I returned I learned that my children died. Only my wife survived”.[^src-eccc-tr-20090629 p53]

Chum Mey, who told the court that “Before 1975, I was a mechanic fixing tractors, cars”, spoke of the evacuation, on which “my son died. Where upon then I borrowed a hoe and then I dug the ground and buried my son. He was two years old at the time.”[^src-eccc-tr-20090630 p3][^src-eccc-tr-20090630 p6] Of the interrogation he said: “I put my hands up to protect myself, then it broke my fingers. And one day they removed my nails from the toes and I was trembling in pain. And later on, about 10 days later, I was electrocuted.”[^src-eccc-tr-20090630 p11] He named the men who beat him and said that “after my research I found out that three of them already died”.[^src-eccc-tr-20090630 p12] The court's later judgment records the same account as a finding, that he was “beaten up for 12 days and nights”, “electrocuted twice until he lost consciousness”, and that “two of his toenails were removed”, and it records his words: “I was treated like an animal, like a dog. A dog would be given steamed rice to eat, but for me, I was given only watery gruel.”[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para4447] The same judgment tested his survival against the list another survivor gave the 1979 tribunal and against the photographs, noted that he was on neither, and found “that there is nothing to indicate that UNG Pech exhaustively listed or could recollect the names of all survivors”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para2083]

::: exhibit ex-survivors-2012

### 3. The word

The law that made the court gave it “the power to bring to trial all Suspects who committed the crimes of genocide as defined in the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide of 1948”, acts “committed with the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group”.[^src-eccc-law-2004 art4] The agreement with the United Nations gave the same definition as the court's subject-matter jurisdiction.[^src-un-rgc-agreement-2003 art9] The tribunal of 1979 had used another: “Planned mass killing of innocent people, forced evacuation of the population from cities and villages, concentration of the population and forcing them to work in physically and morally exhausting conditions, abolition of religion”, and the destruction of economic, cultural, family and social structures.[^src-prt-1979 secjudgment] Under that definition it found the acts it laid to the clique to be the crime of genocide.{i}

The United Nations' experts wrote that the evidence “suggests the need for prosecutors to investigate the commission of genocide against the Cham, Vietnamese and other minority groups, and the Buddhist monkhood”, and that these “qualify as protected groups under the Convention”.[^src-un-goe-1999 para63] Of the majority they wrote that “The Khmer people of Cambodia do constitute a national group within the meaning of the Convention”, but that whether the regime committed genocide against part of it “turns on complex interpretive issues, especially concerning the Khmer Rouge’s intent with respect to its non-minority-group victims”, and that “The Group does not take a position on this issue”.[^src-un-goe-1999 para65]

::: exhibit ex-goe-65

The court answered for two groups.{i} It found that the party “targeted the Cham as an ethnic and religious distinct group throughout the DK period”, and that “A final shift occurred between 1977 and 1978, when purges of all Cham were ordered”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para3228] It found that the policy “had as its primary objective their physical destruction as an ethnic and religious group, as such”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para3993] Its summary, issued in Khmer and in English, entered “the crime of genocide and the crimes against humanity of murder, extermination, imprisonment, torture, persecution on political and religious grounds” with respect to the Cham, and “the crime of genocide and the crimes against humanity of murder, extermination, deportation and persecution on racial grounds” regarding the Vietnamese.[^src-eccc-002-02-summary-km para30][^src-eccc-002-02-summary-km para34] Nuon Chea was convicted “under the doctrine of superior responsibility for the crime of genocide by killing members of the Cham ethnic and religious group”; Khieu Samphan was found “not responsible for the crime of genocide by killing members of the Cham ethnic and religious group, neither as aider or abettor nor as superior”.[^src-eccc-002-02-summary para53][^src-eccc-002-02-summary para62]

The court's own judge dissented from the question, not the answer.{i} Judge You Ottara wrote in a separate opinion that “this case focused exclusively on charges of genocide by killing Vietnamese and Cham”, and that “the reasons for such a narrow focus have not been explained”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para4468] He recorded that the civil parties had asked for the charge to be examined “specifically in relation to the Khmer national group”, that the investigating judges rejected the request, and that the Closing Order “does not explain the restrictive approach to genocide which the Co-Investigating Judges adopted”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para4468] The approach was, in his view, “much too narrow”, and the court “could have considered, for example, whether persons who were targeted for destruction were essential to the survival of the Cambodian national group as it existed in 1975”.[^src-eccc-002-02-tj para4469]

::: analysis an-genocide-answers

### 4. The war

Two accounts of the war that ended the regime were given to the Security Council in the same sitting, and the record holds both.{i} For Democratic Kampuchea, Prince Sihanouk said that “my country is the victim of a large-scale act of flagrant aggression by the Socialist Republic of Viet Nam, a country which had described itself as our “brother””.[^src-un-spv2108 para73] China's delegate said that “with Soviet support, Viet Nam has carried out a large-scale naked armed aggression against Democratic Kampuchea”.[^src-un-spv2108 para18] The United States' delegate said that “We make no judgements one way or the other. We just think that that Government has a right to be heard.”[^src-un-spv2108 para59] For Viet Nam, Ha Van Lau drew “a clear distinction between two wars: one, the border war started by the Pol Pot-Ieng Sary clique against Viet Nam”, the other “the revolutionary war of the Kampuchean people against the dictatorial rule of the Pol Pot-Ieng Sary clique”.[^src-un-spv2108 para115] He said that of the 23 divisions the Kampuchean army then had, the clique “massed 19 on the border between Viet Nam and Kampuchea”.[^src-un-spv2108 para120]

The experts wrote that the two states engaged in a low-intensity border war from 1975 to 1977, that “By 1977, Cambodia had escalated the conflict to include raids in which it massacred hundreds of Vietnamese in border villages”, and that “Viet Nam eventually responded by sending troops into Cambodia in December 1977”.[^src-un-goe-1999 para37] The court dated “the ongoing international armed conflict between Vietnam and Democratic Kampuchea from May 1975”.[^src-eccc-002-02-summary para34]

The leadership's account is held in its own words.{i} Asked about the toll in the interview, Pol Pot said: “To say that millions died is too much. Another aspect you have to know is that Vietnamese agents, they were there. There was rice, but they didn't give rice to the population”.[^src-thayer-1997 p4] Of the prison he said that it was a “Vietnamese exhibition”; of the war he said: “We had no other choice. Naturally we had to defend ourselves”.[^src-thayer-1997 p4][^src-thayer-1997 p3] The one figure the interview gives for the dead is the reporter's, not the leader's, and this thesis does not carry it.{i} The scholarship describes that account as a frame: the regime “framed their regime as essential for the Cambodian people’s survival, while stoking fears of internal and external “enemies of the revolution.””[^src-williams-jessee-2024 p11] The same authors put the dead at 1.7 to “2.2 million deceased victims over approximately four years”, and count the prosecutions as reaching very few of the regime's high officials.[^src-williams-jessee-2024 p4] The Melbourne case note records that the Supreme Court Chamber “affirmed the former Head of State Khieu Samphan’s conviction for the crime of genocide perpetrated against Cambodia’s ethnic Vietnamese population”, and that the Trial Chamber had “found Khieu Samphan guilty of genocide against the Vietnamese but not the Cham”.[^src-sperfeldt-2023 p118] It reports that many of the ethnic Vietnamese in Cambodia “are stateless or at risk of statelessness”, with “75,000 individuals falling under the organisation’s statelessness mandate”.[^src-sperfeldt-2023 sec2] On the regime's treatment of them it reports that the regime “expelled an estimated 150,000 to 170,000 Vietnamese across the border to Vietnam” and that “A demographic expert report concluded that 100% of the remaining 20,000 Vietnamese were systematically killed”; those two counts are the demographic report's, which this record could not read.[^src-sperfeldt-2023 p120] The demographer's own conclusion is that “the uncertainty surrounding PPR death toll estimates is substantial”.[^src-heuveline-2015 secdiscussion] The revisionist count is known here only as the court reports it: “M. VICKERY estimated that 740,800 deaths were caused by the special conditions during the DK”.[^src-eccc-002-01-tj fn523]

## What the record omits

- No source in this record is the regime's own: not the party's statutes, not the prison's confessions in their own hand, not the speech of September 1977, which reached only a host the standard excludes. {against=khmer-rouge-account}
- No source in this record is a Cham survivor in his or her own words; the Cham are known here through the court's findings and the witnesses the 1979 tribunal called. {against=race-genocide-scholarship}
- No source in this record is by an ethnic Vietnamese survivor of Cambodia, and none is in Vietnamese; Viet Nam is known here through its delegate's statement to the Council. {against=vietnam-prk-account}
- No woman who survived the regime speaks in this record in her own words; the two survivors heard here are men, and the women on the prison's list are known only as entries. {against=survivors}
- No source in this record is a base person, a peasant who lived in the cooperatives before 1975; both survivors who testify here were arrested as enemies and speak from inside the prison. {against=survivors}
- No source in this record is the court's demographic report itself; its figures are known here as the court's footnote reports them. {against=demographers}
- No source in this record is the text of the Closing Order that confined the genocide charge to two groups; its reasoning is known here as the dissenting judge describes its absence. {against=tribunal-record}
- No source in this record is a Chinese or a Thai voice, though the scholarship's death rates reach both groups. {against=united-nations}
