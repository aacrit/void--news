## The question

::: episode chapter=0

On 29 August 1793 the civil commissioner of the Republic proclaimed at the Cap that all the negroes and people of mixed blood then in slavery were “déclarés libres pour jouir de tous les droits attachés à la qualité de citoyen”.[^src-sonthonax-17930829 art2][^src-sonthonax-17930829 secsign] On 13 November 1801 the First Consul wrote to his foreign minister of “le parti que j'ai pris d'anéantir à Saint-Domingue le gouvernement des noirs”.[^src-napoleon-corr-7 item5863] On 1 January 1804 the general in chief of the native army and his generals swore at Gonaïves to renounce France for ever, and the French historiography dates the proclamation of independence to that day.[^src-madiou-1848-t3 p117][^src-lentz-2003 abstract] On 17 April 1825 the King of France conceded the independence of the former colony on the condition of a payment to the former colonists, and the Haitian legislature declared the sum, 150,000,000 francs, a national debt.[^src-eugene-2003 abstract][^src-ordonnance-18250417 p258-259][^src-haiti-adresse-18260510 p456-457]

This thesis asks who freed the enslaved of Saint-Domingue, what the French state said it was sending an army to do, and what the free record settles about the declaration and the price set on it. It makes three claims. First, that the acts of state held here declared the people of the colony free 5 times between 1793 and 1805, 2 of them in Bonaparte's own name in November 1801, and that the law of 1802 which maintained slavery named other colonies and not this one. Second, that Bonaparte's note to his minister, written in the same fortnight as his guarantee of liberty, states the expedition's course as annihilating the government of the blacks, and that the record holds the arrest in the prisoner's own hand. Third, that the Act of 1804 carries 37 names as the Haitian historian prints it, that the ordinance of 1825 set 150,000,000 francs as the condition of recognition and the Haitian legislature declared it a national debt, and that the ceremony of 1791, the day of Toussaint Louverture's death and the count of the dead are disagreements, which this thesis states as disagreements.

## The record

The primary documents this thesis holds were fetched from the John Carter Brown Library's collection on the Internet Archive, from the Internet Archive's scans of the Correspondance de Napoléon Ier and of the Haitian Recueil général des lois et actes, from the United States Statutes at Large as GovTrack serves them, and from the abstracts of open articles on Persée, Brill, SciELO and an open journal, and every passage below was sliced from that copy by a script rather than retyped.{i} The commissioners' proclamations, Toussaint Louverture's proclamation and constitution, the law of the year X, the memoir he wrote in prison and the two statutes of the United States are held whole or by article; Bonaparte's letters are held by item; the Haitian acts of the recognition, and the ordinance of Charles X as the Recueil reprints it, are held by page.{i} Where a scan's text layer is the free copy the extract keeps the layer's misreadings and says so, and where the layer garbles a figure, as it does the year of the Act and the sum of the ordinance, the figure is cited from a page where it is printed clean.{i} The Haitian record is held in French from Madiou's history and Saint-Rémy's edition, the one printed at Port-au-Prince and the other by a Haitian in Paris, and in Spanish from a Haitian scholar writing in Argentina; the one document in Creole is the commissioners' broadside, held beside its French column as the official parallel text, with a rendering marked as this thesis's own.{i} The scholarship is held only as the passages cited, and the Persée articles only as their abstracts, because their pages are behind a challenge from this container.{i} What the record does not hold is listed under the gaps below, by name, with what was tried.{i} The largest are the manuscript of the Act in the National Archives at Kew, which the count of its signatures could not be checked against; the Convention's decree of the year II, of which no free printing was found; the treaties Eugène describes, which carry the reduced sum the event record states and this thesis does not; Leclerc's own letters, on Persée and unreadable from here; and the standard works of James, Fick, Dubois and Trouillot, which sit in lending collections this container cannot open, so that the one Haitian scholar the event record leans on most is absent, and every count of the dead below comes from a colonist, a commission or a Haitian historian, and none from the dead's own side.{i} The prisoner's own statement of what was done to him comes first.{i} He wrote that when he was arrested he had no other clothes than those he wore, that a note to his wife was handed to General Leclerc's aide-de-camp and never arrived, that he was put aboard the frigate “la Créole” and then the ship “le Héros”, and that his wife and children, arrested with her, arrived the next day.[^src-toussaint-memoire p83]

## The argument

### 1. The oath and the fire

The Convention's commission, reporting on the troubles, wrote that the slaves of some plantations of the Acul and the Limbé wanted to begin the war against the whites before the troops arrived, that the slaves of Turpin and Flaville came to fetch those of Clément in the evening, that the two workshops chose Boukmans and Auguste as chiefs, and that the fire began towards midnight; by the morning of the 23rd, it counted 37 proprietors, attorneys and refiners assassinated.[^src-garran-1797-t1 p212-213] Madiou, from Port-au-Prince, tells of a night on which delegates of the workshops of the North met at the Lenormand plantation, heard a supposed decree of the king read by a man of colour, and decided that on the 22nd the insurrection would be general.[^src-madiou-1847-t1 seclenormand] Dalmas, a colonist writing under the Restoration, is the one source in this record for a sacrifice.{i} He places the plan among the chiefs at Le Normand at Morne Rouge, and before its execution a feast or sacrifice in a wooded ground of the Choiseul plantation called the Caïman, where a black pig was the offering; he dates the meeting in a footnote and the sacrifice not at all.[^src-dalmas-1814 p117-118] The event record's prayer, which it says Dalmas recorded, is in none of these pages, and this thesis does not print it.{i} The three accounts are set beside each other in the disagreements below.{i}

### 2. Liberty granted and liberty taken

The commissioner's proclamation freed and bound in the same breath.{i} Article II declared the enslaved free citizens, subject to a regime set out in the articles that followed; article IX held the negroes then attached to the plantations of their former masters to stay on them and to be employed in cultivating the land; article XII divided each plantation's revenue into three equal portions, one for the owner of the land, one for the costs of working it, and the third among the cultivators.[^src-sonthonax-17930829 art2][^src-sonthonax-17930829 art9][^src-sonthonax-17930829 art12] Two months earlier the same commissioners had told the colony, in French and in Creole, that it would not die, that it would never again know a king or wicked masters, and that it would be “comme la France la terre de la liberté”.[^src-commissaires-17930702-fr sec1][^src-commissaires-17930702-ht sec1]

::: exhibit ex-creole-1793

In a proclamation dated at the Cap on 12 fructidor of the fifth year of the Republic, Toussaint Louverture, general in chief of the army of Saint-Domingue, told the cultivators of the plain that the gratitude they owed France “pour le bienfait inestimable de la liberté qu'elle vous a donné” bound them to the duties of their state, and that the war had been undertaken by their enemies only to return them to slavery.[^src-toussaint-procl-17970829 sec3][^src-toussaint-procl-17970829 secsign] The constitution he had prepared in 1801, which Schneider's colonel Vincent carried to the First Consul, said in its third article that slaves were not permitted in the territory, that “l'esclavage est aboli” for ever, and that all men born in the country lived and died free and French; its article 28 gave him the reins of government for the whole of his life.[^src-toussaint-const-1801 art3][^src-toussaint-const-1801 art28][^src-schneider-2002 abstract]

::: exhibit ex-const-1801-art3

Bonaparte answered in three documents of the same fortnight.{i} The proclamation of 8 November told the inhabitants of Saint-Domingue, whatever their origin and colour, that they were all French, all free and all equal, and that if anyone said the forces coming were meant to take their liberty they should answer that the Republic had given it and would not suffer it to be taken.[^src-napoleon-corr-7 item5859] The note to Talleyrand of 13 November said the expedition could not be delayed a day because after germinal the climate was impracticable for Europeans, that to put it off would oblige him to recognise Toussaint and to constitute French blacks there, and that in the course he had taken of annihilating the government of the blacks he had been guided less by commerce than by the necessity of stifling every seed of trouble in every part of the world.[^src-napoleon-corr-7 item5863] The Exposé of 22 November told the nation that at Saint-Domingue and Guadeloupe there were no more slaves, that all was free and would stay free, and that Martinique had kept slavery and would keep it.[^src-napoleon-corr-7 item5874]

::: exhibit ex-napoleon-5863

The law of 30 floréal an X maintained slavery in the colonies restored to France by the treaty of Amiens “conformément aux lois et réglemens antérieurs à 1789”, extended the same to the colonies beyond the Cape of Good Hope, and allowed the slave trade and importation into those colonies as before 1789.[^src-loi-30-floreal-anx whole] It does not name Saint-Domingue.{i}

::: analysis an-slavery-documents

### 3. The expedition and the prisoner

On 8 brumaire, before the proclamation of 8 November 1801, Bonaparte had Talleyrand tell the British cabinet, port by port, which ships would sail for Saint-Domingue in the last decade of brumaire, carrying “à peu près 20,000” men of landing under Leclerc, and asked that Jamaica supply the army's provisions, the interest of civilisation being to destroy “la nouvelle Alger qui s'organisait au milieu de l'Amérique”.[^src-napoleon-corr-7 item5845][^src-napoleon-corr-7 item5859] On 18 November the First Consul wrote to Toussaint Louverture that the peace let the government attend to the colony, that his brother-in-law Leclerc was sent as captain general with forces suitable to make the sovereignty of the French people respected, and that the constitution Toussaint had made contained things contrary to the dignity and sovereignty of that people.[^src-napoleon-corr-7 item5867]

::: analysis an-expedition-ships

Toussaint's own account of the end is the memoir he wrote in prison and addressed to the First Consul.{i} It says that when he was arrested he had no clothes but those he wore, that a note to his wife given to Leclerc's aide-de-camp never arrived, that he was carried four leagues from the Cap to the ship “le Héros” and made to board it, that his wife and children, arrested with her, arrived the next day, and that they sailed at once for France on a crossing whose days the memoir counts.[^src-toussaint-memoire p83] The memoir ends by telling the First Consul that his wounds are very deep, that he is a physician, and that the prisoner counts entirely on his justice.[^src-toussaint-memoire p101]

::: exhibit ex-toussaint-arrest

The words the event record has Toussaint speak to his captors are carried in this record by one source, the memoir of a French general of the expedition, and by the Haitian editor of the memoir who quotes that general; they are quoted in the historiography, as that account's own, and nowhere else.{i} On the day of his death the record disagrees with itself, and the disagreement is stated below rather than resolved.{i} Gainot and Macé write that the French occupation's last period ran from the death of Leclerc in November 1802 to the capitulation of Rochambeau in November 1803, and that yellow fever and the renewed war with Britain are facile explanations that conceal the responsibilities of the colonial authorities.[^src-gainot-mace-2003 abstract] Madiou places the attack that opened the last battle at daybreak on 18 November 1803, when General Clervaux ordered it to begin.[^src-madiou-1848-t3 p85]

### 4. The declaration

::: episode chapter=4

The proclamation of January 1804, as Madiou prints it, ends by asking the people to swear to live free and independent and to prefer death to anything that would put them back under the yoke, and is dated at the headquarters of Gonaïves on the first of January over the signature of Dessalines.[^src-madiou-1848-t3 p115][^src-lentz-2003 abstract] In the translation of Dubois and Garrigus the same text opens “It is not enough to have expelled the barbarians who have bloodied our land for two centuries”.[^src-dubois-garrigus-2006 sec0] It says “We have dared to be free, let us be thus by ourselves and for ourselves”.[^src-dubois-garrigus-2006 sec11] The Act that followed records that the general in chief, with the generals assembled, had made known to foreign powers the resolution to render the country independent, and that each general swore to renounce France for ever, to die rather than live under its domination, and to fight for independence to the last breath.[^src-madiou-1848-t3 p117][^src-dubois-garrigus-2006 sec21][^src-dubois-garrigus-2006 sec25]

::: analysis an-act-signatories

::: analysis an-two-acts

The name the new state took is the subject of Geggus's article, which asks what the founders might have known of the Amerindian past of the Caribbean and of South America.[^src-geggus-1997 abstract] The proclamation of April that announced the killing of the French, which Madiou says almost every arm had carried out, counted its victims, men, women and children, at “trois mille” or thereabouts, and its text declares that the hour of vengeance had sounded and that its author, having saved his country, had “vengé l'Amérique”.[^src-madiou-1848-t3 p141][^src-madiou-1848-t3 p142] The event record places those words in the declaration of the first of January; in this record they stand in the proclamation of April.{i} The constitution of the empire, as Madiou prints it, formed the people of the island formerly called St. Domingue into a free, sovereign and independent state under the name of empire of Haiti, and declared in its article 2 that “L'esclavage est à jamais aboli”; its article 12 barred any white, of whatever nation, from setting foot on the territory as master or proprietor, its article 13 excepted the naturalised white women and the naturalised Germans and Poles, and the article that follows held that the Haitians would henceforth be known only under the generic name of blacks.[^src-madiou-1848-t3 p471][^src-madiou-1848-t3 p472]

### 5. The price of recognition

The Congress of the United States enacted in 1806 that all commercial intercourse between residents of the United States and any part of the island of St. Domingo “not in possession, and under the acknowledged government of France” was prohibited, and that a vessel and cargo carried there would be forfeited.[^src-us-act-18060228 sec1] It authorised the President to appoint diplomatic representatives to the republics of Hayti and Liberia by the act approved on June 5, 1862.[^src-us-act-18620605 whole]

::: exhibit ex-us-act-1806

The ordinance Charles X signed at the Tuileries opened the ports of the French part of Saint-Domingue to the commerce of all nations, made the duties equal for every flag except the French, in whose favour they were reduced by half, required the present inhabitants to pay into the Caisse des dépôts et consignations, in five equal annual terms, a sum destined to compensate “les anciens colons” who would claim an indemnity, and on those conditions conceded them the independence “pleine et entière” of their government.[^src-ordonnance-18250417 p258-259] Eugène writes that the ordinance of 17 April 1825 followed fruitless negotiations from 1814 to 1824, fixed the indemnity unilaterally at cent cinquante millions de francs or, and was in form an act of authority imposed on Haiti though Boyer had accepted the principle, and that Louis-Philippe's government reconsidered the policy in 1838 by a double treaty.[^src-eugene-2003 abstract]

::: exhibit ex-ordonnance-1825

The Haitian Chamber of Representatives, on 26 February 1826, considered that an indemnity had been consented to in favour of France for the recognition of the full and entire independence of the Republic, and declared it a national debt.[^src-haiti-loi-18260226 p353] At the close of its session on 10 May 1826 it told the Haitians that the legislature, having passed a law recognising as national debt the indemnity of 150,000,000 francs consented to France, had to provide the means to extinguish that sacred engagement, and had passed a law establishing an extraordinary contribution of “30 millions de gourdes payable” over a decade.[^src-haiti-adresse-18260510 p456-457] In the Chambre des Pairs, on 19 April, Barbé-Marbois opened his opinion on the law dividing the indemnity among the colonists by asking whether it had been necessary to recognise the independence of Saint-Domingue, and answered that for a generation relations with the island had been almost entirely interrupted, that the regular profits of the colonial commerce had gone to enrich other countries, and that it mattered to order and general repose to end a state of things that kept Haiti outside the universal society.[^src-barbe-marbois-1826 p1-2] Blancpain's note holds that there is a long way from the immense traffic of the slave trade to the small quarrel over the indemnity and the later loans, and that ignorance and demagogy are too often mixed in it.[^src-blancpain-2003 abstract] How long Haiti paid and to whom is set out in the disagreements below, and this thesis states no single number for it.{i}

Lacroix's memoir is where the words on the Héros stand, and it is a French general's memoir published under the Restoration.{i} It has Toussaint say to the commander of the ship that in overthrowing him they had cut down at Saint-Domingue only the trunk of the tree of the liberty of the blacks, and that “il repoussera par les racines, parce qu'elles sont profondes et nombreuses”; the same page says he died at the fort de Joux, cramped by cold and gnawed by regret, “après dix mois de captivité”.[^src-lacroix-1819 p203-204] Saint-Rémy, editing the memoir, quotes Lacroix for the words and names him as the source.[^src-saint-remy-1853 p83] Dalmas, on the ceremony, writes that the religious ceremonies the negroes practised in slaughtering the pig, the avidity with which they drank its blood and the price they set on its hairs as a talisman “servent à caractériser l'Africain”, and that a caste so ignorant and brutalised naturally preluded the most appalling attacks with the rites of an absurd and bloody religion.[^src-dalmas-1814 p117-118] Edwards's computation of the white persons massacred within two months of the revolt, of the plantations and settlements destroyed and of the insurgents who perished by the sword or by famine is set in the disagreements below; his footnote says two of them were broken on the wheel under his own window at Cape François on 28 September 1791.[^src-edwards-1797 p75-76] Yakubu and Opondo, from Lagos and Eldoret, hold that the abolition of slavery in the nineteenth century had its roots in slave revolts, in Berbice in 1773 and in Saint-Domingue between 1791 and 1804, and that the successful Haitian insurgency served as an impetus for the freedom of black slaves across the globe.[^src-yakubu-opondo-2025 p166] Mezilas, a Haitian writing in Spanish, reads the revolution of 1804 by its political repercussions on Latin America and by the help Haiti's leaders gave the Spanish American revolutionaries.[^src-mezilas-2009 p31] Schneider's colonel Vincent, who carried the constitution of 1801 to the First Consul, tried in vain to dissuade him from sending the expedition and was hostile both to independence and to the return of slavery.[^src-schneider-2002 abstract] Lentz reads the Consulate's colonial choices as disastrous: the expedition against Toussaint Louverture's autonomist plans, the decision to re-establish slavery that brought repression and massacres in Guadeloupe and then the irreversible break with Saint-Domingue, proclaimed independent on the first of January 1804.[^src-lentz-2003 abstract]

## What the record omits

- No source in this record is a testimony by one of the enslaved of 1791 in his or her own words; the rising is known here through a colonist, a commission of the Convention and a Haitian historian, and the one voice of a formerly enslaved man in his own hand is a general's, written in a French prison. {against=haitian-national}
- No source in this record is a letter of Leclerc; the French side of 1802 is known here through Bonaparte's correspondence and a general's memoir, and the expedition commander's own reports of the epidemic and the killings are a gap. {against=french-imperial}
- No source in this record dates the sacrifice at the Caïman wood; the one account that tells it dates the meeting that preceded it and not the sacrifice, and the prayer the event record attributes to that night is in no page held here. {against=colonist-and-claimant}
- No source in this record is a woman's account, enslaved or free; the women appear as an arrested wife, as the naturalised white women of an article of the constitution of 1805, and as counted victims. {against=haitian-national}
- No source in this record is the treaty of 1838 or a Haitian treasury account of what was paid and when; the payments after 1826 are known here only through two French abstracts. {against=the-indemnity-accountants}
- No source in this record is by a scholar of the Atlantic school reading the revolution from below; the standard monographs sit in lending collections this container cannot open, and the one Haitian scholar the event record leans on most is absent for the same reason. {against=anglophone-academic}
- No source in this record is the Convention's decree of 1794; the metropolitan abolition is known here through the commissioner's proclamation it ratified and through what Bonaparte's own documents say of it. {against=french-republican-scholarship}
