## The question

::: episode chapter=0

The General Act of the Conference of Berlin was signed at Berlin on 26 February 1885.[^src-berlin-act-1885 sec128] Its preamble says the sovereigns met “to regulate the conditions most favourable to the development of trade and civilization in certain regions of Africa”, and to obviate the disputes “which might in future arise from new acts of occupation” on the coast.[^src-berlin-act-1885 pre1] With the conference sitting, on 20 November 1884, the Under-Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs told the Commons that “the decision of territorial claims is not among the bases of the Conference”.[^src-hansard-18841120 whole] The historian Uzoigwe, writing in the UNESCO General History, holds that the view that the conference did not partition Africa “is correct only in the most technical sense”.[^src-uzoigwe-1985 p30]

This thesis asks what the Act allocated, what the rules it laid down cost the people they reached, and what the free record settles about the counts. It makes three claims. First, that the Act allocates no territory and names no border, and that its articles on occupation are rules for future acts on the coasts. Second, that the rulers whose land those rules reached speak in this record in their own words, and that each refuses to let a European text stand for his consent. Third, that the Congo's rubber system and the Herero war are in the record from both sides, and that on the deaths the record holds counts of unlike things, which this thesis states as counts and never as one number.

## The record

The primary documents this thesis holds were fetched from the Internet Archive's scans of Hertslet's treaty collection and of four parliamentary papers, from the Historic Hansard service, from the German Historical Institute's documentary edition, from the Assemblée nationale's own site and from the Bundestag, and every passage below was sliced from that copy by a script rather than retyped.{i} The Act is held in the Foreign Office's English translation, article by article; the French text that governs it was not fetched, because Gallica refused this container.{i} The African voices held in their own words are a Nama chief's letters in Cape Dutch, with renderings marked as this thesis's own that the page never quotes as text, a Matabele king's letters as a Member of Parliament read them to the Commons, an Ethiopian emperor's letter in a Foreign Office translation, and Herero and Congolese witnesses as the British administration and the British consul recorded them, which is the colonisers' record of the colonised and is treated so under Rule A.{i} The German side speaks through its commander's order and, a century on, through the federal parliament's research service; the Free State speaks through its own commission of inquiry.{i} The scholarship is held only as the passages cited, from two African historians in the UNESCO General History, an Ethiopian journal, a South African journal, a Sheffield manuscript and a Leiden chapter; the standard monographs on the partition and the paywalled articles on the conference and on the Herero war are known here by name alone and are listed under the gaps.{i} What the record does not hold is listed under the gaps by name, with what was tried, and the largest are the conference's own protocols, the Rudd Concession in its own words, Menelik's circular to the powers, the Reichstag debates on the Herero war, and any Portuguese source.{i} The German paper's own statement of the limits of counting comes first.{i} It writes that the pre-war estimates of the Herero rest on missionaries' figures of doubtful reliability and the post-war figures on the German military's prisoner counts, that the estimates for the whole people run from 35,000 to 100,000 before the war and from 14,000 to 25,000 after it, and that at the extremes this yields loss rates of between 29 and 86 per cent.[^src-bundestag-2004 p19-20]

## The argument

### 1. The table

The Act's preamble names the sovereigns and presidents who sent plenipotentiaries, and says they met on the invitation of the Imperial Government of Germany, in agreement with the Government of the French Republic.[^src-berlin-act-1885 pre1] The Act itself lists the documents it combines: a declaration on freedom of trade in the basin of the Congo, a declaration on the slave trade, a declaration on the neutrality of the conventional basin, an act of navigation for the Congo, an act of navigation for the Niger, and a declaration “introducing into international relations certain uniform rules with reference to future occupations on the coast of the African Continent”.[^src-berlin-act-1885 toc] The first article opens the trade of all nations in the basin of the Congo, and defines that basin by the watersheds of the Niari, the Ogowe, the Schari and the Nile on the north and of the Zambesi and the Loge on the south.[^src-berlin-act-1885 art1] Article VI binds the Powers exercising sovereign rights or influence in those territories “to watch over the preservation of the native tribes, and to care for the improvement of the conditions of their moral and material well-being, and to help in suppressing slavery, and especially the slave trade”.[^src-berlin-act-1885 art6] No article names a Power's possession.{i}

::: exhibit ex-act-art34

Article XXXIV provides that any Power which henceforth takes possession of a tract of land on the coasts of the African Continent, or assumes a protectorate there, shall accompany the act with a notification “addressed to the other Signatory Powers of the present Act, in order to enable them, if need be, to make good any claims of their own”.[^src-berlin-act-1885 art34] Article XXXV has the signatories recognise “the obligation to insure the establishment of authority in regions occupied by them on the coasts of the African Continent sufficient to protect existing rights”.[^src-berlin-act-1885 art35] Both articles speak of the coasts, of future acts, and of the signatories among themselves.{i} When Jacob Bright asked in the Commons whether the British representative would be instructed to support the claims of Portugal, the Under-Secretary declined to say what the instructions were, said that “a pledge of secrecy has been given by all the Powers” for the proceedings, and repeated that the decision of territorial claims was not among the bases of the conference.[^src-hansard-18841120 whole]

::: analysis an-act-chapters

::: exhibit ex-afrikakonferenz

Uzoigwe reads the notification article as the doctrine of spheres of influence and Article 35 as “the so-called doctrine of effective occupation that was to make the conquest of Africa such a murderous business”, and writes that by 1885 the broad lines of the final partition had already been drawn.[^src-uzoigwe-1985 p28][^src-uzoigwe-1985 p30] He also writes that the conference did not partition Africa in the technical sense, and the two readings are set against each other in the disagreements below.[^src-uzoigwe-1985 p30] In the Chambre des députés on 28 juillet 1885, Ferry, a former premier, told the deputies that the Berlin conference had just translated into positive law the duty to combat the slave trade, and, over interruptions from the far left and the right, that “les races supérieures ont un droit vis-à-vis des races inférieures”.[^src-ferry-1885 sechead][^src-ferry-1885 secberlin][^src-ferry-1885 secraces] The sixth article and Ferry's sentence are the same claim in a treaty and in a speech, and the next three sections test it against what the Powers' own records say was done.{i}

### 2. The rulers and the papers

::: exhibit ex-witbooi-letter

In Mei 1890, Hendrik Witbooi wrote from Hornkrans to Maharero, chief of the Herero, that he had heard from a letter of Goering's that Maharero had given himself into German protection.[^src-witbooi-1929 p77] He wrote that every chief has his own people and land, over which he alone commands, and that every chief on this earth is only a place-holder of God, answerable to God alone.[^src-witbooi-1929 p78] He told Maharero “gy zult eeuwig berouw hebben”, that he would have everlasting regret, for giving his land and his right of government into the hands of the white people.[^src-witbooi-1929 p79] In June of the next year he wrote to a missionary that the Germans told the chiefs of the land to make friendship with them because other strong nations would seize their ground, but that it now looked as if the German was the man who wanted to seize the land.[^src-witbooi-1929 p105]

::: exhibit ex-lobengula-letter

In the Commons in November 1893, Labouchere read the letter Lobengula had sent to Queen Victoria after learning what he had signed: “A document was read and presented to me for signature. I asked what it contained, and was told that in it were my words and the words of those men. I put my hand to it. About three months after I heard from other sources I had given the right to all the minerals in my country”.[^src-hansard-18931109 p546] The Queen's reply, sent by Lord Knutsford, said that Lobengula was the ruler of his country, that the Englishmen who asked leave to dig had not gone with the Queen's authority, and that “A King gives a stranger an ox, not a whole herd of cattle”.[^src-hansard-18931109 p546-547] In August the king wrote again: “If the Queen hears that I have given away the whole country it is not so. I have no one in my country who knows how to write”.[^src-hansard-18931109 p547] The Crown's editor of treaties records the other side of the same exchange in a footnote: an agreement of February 1888 by which Lobengula undertook to make no treaty with any foreign State or Power ceding any part of his country without the sanction of the High Commissioner, and a concession granted on 30 October 1888 to the representatives of the British South Africa Company.[^src-hertslet-charter-1889 p174] Labouchere told the House that in October, 1889, “without any further communication with Lobengula, the Company's Charter was granted”.[^src-hansard-18931109 p547] The concession itself is not in this record, so the king's account of the paper and the Company's are known here only as the British recorded both.{i}

The emperor who kept his throne was the most careful of the three with paper.{i} On 14 May 1897, Menelik wrote to Rodd proposing that in any future misunderstanding between the Amharic and English versions of the treaty they had written, the French translation he enclosed “be the witness between us”, “as I have no interpreter with me who understands the English language well enough to compare the English and Amharic versions”.[^src-menelik-1897 p425] An Ethiopian journal puts the battle of Adwa on March 1, 1896, with 11,000 casualties in total and nearly 43% of the Italian forces killed, wounded or missing.[^src-waktola-2024 p94] Boahen's reading of the whole generation is that Africans were faced with two options, to surrender their sovereignty or to defend it, and that the great majority of them “did decide to defend their sovereignty and independence”.[^src-boahen-1985 p9-10]

### 3. The Congo

The Commons resolved on 20 May 1903 that the Government of the Congo Free State had at its inception “guaranteed to the Powers that its Native subjects should be governed with humanity, and that no trading monopoly or privilege should be permitted within its dominions”, and asked the Government to confer with the other signatories of the Berlin General Act “by virtue of which the Congo Free State exists”.[^src-hansard-19030520 p1332][^src-hansard-19030520 p1307-1308] The words “and both these guarantees having been constantly violated” were in the motion as Samuel moved it and were left out by amendment before the House agreed to it.[^src-hansard-19030520 p1307-1308][^src-hansard-19030520 p1332] Gorst told the House that “The evidence was perfectly overwhelming”.[^src-hansard-19030520 p1307-1308]

The consul who went up the river that year had known it before.{i} At Bolobo, once one of the most important settlements on the south bank of the Upper Congo, the population “in the early days of civilized rule numbered fully 40,000 people”, and was believed to be not more than 7,000 or 8,000.[^src-casement-1904 p27] On the south shore of Stanley Pool, where fully 5,000 Bateke had lived in three towns, the great majority had crossed in one night to the French territory, and not 100 of the original people remained at Leopoldville.[^src-casement-1904 p22] Casement gave sleeping sickness “a prominent place” among the causes of the diminution of life he observed, and wrote that the people attributed their decrease to other causes as well, “and I think principally”, which is the consul's own judgement of what they told him.[^src-casement-1904 p21] The girl the report calls T 7 told him that when the State soldiers came to her village in the afternoon, “they began catching and tying the people, and killed lots of them”, and that she alone of those who ran escaped.[^src-casement-1904 p75] At Lake Mantumba the local official told him that men still came to him whose hands had been cut off by the Government soldiers, and Casement saw two cases himself, one of them “a young lad of 11 or 12 years of age, whose right hand was cut off at the wrist”.[^src-casement-1904 p34]

::: analysis an-congo-counts

::: exhibit ex-casement-t7

The Free State's own commission, sent after the report, found that when the prestations were not forthcoming the chiefs were arrested and some inhabitants taken at random, and that “often the women were held as hostages”; the State, it wrote, “has, for a long time, forbidden this practice, but has been unable to suppress it”.[^src-congo-commission-1906 p67] Of the armed overseers it called sentries, the strangers detailed to a village were “by far the worst sort”.[^src-congo-commission-1906 p70] On depopulation it agreed that from Stanley Pool to New Antwerp the banks of the river were almost deserted, and read the river people as having fled the impositions or been scattered rather than destroyed.[^src-congo-commission-1906 p115] It then set every such cause below smallpox and sleeping sickness: “All of these causes of depopulation are secondary in importance”.[^src-congo-commission-1906 p117] The two records agree on the hostages, the sentries and the emptying of the river, and divide on what the people died of.{i}

### 4. The desert

::: episode chapter=3

::: exhibit ex-trotha-order

The order the German commander signed at Osombo-Windhuk on 2 October 1904 reads, in its own German, “Innerhalb der Deutschen Grenze wird jeder Herero mit oder ohne Gewehr, mit oder ohne Vieh erschossen, ich nehme keine Weiber und keine Kinder mehr auf”, which Ciarlo's published translation renders “Any Herero found within the German frontier, with or without a gun, with or without cattle, will be shot. I spare neither women nor children”.[^src-trotha-1904 whole][^src-ciarlo-trotha-en whole] The addendum to the troops says, in that translation, that the shooting at women and children “is to be understood as shooting above their heads”, and that the order will result in “no more male prisoners being taken”.[^src-ciarlo-trotha-en whole] Gewald's translation of von Estorff has the officer writing that the people could still have been saved and pardoned, that “I suggested this to General von Trotha”, and “but hè wanted their total extermination”.[^src-gewald-2004 p62-63]

The Herero causes are in this record as sworn statements taken by the British administration that replaced the German one.{i} Daniel Kariko, an under-chief, swore that “Our people were compelled to work on farms, and the farmers had them chained up by the police and flogged without mercy for the slightest little thing”, that a German was declared not guilty of murdering a chief's daughter and then given three years on a new trial, and that “This decided us for war”.[^src-bluebook-1918 p54-55] Gottlob Kamatoto swore that after messages that the war was over, “seven Herero leaders came into the German camp to discuss peace terms”, and that at sunset “the seven peace envoys were caught and tied with ropes. They were led aside and shot”.[^src-bluebook-1918 p59] Gerard Kamaheke swore that he went in to Ombakaha with the chiefs Saul and Joel and 70 Herero soldiers, that the Germans opened fire, and that “I lay quite still and pretended to be dead”.[^src-bluebook-1918 p59-60] The same report prints the amnesty proclamation of 1st December 1905 that called the survivors in, “Come and lay down your arms, Hereros!”, and told them that Hendrik Witbooi had been killed by a German bullet.[^src-bluebook-1918 p102-103] A review in a South African journal records that in 1926 the report was withdrawn from circulation in libraries and archives throughout the British Empire.[^src-adhikari-2008 secbluebook]

::: analysis an-herero-counts

The counts do not agree, and the page states them as counts.{i} The British report put the Herero at between 80,000 and 90,000 souls, of whom only about 15,000 were alive at the end of 1905, and Gewald writes that no fewer than 80 per cent of the Herero lost their lives and that the Nama lost at least 30 per cent of their people.[^src-bluebook-1918 p67][^src-gewald-2004 p60-61][^src-gewald-2004 p74-75] The Bundestag's research service reports that 7682 prisoners died in the camps between October 1904 and March 1907, a death rate of 30 to 50 per cent, and sets the range of the whole from 29 to 86 per cent.[^src-bundestag-2004 p19][^src-bundestag-2004 p19-20] The same paper summarises the critics of the genocide thesis, who hold that the camps were set up to save the Herero from certain death in the desert and that the counts are put far too high.[^src-bundestag-2004 p25]

The colonial position states itself in this record in a governor's treatise, quoted here as the position's own words and nowhere as fact.{i} Lugard wrote that “Europe is in Africa for the mutual benefit of her own industrial classes”, and of the native races in their progress to a higher plane, and called it a dual mandate.[^src-lugard-1922 p617] The reform campaign's own book is held in the same way.{i} Morel's summary of the missionary Sjoblom has sentries who “bring the hands to the Commissioner”, and the missionary saying “I also saw smoked hands”.[^src-morel-1906 p46-47] The historians of Congo violence describe a maximalist position that puts the dead at “roughly fifty per cent” of the population and agnostics who call the data unreliable, and Roes writes that “a firm estimate of the number of victims must indeed remain elusive”.[^src-roes-2010 p12-13] He names “the estimated 10 million victims forwarded by Morel, Hochschild and others”.[^src-roes-2010 p15] Uzoigwe writes that the European notion of effective occupation and the African notion of the meaning of the treaties were “in fundamental contradiction with one another”.[^src-uzoigwe-1985 p34]

## What the record omits

- No source in this record is the French text of the Act or a protocol of the conference; what was said at the table is known here only through what was signed. {against=berlin-not-partition}
- No source in this record is a delimitation treaty between two European Powers after the conference; the partition the treaties made is known here through the African historians' description of them. {against=african-historiography}
- No source in this record is the Rudd Concession in its own words, or the Herero protection treaty of 1885; the rulers' account of the papers and the Crown's are both British records. {against=african-rulers}
- No source in this record is a Congolese witness in his or her own words; the people of the river are known here as a British consul and a Belgian commission recorded them, and the one Congolese scholarship found sits behind a paywall. {against=congo-reform}
- No source in this record is a German officer's or settler's account of the war in his own words beyond the commander's order; the governor's memoir was fetched and could not be read, and the Reichstag debates were refused. {against=german-record}
- No source in this record is a Herero woman's or a Nama survivor's statement; the Herero witnesses held are three men sworn before the British administration. {against=herero-genocide}
- No source in this record is from Portugal or in Portuguese, and no source on Samori, Asante, the Zulu, the Mahdist state, Algeria or Libya was reached; the record argues the continent from the Congo basin, Namibia, Matabeleland and Ethiopia. {against=african-historiography}
- No source in this record is a European critic of the conference at the time other than the Members who questioned the Under-Secretary; the case for the conference is made here by the Act, a minister and a French premier. {against=european-diplomatic}
